# Ackoff's Best: His Classic Writings on Management

## Core models

- **The systems approach to problems**: a system is a set of interdependent parts; to understand (or manage) any part you must understand the whole containing system and its environment/purpose. Analyze by synthesis, not by dissection.
- **Three ways to treat a problem** — resolve (fix the immediate instance), solve (optimize a satisfactory outcome under constraints), and **dissolve** (redesign the containing system so the problem ceases to exist). Dissolution is the systems answer; the other two leave the problem-generating structure intact.
- **Mess management**: a "mess" is a system of problems; problems are extracted from messes by abstraction, and treating extracted problems as independent is the classic managerial error. The mess, not any single problem, is the unit of intervention.
- **Machine, organism, and social-system views of organization**: how management conceives the enterprise determines what management acts are even possible; only the social-system view (purposeful parts with their own goals) supports learning and adaptation.
- **Backward/interactive planning (idealized design)**: start from the system you would design today if you could start fresh ("mess age" → idealized redesign), then work backward to feasibility; planning should be continuous and participative, not episodic forecasting.
- **Performance vs. learning/development**: doing things right (efficiency) vs. doing the right thing (effectiveness) vs. wanting the right thing (aesthetics/purpose); growth vs. development — development requires no growth in resources, only learning.

## Key definitions

- **System**: a set of two or more elements whose properties determine the behavior of the whole, where the behavior of each element has an effect on the whole, and no subset has an independent effect.
- **Dissolving a problem**: changing the system (or its environment) so the problem disappears — the third and highest mode of treatment.
- **Idealized design**: the design of the system its designers would have now if they could start over, constrained only by technological feasibility (not current practice), used as the anchor for planning.

## The one diagram (in words)

Three nested boxes: the problem sits in the middle, the *system* that generates it around it, and the *environment* around that — with an arrow bypassing the inner box from problem straight out through both rings: the dissolver's path, which redraws the system's boundary or purpose so that the inner box simply no longer contains the problem, versus the resolver's/solver's arrow that terminates inside the middle box.

## Three quotable ideas

1. "The best thing that can be done to a problem is to dissolve it, to redesign the entity that has it in such a way that the problem no longer exists."
2. "Managers are not confronted with problems that are independent of each other, but with dynamic situations that consist of complex systems of changing problems that interact with each other" — messes, not problems.
3. "It is better to do the right thing wrong than the wrong thing right" — effectiveness outranks efficiency in any system of systems.

## Studio relevance

- **frame**: the mess-extraction warning — framing must declare the containing system, not just the presenting problem; the studio's boundary question is Ackoff's boundary question.
- **leverage**: resolve/solve/dissolve is the sharpest test of an intervention's ambition: a proposal that only resolves is rank-12 thinking; dissolution lives at rules/goals/paradigm ranks.
- **diagnose**: archetype diagnoses should end by asking which containing-system redesign would dissolve the archetype.
- **ground**: cite for dissolve-vs-solve and idealized-design vocabulary.

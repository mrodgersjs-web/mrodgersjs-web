# Business Dynamics: Systems Thinking and Modeling for a Complex World

## Core models

- **The modeling method end-to-end**: dynamic problem → dynamic hypothesis (causal loop diagram + reference mode of behavior over time) → formulation as a stock-and-flow simulation model → testing (extreme conditions, behavior reproduction) → policy design and evaluation. Models are built to answer a question, never as catalogs.
- **Feedback as the universal architecture**: all decisions sit in loops — state of system → information (with delays and distortion) → decisions → action → new state. Bounded rationality inside those loops, not stupidity, generates the pathologies.
- **Stocks, flows, and the stock-difference identity**: net rate of change = inflow − outflow; stocks integrate and decouple flows, create memory, inertia, and the possibility of disequilibrium.
- **Oscillation fundamentals**: phase lag between correcting action and its delayed effect generates oscillation; supply chains amplify it (the beer game — inventory oscillation from rational actors + structure + delays).
- **Limit cycles and nonlinearity**: table functions, saturating gains, tipping dynamics; linear intuition is systematically wrong about compounding and delay systems.
- **Model quality as testability**: dimensional consistency, extreme-condition tests, structure verification against knowledge of the real system — a model you cannot break is a model you do not understand.

## Key definitions

- **Dynamic hypothesis**: an explanation of a problematic behavior mode, expressed as a feedback structure expected to reproduce it — the hypothesis simulation then tests.
- **Reference mode**: the graph of the problem behavior over time (level, trend, period, amplitude) that the model must reproduce before any policy is evaluated.
- **Bounded rationality**: decision-makers act reasonably on locally available, delayed, filtered information — aggregate behavior emerges from these local rationalities.
- **Policy robustness**: a policy's performance under conditions other than those motivating it; tested by sweeping assumptions, not by fitting one run.

## The one diagram (in words)

The beer-game supply chain board: four positions (retailer, wholesaler, distributor, factory) in a line, each holding an inventory stock between an incoming case flow and an outgoing case flow, each placing orders upstream that travel along a delay line, each receiving deliveries after another delay — four rational players, no maliciousness, and the board's inventory graphs oscillate wildly out of phase with demand that never changed after step two.

## Three quotable ideas

1. "All decisions are embedded in feedback loops" — policy resistance is the system pushing back against a decision that ignored the loops it sits in.
2. "The most important delays are in the mind" — perception and decision delays routinely dominate physical ones in generating oscillation.
3. People systematically misperceive feedback: we act like naive physicists on a system that behaves like a bathtub — treating flows as levels and stocks as flows.

# Thinking in Systems: A Primer

## Core models

- **Stock–flow–feedback triad**: a system is stocks (accumulations), flows (rates filling/draining them), and feedback loops (decisions controlling flows in response to stock levels). Behavior comes from structure, not from individual actors.
- **Balancing (negative) loops**: goal-seeking, stabilizing loops that oppose change; strength measured by gain and delay. They keep systems on target — or resist your reform.
- **Reinforcing (positive) loops**: compounding growth or collapse engines; the same loop that builds an empire runs its ruin.
- **Delays**: perception, decision, action, and response delays cause overshoot, oscillation, and policy failure; a system reacting to stale signals at full speed oscillates.
- **Nonlinearity and limits**: loop gains shift with context; reinforcing loops always eventually meet a balancing constraint (carrying capacity, market saturation), producing S-curves or collapse.
- **Hierarchy and self-organization**: systems grow by decomposing into subsystems and reorganizing from the bottom up; healthy hierarchies serve the parts as much as the top.
- **Resilience, self-organization, hierarchy** as system properties that matter more than static efficiency or stability.
- **Leverage points and paradigms**: the famous 12-point intervention ladder (parameters weakest → paradigms strongest), distilled in a dedicated companion note.

## Key definitions

- **Stock**: an accumulation of material or information visible as a level at a moment (inventory, cash, trust).
- **Flow**: the rate of change of a stock over a period (production, hiring, churn).
- **Feedback loop**: closed chain of causal connections from a stock through decisions back to its flows.
- **Resilience**: the capacity of a system to recover from disturbance and restore self-organization — distinct from stability (resistance to disturbance).
- **Leverage point**: a place in a system's structure where a small change yields large, durable behavior change.
- **Hierarchy**: arrangement in which subsystems aggregate into larger systems, formed by self-organization.

## The one diagram (in words)

The plumbing-and-loop diagram: a rectangular bathtub (the stock) fed by an inflow pipe with a faucet and drained by an outflow pipe with a drain valve; a curved arrow leaves the stock, passes through a "perceived level" bubble that compares against a goal, and loops back to turn the faucet — a balancing loop drawn as a closed circle with a minus sign. The picture to hold: a tub of water with a thermostat brain.

## Three quotable ideas

1. "The behavior of a system cannot be known by knowing the elements of which the system is made." — structure, not parts, produces behavior.
2. The most important delays are the ones in our heads: we act on perceived, delayed pictures of reality, then blame the world for oscillations we created.
3. "There are no separate systems. The world is a continuum." — every system boundary is a drawing decision made by the observer, not a fact of nature.

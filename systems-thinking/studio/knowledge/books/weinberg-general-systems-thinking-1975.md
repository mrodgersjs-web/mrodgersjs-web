# An Introduction to General Systems Thinking

## Core models

- **The Square Law of Computation**: the number of interactions to be considered grows as the square of the number of components — so absolute thinking about a large system is impossible; observation is always selective, and *how* we select is the science.
- **The Law of Medium Numbers** ("Median")-> systems with a medium number of components behave neither like small systems (analyzable exactly) nor like large systems (statistically lawful): "medium-number systems give us trouble" — most real organizations, codebases, and markets live exactly there.
- **The Law of Large Numbers, the Law of Small Numbers, and stability laws** (homeostasis, complementarity, compensation) as the toolbox of simplified laws that let generalists reason about systems they cannot fully compute.
- **Observation as the core act**: the observer's choice of what to look at, and with what instrument/abstraction, partially determines the behavior observed; the "naturalist" stance — keeping field notes and letting general laws emerge from disciplined observation — over the "numerologist" stance of premature formalization.
- **The three regimes — small, medium, large** — and the generalist's strategy of moving a problem across regimes (decompose to small, aggregate to large) when stuck at medium.

## Key definitions

- **System**: a way of looking at the world ("a set of things together with relationships among them") — Weinberg's pragmatic, observer-relative definition rather than an ontological one.
- **General Systems law**: anything reasonably unexpected that keeps happening — an empirical regularity broad enough to cross disciplines, and the generalist's unit of knowledge.
- **Law of Medium Numbers**: for medium-number systems, neither the average nor the individual case tells you what will happen — the regime where intuition is least reliable and trouble concentrates.

## The one diagram (in words)

A single triangle of observation: observer, observation (what is seen), and thing (what is) — with arrows showing that the observer's assumptions travel down into the observation and the observation travels back up to reshape the assumptions; systems thinking, in this picture, is the discipline of noticing that you are always inside this triangle, choosing what to see.

## Three quotable ideas

1. "The Law of Medium Numbers: for medium numbers, the average is unreliable and the extremes are uninformative — medium-number systems give us trouble."
2. "A system is a way of looking at the world" — the boundary is drawn by the observer, and can be redrawn by the observer.
3. "The science of systems is the science of simplification without loss of what matters" — we observe selectively because we must; wisdom is choosing the selection.

## Studio relevance

- **frame**: Weinberg's observer-relative definition licenses the studio's insistence that framing is an explicit drawing decision (boundary, stock choice) rather than a fact to discover.
- **diagnose**: the Law of Medium Numbers explains why the studio's targets (orgs, GTM engines, agent fleets) resist both reductionist debugging and statistical averaging — archetype reasoning exists precisely for the medium regime.
- **leverage**: regime-shifting (decompose to small, aggregate to large) is itself a lever when a medium-number system proves undiagnosable.

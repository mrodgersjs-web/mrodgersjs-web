# Leverage Points: Places to Intervene in a System

## Core models

- **The 12-rank intervention ladder**, weakest (12) to strongest (1):
  12. Numbers/constants/parameters (subsidies, taxes, standards) — where everyone argues because it is easiest, not because it works.
  11. Sizes of buffers relative to flows.
  10. Structure of material stocks and flows (network topology, plumbing).
  9. Lengths of delays relative to rates of change.
  8. Strength of balancing (negative) feedback loops — the system's self-correction.
  7. Gain around reinforcing (positive) loops — the compounding rate.
  6. Structure of information flows — who sees what, when (missing feedback is often the cheapest high-value fix).
  5. Rules of the system — incentives, punishments, constraints; whoever writes them steers all players' rational strategies.
  4. Power to add, change, or self-organize system structure.
  3. Goals of the system — the variable the whole machine optimizes.
  2. Mindset/paradigm out of which the system arises — the beliefs that make the system make sense.
  1. Power to transcend paradigms — holding every way of seeing loosely.
- **Counterintuitive leverage**: people consistently push on low-leverage points (parameters) and ignore high-leverage ones (information flows, rules, goals, paradigms); pushing harder on a mis-chosen point makes things worse.
- **Growth-engine governance**: reinforcing loops build and destroy; the gain must be governed before it governs you.
- **Information beats punishment**: adding a feedback loop of information to the actor causing a consequence is often the highest value-for-effort intervention available.

## Key definitions

- **Leverage point**: a place in system structure where a small shift produces large, durable behavior change.
- **Paradigm**: the shared mindset — the collection of assumptions a society/system holds so deeply it cannot see them — from which goals, rules, and loops derive.
- **Self-organization**: the power of a system to change its own structure; the strongest structural capacity a system can possess.

## The one diagram (in words)

A ball-in-bowl picture: the ball (system state) sits in a landscape of dips and ridges; parameters nudge the ball around inside its current dip, while paradigm shifts reshape the entire landscape so the ball rolls somewhere no dip existed before. The essay itself is the diagram: one list, twelve rungs, arrow pointing up past the top.

## Three quotable ideas

1. "The least effective place to intervene in a system is where everyone spends most of their effort: the numbers."
2. "There are no lever, no fulcrum, no place to stand" once you reach paradigms — you change them by pointing at anomalies, inserting people into new communities, and acting from a different one yourself.
3. " paradigms are the sources of systems" — and the power to transcend paradigms altogether cannot be held, only kept alive.

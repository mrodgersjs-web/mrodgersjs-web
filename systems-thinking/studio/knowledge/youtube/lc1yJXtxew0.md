# What is institutional economics?

Institutional economics applies economic tools to study the rules, roles, and enforcement mechanisms that structure human interaction, treating institutions as endogenous and evolving rather than fixed.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Define an institution as the rules, roles, and enforcement mechanisms that structure human interaction and relationships.
- Distinguish organizations (specific groups of people) from institutions (broader rule systems that contain and pattern organizations).
- Treat institutions as the “software” that runs the economy—markets, marriage, corporations, legal systems, journalism, higher education, regulation, etc.
- Contrast the neoclassical assumption that institutions are exogenous and fixed with the institutional-economics view that they are endogenous and changeable.
- Apply the economist’s toolkit—cost–benefit analysis, optimization, opportunity cost, marginal analysis—to institutional design and public policy.
- Use repeated-game and game-theory reasoning (e.g., repeated prisoner’s dilemma, chicken) to analyze reputation, trust, and coordination within ongoing relationships.
- Examine coordination, efficiency, collective action, property rights, and repeated interaction as core institutional questions.
- Model hierarchical roles with principal–agent theory to diagnose incentive misalignment between principals and agents.
- Design rules of the game so that incentives align across hierarchy levels and institutional purposes can be achieved.
- Map “external” institutions (formal rules and structures) against “internal” institutions (shared norms, values, mental models held by people).
- Treat institutional alignment as a systems condition: formal rules work only when they match internalized norms and values.
- Study institutional change, evolution, and depreciation rather than assuming institutions are static.
- Evaluate public policy proposals by how they interact with, reshape, and fit existing institutions.

## Failure modes named
- Treating institutions as exogenous and unchangeable when they are actually evolving and shaped by policy.
- Designing formal external rules that are misaligned with people’s internal norms, values, and mental models.
- Assuming public policy acts on institutions perfectly without friction or unintended adaptation.
- Ignoring principal–agent incentive gaps within hierarchical institutions.
- Overlooking the repeated-game dynamics that make reputation, trust, and cooperation possible or break down.
- Applying a single branch or lens of institutional economics as if the field were unified, when its thinkers and assumptions often conflict.

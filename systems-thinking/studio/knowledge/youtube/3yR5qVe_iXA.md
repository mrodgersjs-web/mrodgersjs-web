# 5 Lessons in Business Negotiation from an FBI Hostage Negotiator

Chris Voss reframes negotiation as a trust-building discipline centered on tactical listening, empathy, and removing yourself as a threat before seeking any concession.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Let the other side go first and listen all the way through without interrupting.
- Recognize the "hijack point" in listening: the irresistible urge to correct or relate, and deliberately stay silent through it.
- Use the "urge to correct" as a tactical tool: intentionally misstate something so the other side corrects you with truthful information they otherwise wouldn't volunteer.
- Avoid "story stealing"—the fallacy of common ground where you jump in with "the same thing happened to me," which squashes the other person's feeling of being understood.
- Make the other side *feel* heard by describing back to them what they just went through, not by simply proving you intellectually understood.
- Start every negotiation by removing yourself as a threat through a calm, non-threatening tone and genuine concern for the other side's situation.
- Anchor the objective of negotiation in long-term trust and mutual prosperity rather than one-time extraction.
- Use "tactical empathy": label emotions, mirror, paraphrase, and state negatives before positives to demonstrate real understanding and appreciation of the other side's expertise.
- Make the other side's case for them first, especially on price or value, so they run out of counter-arguments before you apply pressure.
- Before applying hard pressure, calculate whether you will ever need a future relationship with the counterpart; if yes, stay collaborative.
- Help children think instead of ordering them; challenge them incrementally rather than defaulting to "because I said so."
- Treat curiosity as a superpower, but pair it with a hard stop and a clear progress goal so meetings don't become enjoyable but unproductive.
- Make the counterpart feel like the most interesting person at the table, because that accelerates bonding and deal-making more than appearing charming yourself.

## Failure modes named
- Hijack to correct: interrupting to fix a detail, which kills real listening.
- Hijack to relate / story stealing: turning someone else's story into your story and making them feel one-upped.
- Win-lose / battle-of-arguments mindset, which destroys trust and repeat collaboration.
- Power negotiation: trying to force the other side through intimidation, threats, or leverage rather than removing yourself as a threat.
- Confusing being heard with feeling understood; intellectual agreement without emotional validation.
- Using aggressive price tactics when a future relationship is needed, which makes the counterpart unwilling to help later.
- Parenting default of coercion ("because I said so"), teaching kids that force is the only way to get things done.
- Curiosity without a clock: pleasant conversations that produce no progress and waste the other side's time.

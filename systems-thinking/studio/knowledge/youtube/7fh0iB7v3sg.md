# Back to Basics–Annual Incentive Design

This episode defines annual incentive plans as near-term, typically one-year, cash-oriented programs and walks through selecting metrics, calibrating payout leverage, and avoiding common design and governance pitfalls.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Define an annual incentive plan by its one-year time horizon, near-term operational focus, and cash orientation rather than by vehicle or form alone.
- Select metrics by triangulating internal strategic priorities, peer benchmarking, long-term value-creation alignment, and how the organization communicates success.
- Anchor the plan on a financial success metric (e.g., EBITDA, operating income, net income) before adding supporting operational or strategic metrics.
- Translate long-term strategy into annual building-block goals (cost reduction, safety, inventory management, etc.) that may reduce near-term profit but create long-term value, at corporate, business-unit, or individual levels.
- Manage the tension between metrics with strong individual line of sight and metrics clearly tied to shareholder value by calibrating the right mix for each level.
- Design the payout curve around a bell-curve expectation: roughly 1–2 years in 10 at or below threshold, 1–2 at or above maximum, and 70–80% within the range.
- Set a threshold payout (commonly 50% of target, sometimes 25%) and a maximum payout (commonly 150–200% of target), with interpolation between threshold, target, and maximum.
- Calibrate goal ranges relative to where the metric sits on the income statement (e.g., revenue ±3–5%, profit ±15–20%) rather than blindly applying rules of thumb like 80/120.
- Use metric-specific caps, enhancers/kickers, or individual/strategic modifiers to create targeted upside without necessarily raising the overall plan maximum.
- Back-test plan cost and appropriateness with a profit-sharing ratio, measuring how much incremental performance is shared with participants versus retained by the company.
- Apply individual performance components only where a robust performance-management system, consistent goal setting, and proxy-disclosure readiness exist, especially at the senior executive level.

## Failure modes named
- Rewarding individual effort or results when the company or business unit was not successful overall.
- Over-relying on simplistic rules of thumb (e.g., 80/120) without adjusting for business volatility, size, or income-statement position.
- Setting goals so easy that executives max out annually without sustained outperformance, undermining incentive value.
- Creating feast-or-famine outcomes by placing threshold or maximum too close to typical performance.
- Pushing individual performance differentiation too deeply without the performance-management infrastructure to support consistent measurement.
- Failing to assess the aggregate profit-sharing ratio and whether the board is comfortable with how much value participants capture across the leverage curve.
- Treating senior executive individual performance as too bespoke or avoiding public distinction between high and low performers in proxy disclosures.
- Letting the plan swing to an extreme on corporate-only or individual-only metrics instead of balancing corporate success, business-unit results, and individual contribution.

# Small-World Networks: "Six-degrees of separation" - John D Barrow Gresham College maths lecture

John D. Barrow explains Stanley Milgram's letter-passing experiment and a simple branching calculation to show why large social networks exhibit short average path lengths known as "six degrees of separation."

## Procedure / models distilled
- Use an incomplete-address letter-passing task: give participants a target and the instruction “get this letter closer to its destination,” then track each hop to measure social path length.
- Interpret the resulting average hop count as the “degrees of separation” for the network.
- Treat Milgram’s Omaha-to-Boston experiment as the canonical empirical demonstration that large populations can be spanned by short acquaintance chains.
- Map the small-world pattern across domains: neural networks, business networks, and other acquaintance structures, not just social ties.
- Estimate reach with an exponential branching model: if each person knows ~100 people, then n steps away reaches roughly 10^(2n+2) acquaintances.
- Compare the exponential reach estimate to the total population size to find the order-of-magnitude number of hops needed to span the network.
- Correct the naive estimate by accounting for overlapping friends and double-counted contacts, which raises the predicted lower bound from about four to about six steps.
- Recognize that efficient short paths depend on hubs and random long-range links that “jump across” the network, not only on local chain-like connections.
- Distinguish high-connectivity “famous” nodes from low-connectivity outliers; isolated individuals are the real counterexamples, not celebrities.
- Use second- and third-degree acquaintances as the main source of diverse information and opportunity, because first-degree friends tend to be highly similar to you.

## Failure modes named
- Believing links must run in a straight, chain-like line rather than jumping through hubs or random long-range ties.
- Using famous or highly connected people as the test case and then overestimating how close everyone is.
- Ignoring isolated or low-connectivity individuals as the real exceptions that inflate average path length.
- Confusing “friends” with the full useful network; close friends are too similar to you to provide diverse inputs.
- Neglecting overlap and double-counting in the exponential acquaintance model, which produces an unrealistically low path-length estimate.
- Treating a single average number such as six degrees as a universal guarantee for every pair of individuals.

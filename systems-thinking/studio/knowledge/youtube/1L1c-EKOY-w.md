# Systems Theory of Organizations

This video introduces systems theory as an alternative to the classical machine metaphor of organizations, presenting organizations as open, interdependent systems shaped by inputs, throughputs, outputs, feedback, entropy, and equifinality.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Reframe organizations as open living systems rather than closed machines, prioritizing description and explanation over control and the search for one best way.
- Map organizational activity as a flow of inputs (resources, information), throughputs/processes (internal work), and outputs (products, services, profit, waste).
- Treat organizational boundaries as permeable so that resources, information, and energy can move between the system and its environment.
- Scan the environment through boundary-spanning leaders who track competitors, customers, vendors, technology, and economic conditions.
- Apply holism: view the organization as a whole that is greater than the sum of its interdependent parts.
- Identify nested subsystems (organization → departments → teams) and anticipate that changes in one part ripple through others.
- Treat goals as contingent and negotiated, adapting them as the situation and environment develop.
- Use equifinality to reject the single best way while still recognizing that not all alternatives are equally effective.
- Deploy negative feedback to detect and correct deviations back toward system goals.
- Use positive feedback to amplify desired behavior, while guarding against accidentally rewarding harmful behavior.
- Recognize entropy: left alone, systems naturally deteriorate toward disorganization.
- Invest energy, resources, and information to achieve homeostasis/equilibrium and counteract entropy.
- Build on systems theory as a foundation for later frameworks such as complex adaptive systems, learning organizations, and loosely coupled systems.

## Failure modes named
- Classical-management trap: viewing the organization as a machine and pursuing the one best way to maximize efficiency and control.
- Equifinality misconception: interpreting "no one best way" to mean all possible ways are equally valid.
- Misapplied positive feedback: rewarding destructive behavior and unintentionally amplifying it across the system.
- Entropy neglect: failing to maintain the system, allowing it to run down and deteriorate.
- Isolation fallacy: analyzing parts in isolation instead of accounting for interdependence and systemic ripple effects.

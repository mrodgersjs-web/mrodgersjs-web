# The Laws of Thermodynamics, Entropy, and Gibbs Free Energy

A conceptual introduction to the laws of thermodynamics, entropy as disorder and energy dispersal, and Gibbs free energy as the predictor of whether a process will occur spontaneously.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Apply the first law by tracking energy transformations rather than creation or destruction: energy changes forms but is conserved.
- Use entropy as a disorder model: the universe and isolated systems tend toward states of higher entropy.
- Use entropy as an energy-dispersal model: entropy measures how spread out energy is among the available ways a system can contain energy.
- Use entropy as an information model: ordered states require more descriptive information, while disordered states require less, making higher entropy thermodynamically favorable.
- Predict heat flow direction by entropy increase: heat spontaneously moves from hot to cold because dispersal increases disorder.
- Apply the third law by treating a perfectly crystalline solid at absolute zero as the state of zero entropy.
- Distinguish entropy from enthalpy: entropy is measured in joules per kelvin and describes energy distribution, not total energy.
- Predict spontaneity with Gibbs free energy: calculate ΔG = ΔH − TΔS.
- Read the sign of ΔG: negative means spontaneous; positive means nonspontaneous.
- Decompose driving forces into enthalpy and entropy: a spontaneous process must be enthalpically favorable, entropically favorable, or both; it cannot be neither.
- Tune temperature to favor entropy-driven processes: a positive ΔS becomes more influential as temperature rises.
- Tune temperature to favor enthalpy-driven processes: a negative ΔS becomes less costly at lower temperatures.
- Allow for local order despite global increase: ordered structures can form spontaneously when they are enthalpically favorable, provided total universal entropy still increases.

## Failure modes named
- Misapplying the second law to claim that order cannot arise spontaneously.
- Treating entropy as a measure of energy rather than a measure of energy distribution.
- Overlooking that locally ordered structures can form when they lower the system's enthalpy.

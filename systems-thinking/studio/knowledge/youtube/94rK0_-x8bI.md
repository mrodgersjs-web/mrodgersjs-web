# Epistemology in Philosophy Simply Explained (Past to Present Day Theory)

This video traces epistemology from the traditional "justified true belief" model of knowledge through Gettier cases, internalist/externalist theories of justification, skeptical challenges, and modern Bayesian probabilistic accounts.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Sort epistemological questions into two categories: the nature of knowledge and the extent/limits of what can be known.
- Classify knowledge by type: acquaintance knowledge (objects), procedural knowledge (skills), and propositional knowledge (facts).
- Identify sources of knowledge: perception, introspection, memory, testimony, and reason.
- Apply Kant's distinction between a priori knowledge (independent of experience, via reason) and a posteriori knowledge (dependent on sense experience).
- Analyze propositional knowledge through the traditional three-part model: belief + truth + justification (JTB).
- Test candidate knowledge against Gettier cases to detect when a justified true belief is still only accidentally true and therefore not genuine knowledge.
- Choose between internalism (justification depends solely on factors in the believer's mind) and externalism (justification can include factors outside the mind, such as perception or process reliability).
- Use reliabilism to evaluate whether a true belief was produced by a reliable belief-forming process.
- Map the structure of justification as either coherentist (beliefs mutually support each other like a spider web) or foundationalist (beliefs rest on other beliefs like a skyscraper).
- Apply Agrippa's Trilemma / the Regress Problem to interrogate whether a chain of justification terminates in basic beliefs, circles back on itself, or runs infinitely.
- Run Cartesian skepticism by asking whether perceptions could be systematically deceptive, as in a dream, an evil demon scenario, or a brain-in-a-vat simulation.
- Distinguish qualitative identity (similarity of experience) from numerical identity (one and the same object) when inferring that objects persist over time.
- Detect the problem of induction by checking whether arguments for the uniformity of nature secretly assume the conclusion they are trying to prove.
- Replace binary certainty with Bayesian epistemology: treat beliefs as credences (degrees of probability), update them with evidence, and combine new data with prior probabilities.

## Failure modes named
- Epistemic luck producing true beliefs without genuine knowledge.
- Gettier cases where justified true beliefs are true only by accident.
- Coherent systems of belief that are internally consistent yet factually false.
- Circular reasoning in coherentist justification.
- Infinite regress of justification undermining foundationalist claims.
- Equivocation between qualitative and numerical identity in arguments for persistent objects.
- Circular defense of the principle of the uniformity of nature (the problem of induction).
- Reliable belief-forming processes that produce true beliefs without the believer's awareness or justification (Mr. Truetemp).
- Assuming that 100% certainty is required for knowledge.

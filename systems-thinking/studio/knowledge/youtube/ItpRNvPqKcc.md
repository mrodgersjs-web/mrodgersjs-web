# Charles Perrow - The cutting edge in organization theory?

Charles Perrow explains how U.S. society became an organizational society through legal, industrial, and financial centralization, while tracing the forgotten alternative paths (flexible production, regional railroads) that lost to mass production and finance capitalism.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Model society as an organization: trace how functions once performed by families, communities, peer groups, and small standalone organizations (healthcare, recreation, religion, shopping, marriage counseling, even funerals) have been absorbed into large corporations.
- Apply the Normal Accidents framework to social systems: recognize that as many large organizations become tightly coupled and interactively complex, unexpected interactions can produce large-scale problems.
- Compare alternative organizational models rather than assuming a single trajectory, e.g., mass production versus flexible production in early U.S. textile mills.
- Use case comparisons to identify structural differences: Lowell-style mass production involved tight labor control and wage dependency, while Philadelphia-style flexible production allowed skilled labor mobility and local entrepreneurship.
- Trace the legal preconditions of big organizations: limited liability, corporate legal personhood, and the right of one organization to own others enabled the concentration of capital and power starting around 1800.
- Treat infrastructure as an organizational driver: railroads created the impetus for large-scale corporate capitalism and the multi-divisional firm, but be ready to question the standard causal story.
- Recover forgotten policy debates by searching newspapers, Senate debates, and archives rather than relying only on canonical business histories.
- Map centralizing forces: finance capital preferred a few large, centralized firms because they were easier to finance, controlled markets, and set prices.
- Evaluate counterfactual paths: a regional railroad system could have decentralized industry, wealth, and political power instead of concentrating them in a few metropolitan centers.
- Treat U.S. privatized capitalism as a historically specific type of organizational society, not a universal default, by contrasting it with state-provided systems in other countries.
- Identify the expansion of “life inside the company” as an indicator of organizational penetration into everyday social and personal domains.

## Failure modes named
- Teleological history: assuming the centralized, national, mass-production outcome was inevitable and ignoring the regionalist and flexible-production alternatives that were actively contested.
- Normal Accidents in social systems: unexpected, large-scale failures emerging from tight coupling and interactive complexity among big organizations.
- Wage dependency and labor subordination produced by mass-production models that rely on tight control and wage suppression.
- Concentration of wealth and power when finance capital backs centralized firms that dominate markets and pricing.
- Loss of public provision when organizational absorption replaces government or community services (e.g., U.S. healthcare tied to employers).
- Historiographic blind spots: canonical accounts can omit major debates because later winners wrote the narrative.

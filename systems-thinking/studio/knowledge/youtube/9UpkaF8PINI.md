# The Harvard Principles of Negotiation

A concise introduction to Fisher and Ury's principled-negotiation method: separate people from the problem, focus on interests behind positions, invent options for mutual gain, and insist on objective criteria while protecting yourself with a strong BATNA.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Separate the person from the issue: treat the other party as a partner in problem-solving, not an opponent to defeat; manage emotions, ego, and relationship dynamics.
- Use empathy and active listening: imagine the other side’s position, let them vent, identify sources of strong emotion, and aim to be understood rather than to “win.”
- Focus on interests, not positions: ask “why” behind positions and “why not” when proposals are rejected; surface underlying concerns, fears, and desires.
- Rank and acknowledge interests for both sides: list each party’s interests and rank them so you know where trade-offs are possible.
- Generate options for mutual gain: separate idea creation from judgment, broaden options before narrowing, and look for shared or complementary interests.
- Make the other side’s decision easy: craft proposals that satisfy their self-interest and are low-cost for you but high-value for them, and vice versa.
- Insist on objective criteria: anchor decisions in independent facts, standards, or common practice (e.g., book value, market norms) to depersonalize disputes.
- Develop your BATNA: brainstorm alternatives to agreement, improve the best one, and never enter a negotiation blind to what you will do if no deal is reached.
- Deploy negotiation jiu-jitsu: when attacked with positions, ask for reasons; when ideas are attacked, ask for specifics; when personally attacked, recast it as an attack on the problem.
- Use the one-text approach: bring in a third party to draft a proposal based on both sides’ interests, gather comments, redraft, and iterate toward agreement.
- Handle dirty tricks with a three-step response: recognize the tactic, call it out to participants, and negotiate the rules of the negotiation before continuing.

## Failure modes named
- Positional bargaining: head-to-head haggling over fixed positions, producing poor outcomes, inefficiency, and damaged relationships.
- Splitting the difference as a default: simple compromise can miss creative solutions that serve both parties’ real interests.
- Judging ideas too quickly during option generation, which stifles creativity.
- Narrowing options too quickly before exploring a wide range of possibilities.
- Fixed-pie or win-lose thinking: assuming one side’s gain must be the other side’s loss.
- Ignoring the other party’s interests, making agreement impossible.
- Entering negotiations without a developed BATNA, weakening your leverage.
- Being drawn into confrontation when the other side uses personal attacks or win-lose tactics.
- Dirty tricks such as leaking information to the press, pressure tactics, and calculated delays.
- Treating negotiation as a debating competition rather than a joint problem-solving exercise.

# Systems thinking as it applies to systems engineering

Joseph Kasser argues that systems thinking alone cannot solve problems; engineers must combine nine holistic thinking perspectives, domain knowledge, and inherited/generic requirements to move from what a customer asks for to what they actually need.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Apply nine holistic thinking perspectives to any system: big picture, operational, functional, structural, generic, continuum, temporal, quantitative, and scientific.
- Group the perspectives by purpose: external perspectives (big picture, operational) understand the situation as a whole; internal perspectives (functional, structural, continuum, quantitative, temporal) analyze how it works; progressive and remaining perspectives generate solutions.
- Match the perspective to the problem: use internal/external lenses to understand a situation, then deliberately shift to progressive/remaining lenses to find solutions.
- Manage complexity by abstraction and hierarchy: focus on the level you need, one level up and one level down, hiding internal detail of subsystems you are not examining.
- Partition systems by observable coupling and behavior; redraw boundaries when interactions cross supposed subsystem lines or when components are decoupled from the rest.
- Treat every system as fractal: any subsystem can be re-framed as a system with its own boundary and adjacent systems, and vice versa.
- Position any engineering activity in a layered framework to identify which domain knowledge, life-cycle state, and perspective apply.
- Build a holistic requirements model that includes not only stated customer requirements but also system-generic requirements inherited from similar systems, laws, regulations, and environmental constraints.
- Distinguish three levels of practice: the engineer who delivers what is asked for, the good engineer who delivers what is wanted, and the outstanding engineer who delivers what is needed.
- Use a situation awareness state model to recognize when something is wrong or improvable, decide to change, and move through action and stabilization.

## Failure modes named
- Believing systems thinking by itself solves problems, when it actually explains emergent behavior but does not reveal where solutions come from.
- Teaching systems engineering as isolated parts and processes without showing how to synthesize complete solutions.
- Demanding all requirements up front while ignoring that requirements evolve over time.
- Optimizing a subsystem independently or optimizing "the system as a whole" without recognizing that every system is itself a subsystem of a larger system.
- Relying on modeling tools or MBSE without populating the model with inherited, legal, regulatory, and generic requirements.
- Letting the customer keep changing requirements because the engineer only captured asked or wanted requirements, not the underlying needed ones.
- Failing to communicate prevention work, so projects without visible problems receive no recognition while only squeaky wheels get attention.
- Mapping all systems engineering practice to a single layer or phase and dismissing other valid perceptions as wrong.

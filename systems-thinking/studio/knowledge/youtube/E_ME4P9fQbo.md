# Are University Admissions Biased? | Simpson's Paradox Part 2

Aggregate acceptance rates can hide or reverse the true pattern because the actual decision happens at a finer level, while the forces that push groups into different subgroups may themselves be the real source of inequality.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Compare outcomes at both the aggregate level and the relevant subgroup level to detect confounding from unequal group composition.
- Recognize that fairness within each decision unit does not guarantee fairness at the aggregate level when applicant pools differ across units.
- Identify the confounder: the distribution of applicants across departments or categories drives aggregate rates as much as per-category selection rates.
- Distinguish between bias in the selection rule and bias in the allocation of applications.
- Locate the real locus of discrimination by checking whether disparities appear at the evaluation point or earlier in the pipeline.
- Consider adversarial institutional design: a policy maker can obscure discrimination by steering groups toward harder or more competitive categories.
- Map reputational, cultural, and structural signals that shape who applies where, not only who gets selected.
- Read aggregate disparities as symptoms requiring decomposition rather than as direct evidence of bias.
- Trace causes upstream to funding, prestige, professional prospects, and social stereotypes that create unequal application distributions.
- Treat the absence of detectable bias at one decision stage as inconclusive about bias in the broader system.
- Use disaggregated statistics to identify where bias enters the system and where it does not.

## Failure modes named
- Interpreting aggregate acceptance rates as direct proof of discrimination at the point of decision.
- Assuming fairness within subgroups automatically eliminates aggregate unfairness.
- Concluding "no bias exists" simply because no bias appears at one isolated stage.
- Ignoring structural and societal forces that steer groups toward different opportunities before selection occurs.
- Mistaking Simpson's paradox for a numerical curiosity instead of a signal about social mechanisms.

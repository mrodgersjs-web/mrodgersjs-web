# Prospect Theory Explained || Why Losses Feel Worse Than Gains

Prospect theory explains that people evaluate choices relative to a reference point, feel losses more intensely than equivalent gains, and shift between risk-averse and risk-seeking behavior depending on whether outcomes are framed as gains or losses.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Use the gain/loss choice test to surface risk preferences: most people prefer a certain gain over a gamble but prefer a gamble over a certain loss.
- Apply the value-function model, an S-shaped curve that is steeper for losses than for gains, to represent asymmetric emotional weighting.
- Evaluate outcomes as deviations from a reference point rather than as final states of wealth.
- Frame choices as gains or losses to change their perceived attractiveness without changing their objective value.
- Use positive framing (e.g., "90% success rate") to increase acceptance when persuasion depends on perceived gains.
- Recognize loss aversion: a loss of a given magnitude generates more negative feeling than an equivalent gain generates positive feeling.
- Identify domain-dependent risk attitudes: risk-averse in gain domains and risk-seeking in loss domains.
- Map insurance behavior as accepting a small certain loss (the premium) to avoid a larger uncertain loss.
- Detect sunk-loss behavior such as holding losing stocks too long to avoid realizing or admitting the loss.
- Reframe decisions around objective reality to counter the immediate emotional bias produced by loss aversion.
- Audit how the reference point shifts the subjective value of identical dollar outcomes.
- Balance emotional responses with clear-headed assessment before acting under uncertainty.
- Use prospect theory to diagnose why seemingly irrational choices follow predictable psychological patterns.

## Failure modes named
- Assuming human decisions follow pure logic rather than emotionally weighted value curves.
- Treating gains and losses as symmetric when designing offers, policies, or communications.
- Using loss-framed language when trying to encourage acceptance of a cautious choice.
- Holding depreciating assets too long to avoid the pain of admitting a loss.
- Making impulsive decisions driven by immediate emotional reactions to gains or losses.
- Ignoring how a reference point changes whether an outcome is perceived as a gain or a loss.
- Falling for discount framing without comparing the absolute underlying price.

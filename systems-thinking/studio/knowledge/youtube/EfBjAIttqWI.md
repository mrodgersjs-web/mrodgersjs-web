# Resilience Engineering for Systems Engineering - Part 1

David Woods reframes systems engineering as an adaptive, pressure-saturated process that creates the very complexity it tries to manage, and argues resilience engineering is the discipline of continuously reframing and extending adaptive capacity before saturation propagates failure.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Treat systems engineering itself—not just its artifacts—as a unit of adaptive behavior embedded in a tangled, layered, multi-actor network.
- Use anomalies as prompts to update, revise, and reframe your model of the world instead of dismissing or localizing them.
- Hold both a local (inside a role) and a regional (cross-role, cross-organization) perspective to reveal interdependencies and hidden couplings.
- Expect that disruption forces reframing but also triggers counter-adaptation, retrenchment, and blame-shifting by actors defending old models.
- Map the pressures (faster, better, cheaper, growth, efficiency, scale) that drive complexification so you can see when coping mechanisms create new complexity.
- Monitor the approach to saturation, not just saturation itself, and build graceful extensibility before limits are reached.
- Avoid “going big late” near saturation; late, local responses propagate trouble across interdependent units.
- Design for reciprocity: build relationships where roles stretch across boundaries to help each other under load rather than retreating into silos.
- Reject the semi-omniscient outside observer as a fictional comfort; work from inside the constraints of the adaptive universe.
- Provide paths and reshape constraints rather than selling one-size solutions; enable guided continuous adaptability across many interacting units.
- Avoid “bang-bang” control—overcorrecting between extremes in a restricted trade space—and instead expand the range of adaptive behavior.

## Failure modes named
- Retrenchment and localization of blame after disruption (“it was just that plant/utility/crew”) to avoid reframing.
- Work-to-rule and retreat into role boundaries when saturation approaches, cutting off cross-role help.
- Treating organizational and human factors as quantifiable line items in a risk analysis instead of core adaptive dynamics.
- The semi-omniscient outside observer / independence assumption, which imagines analysis can stand apart from the system.
- Waiting too long and acting too big near saturation, propagating load and failure across interdependencies.
- Bang-bang control: oscillating between extremes in an unstable mode because the adaptive range is too narrow.
- Slow, fragmented, stale systems engineering that cannot match the velocity of change in the world.

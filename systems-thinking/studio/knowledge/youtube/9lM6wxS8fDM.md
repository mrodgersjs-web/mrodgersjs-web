# High Reliability Organizing

A 2007 Wildland Fire seminar distills high-reliability organizing as a set of five daily principles for making sense of the unexpected, updating beyond initial frames, translating concepts into local practice, and teaching behavior before terminology so reliability becomes embedded in operations.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Treat HRO as five guiding principles for day-to-day practice rather than as abstract processes or a cookbook.
- Manage the unexpected by continuously updating your picture of the situation; repeatedly ask “Is it still current? Is it still accurate?”
- Update outside the initial frame; question whether the problem might be a different kind than the one first labeled.
- Before acting, diagnose how people are framing, labeling, biasing, or missing clues; then choose appropriate action.
- Watch for early signals that reality is diverging from expectations; small anomalies are clues before failures escalate.
- Build sensitivity to operations through briefings, ongoing status checks, and honest after-action reviews.
- Use rookies and diverse crew members as extra sensors; listen to perspectives that experienced veterans may filter out.
- Start with concrete behavior and local examples when teaching reliability; introduce terms only after people recognize the practice.
- Run after-action reviews as concrete practice first; repeated useful experience builds the attitude and motivation to keep doing them.
- Translate each principle into local agency language and routines so it does not sound like imported jargon.
- Model the principles rather than naming them; ask “What could go wrong here?” in ordinary work conversations.
- Defer to expertise wherever it currently sits; move uncertain decisions to the person with the best understanding of the situation.
- Encourage self-reporting of errors and near misses as a group ethic of mutual safety rather than blame.
- Replace checklist ritual with mindful interaction; meetings and candid dialogue often surface what checklists hide.

## Failure modes named
- Updating only within an existing frame, missing that the situation is a fundamentally different kind of problem.
- Labels, biases, and blind spots causing teams to miss early signals of trouble.
- Resistance to “HRO” terminology or acronym fatigue, which makes people tune out.
- After-action reviews performed as empty ritual without real learning.
- Treating HRO as an abstract cookbook, checklist program, or flavor-of-the-month initiative.
- Egos and disrespectful interaction shutting down communication after an incident.
- Checklist proliferation substituting for thinking and degrading operational awareness.
- Discounting inexperienced members whose fresh observations could reveal what veterans miss.
- Assuming changed attitudes produce changed behavior, rather than acting your way into new thinking.

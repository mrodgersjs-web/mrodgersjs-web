# Keynote - Software Architecture, Team Topologies and Complexity Science - James Lewis - NDC Porto

James Lewis reframes microservices, software architecture, and org design through complexity science—arguing that successful scaling comes from building small-world, value-flowing networks of teams and services rather than deepening hierarchies.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Design software as decoupled, cohesive services organized around business capabilities, using decentralized data and governance rather than shared monolithic dependencies.
- Apply Team Topologies archetypes as socio-technical patterns for creating small-world networks, minimizing the hierarchy needed to coordinate work.
- Use Conway’s Law deliberately: align team communication structures with desired architecture so the organization’s shape produces the desired system boundaries.
- Optimize organizational design for the flow of value from idea to a production-like state, measuring success by sustainably reducing lead time to “thank you.”
- Scale by adding small, loosely coupled teams or products at the edges (city-style growth) instead of deepening management layers.
- Constrain team and office size by Dunbar’s numbers to preserve the strong personal ties that make small-world networks effective.
- Create forcing functions—such as two-pizza teams and API-first interaction—that make independent team autonomy the path of least resistance.
- Recognize the two scaling regimes: hierarchies produce sublinear returns and rising mortality, while small-world networks produce superlinear returns.
- Treat organizations as complex adaptive systems where speed is governed by network topology and interaction patterns, not just individual team competence.
- Protect product development and innovation from being subordinated to cost-cutting, risk management, and governance, which increase constraints as companies age.
- Consider the “fractal dimension” of team and software boundaries as a health indicator: more crinkly, edge-growth structures suggest healthier scaling.

## Failure modes named
- Assuming “more people always means slower” without seeing that network topology, not headcount alone, determines communication overhead.
- Deepening hierarchy to scale, which imposes a sublinear ceiling on improvement and eventually slows the organization’s metabolic rate.
- Letting governance, risk, and cost efficiency subsume product development and R&D as the company ages.
- Centralizing decisions among people farthest from the work, degrading information quality and value flow.
- Accidental Conway’s Law: ignoring team communication patterns and ending up with an architecture that mirrors an undesirable org chart.
- Confusing economies of scale (cheaper infrastructure) with returns to scale (faster value creation): hierarchies deliver the former while choking the latter.
- Scaling offices or teams beyond Dunbar’s number, breaking the social ties that enable small-world superlinear effects.

# Premortem Explained as a Change Intervention: Mitigating biases and managing risk in planning

The video presents the premortem as a future-imagining group exercise that surfaces hidden risks, counters optimism and authority biases, and produces a reverse roadmap of critical failure and success factors before a project or decision is committed to.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Schedule the premortem in one of the last meetings before a major decision or project rollout, allocating 30–90 minutes for a first run and as little as 2–5 minutes once habitual.
- Split the decision-making group into two teams: the “Black Swan” (pre-mortem) team and the “White Swan” (pre-parade) team.
- Ask the Black Swan team to vividly describe the worst possible future outcome of the project/decision in detail.
- Ask the White Swan team to vividly describe the best possible future outcome in equal detail.
- Debrief each group round-robin, collecting concrete reasons the project might fail and reasons it might succeed even better than expected.
- Prevent senior stakeholders from speaking first and cap individual contributions so junior or quieter participants can speak up.
- Work backward from each imagined future scenario to build a reverse roadmap of red flags, critical failure factors, and critical success factors.
- Use prompt questions such as: “What could slow us down or make us miss a deadline?”, “What are we already nervous about?”, “What went wrong in analogous past projects?”, “How quickly can we respond?”, and “Who owns each risk area?”.
- Appoint a minute taker to document every identified risk and success factor, assign owners, and create follow-up tasks.
- Hold follow-up review meetings every two weeks or every month to keep risks visible and proactively managed.

## Failure modes named
- Planning fallacy: humans are systematically over-optimistic about timelines, budgets, and complexity, producing cost and schedule overruns.
- Black Swan neglect: treating low-probability, high-impact risks as zero risks because they have never been seen before.
- Authority bias and group silence: experienced participants stay quiet about risks when senior stakeholders or VPs appear unconcerned.
- Senior-first domination: letting the most senior people speak first suppresses diverse warnings and alternative perspectives.
- “Set-up-for-success” complacency: teams feel uncomfortable confronting risks because they already believe the plan is solid.
- Failure to convert insights into action: conducting the exercise without documentation, ownership, and follow-up lets identified risks fade away.

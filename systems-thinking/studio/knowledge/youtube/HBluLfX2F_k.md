# You've (Likely) Been Playing The Game of Life Wrong

Many systems in nature and society follow power-law distributions rather than normal distributions, so the right strategy depends on recognizing which statistical "game" you are actually playing.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Test for power-law behavior by plotting data on a log-log scale and looking for a straight line.
- Distinguish additive systems (produce normal distributions) from multiplicative systems (produce log-normal distributions with long tails) from uncapped power-law systems.
- Recognize Pareto's income law: wealth and income distributions follow a power law across countries and centuries.
- Compute expected value as the sum over all outcomes of probability multiplied by payoff.
- Understand the St. Petersburg paradox: an uncapped multiplicative payout can produce an infinite expected value even though most individual outcomes are modest.
- Detect heavy-tailed systems by noting when the average keeps rising as more samples are taken and standard deviation becomes infinite or undefined.
- Identify self-organized criticality: systems such as forests, sandpiles, and earthquakes naturally tune themselves to critical points that generate power laws.
- Apply universality: near critical points, systems in the same class behave identically regardless of their underlying physical details.
- Use the preferential-attachment model to explain network power laws: new nodes are more likely to link to already well-connected nodes, producing "rich-get-richer" dynamics.
- Map domain type to strategy: average-driven normal domains reward consistency; power-law domains reward persistence and repeated intelligent bets.
- Price or insure against tail risk when operating in power-law hazard domains.

## Failure modes named
- Assuming every important distribution is normal.
- Using the average as a reliable guide in heavy-tailed systems.
- Treating additive and multiplicative systems with the same decision rule.
- Letting frequent small events lull you into false security before a rare catastrophic one.
- Under-reserving for extreme tail events and going bust when they occur.
- Trying to guarantee average outcomes in domains defined by outliers.
- Avoiding risk in power-law domains, where outliers are the entire game.
- Believing human systems are exempt from the statistical organizational principles seen in physics.

# Conway's Law Explained: How Your Company's Org Chart Shapes Your Products

A company's organizational chart silently designs its products because the communication structures of an organization are copied into the systems it builds, making Conway's law both a diagnostic lens and a lever for intentional redesign.

## Procedure / models distilled
- State Conway's law: organizations that design systems are constrained to produce designs that mirror their internal communication structures.
- Apply the law beyond software to any complex system an organization creates, such as cars, government services, or hospital patient journeys.
- Recognize that poor communication paths produce poor integration; if teams cannot communicate effectively, their components will not fit together smoothly.
- Use the house-building analogy to explain how fragmented team communication creates fragmented physical or digital designs.
- Identify monolithic software architectures with multiple siloed teams as a classic expression of unmanaged Conway's law.
- Trace the root cause to communication overhead and information silos that constrain cross-boundary visibility and cause local optimization.
- Detect Conway's law in action by looking for disjointed products, hard-to-integrate features, persistent cross-team communication breakdowns, redundancy, feature bloat, or inconsistent user experiences.
- Perform the inverse Conway maneuver: intentionally design the organization to match the desired product architecture rather than letting the org chart dictate the product.
- Align small autonomous teams to specific product components or services, as seen in microservices architectures.
- Use agile, cross-functional teams to counteract silos by bringing diverse skills into frequent communication.
- Apply DevOps principles to break down the wall between development and operations so operational concerns are integrated early.
- Actively facilitate communication through shared tools, workspaces, regular cross-team meetings, and informal relationship-building events.
- Combine team autonomy with clear boundaries and interfaces so teams can innovate without creating hidden dependencies.
- Treat leadership as responsible for intentionally designing team structures, reporting lines, and communication channels to support product outcomes.
- Build organizational agility by reconfiguring teams as product needs evolve so structure continues to serve product goals.

## Failure modes named
- Siloed teams producing products that feel like a collection of disparate features rather than a cohesive whole.
- Integration challenges caused by teams that do not communicate, leading to bugs, rework, and frustrating user experiences.
- Redundant functionality built unknowingly by separate teams, wasting resources and increasing complexity.
- Feature bloat and inconsistent user experience resulting from isolated local optimization.
- Stifled innovation when new ideas must pass through slow, disconnected approval and coordination paths.
- Monolithic codebases where many teams push into one giant system without the communication needed for cohesion.
- Blaming individuals for systemic outcomes instead of recognizing the structural forces shaping the product.

# Introduction to System Dynamics: Overview

An overview of MIT’s introductory system dynamics course, framing why linear, open-loop, short-horizon thinking causes policies to fail and introducing feedback, mental models, and simulation as tools for improving high-leverage decision-making in complex systems.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Treat a full semester of system dynamics as necessary to build usable capability, because the skill develops only through repeated practice, not through attending lectures.
- Use active assignments and careful textbook reading as the primary vehicles for learning, since the instructor can only create an opportunity for you to learn.
- Frame organizational difficulty as arising from accelerating change and rising analytic cleverness that outpaces our ability to manage unintended consequences.
- Map any intervention as a feedback loop: decisions change the state of the world, and the new state feeds back to shape the next decision.
- Replace the open-loop “beginning-middle-end” project model with an iterative model in which discovery forces unplanned returns to earlier stages.
- Apply the bicycle-steering metaphor: a goal alone cannot select action; you need feedback about current state to choose the next move.
- Expand the boundary of your mental model to include unintended effects, time delays, non-linearities, stocks and flows, and other autonomous actors with conflicting goals.
- Recognize that “side effects” are simply effects that fall outside your current mental model, and they often feed back opposite to your intentions.
- Use stocks-and-flows diagrams and simulation not to produce “right” answers, but to stress and improve the mental models of stakeholders.
- Identify structure—physics, information flows, and decision rules—as the generator of behavior, rather than attributing outcomes solely to individual intentions.
- Counter the fundamental attribution error by assuming people are intelligent, capable, and caring, and look for systemic causes before blaming individuals.
- Design high-leverage policies by improving shared mental models across the actors needed to make change happen.

## Failure modes named
- Short-term decision horizons and delayed feedback allow managers to be promoted for quick fixes before negative consequences appear.
- Externalizing blame as “unanticipated side effects” instead of recognizing that outcomes partly reflect one’s own prior decisions and mental-model limitations.
- Open-loop project management: using a linear “identify-gather-evaluate-select-implement” sequence that omits feedback and iterative learning.
- Fundamental attribution error: explaining one’s own failures with situational reasons while explaining others’ failures as character or capability defects.
- Acting as if knowing a goal is sufficient to choose action, without feedback about the system’s current state.
- Treating simulation or models as truth rather than as intentionally imperfect tools for insight.

## Studio relevance

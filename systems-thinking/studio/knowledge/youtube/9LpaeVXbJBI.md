# Incentive Mechanisms - Tokenomics Design Course (Part 7)

A tutorial on Step 4 of tokenomics design: mapping who to reward or punish, for what behaviors, and identifying mechanism conflicts and open quantitative questions before setting parameters.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Treat tokenomics as a system for incentivizing user behavior toward a specific protocol goal.
- Before deciding reward size, answer two qualitative questions: who is rewarded, and what behavior is rewarded.
- Distinguish value-creating incentives from value-redistributing incentives; the latter grow briefly but unwind when no fundamentals exist.
- For each stakeholder role, list desired behaviors, frictions preventing them, undesired behaviors, and motivations for the undesired behavior.
- Match incentives and disincentives to each role to amplify desired behaviors and suppress undesired ones.
- Use the worksheet row-by-row: complete all roles first, then analyze conflicts and resolutions across roles.
- Identify mechanism conflicts where an incentive for one role accidentally encourages harmful behavior by another role.
- Resolve conflicts by adding counterbalancing mechanisms rather than removing the original incentive.
- Capture open quantitative questions during the qualitative worksheet, such as slash percentage, reward split, and early-voter premium.
- Use community input and modeling to set governance parameters that the protocol itself may later vote on.
- Expect iteration: discovering incentive problems may require revisiting earlier design steps.

## Failure modes named
- **Value redistribution instead of value creation**: pump-and-dump and ponzinomics structures transfer wealth from latecomers to early adopters without underlying fundamentals, eventually collapsing.
- **Goodharting / metric gaming**: Amazon's "hire to fire" quota led managers to hire employees just to fire them, rewarding turnover without improving talent.
- **Spam and low-quality participation**: rewarding voting engagement can attract malicious or uninformed voters and encourage superficial participation.
- **Punishing good behavior accidentally**: slashing "losing" voters can discourage legitimate dissent and make ordinary token holders abstain to avoid risk.
- **Wait-and-see voter apathy**: if slashing is possible, participants may delay voting, undermining governance participation.

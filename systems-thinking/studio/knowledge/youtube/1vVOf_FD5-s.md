# Debating Deterrence

Dr. Heather Williams and Reagan Draper discuss how Russia's nuclear threats in Ukraine reveal deterrence as a psychological, multi-audience activity, expose the collapse of arms-control architecture, and argue for reframing nuclear policy around human security while pursuing a realistic, three-pronged path away from an arms-control "Dark Ages."

## Procedure / models distilled
- Deterrence as psychological signaling — signals are received by multiple adversaries simultaneously, so a message intended for Putin is also heard by Xi, Kim Jong-un, and other nuclear decision-makers.
- Conventional-nuclear deterrence layering — nuclear weapons can backstop conventional deterrence, but the conventional balance may be the actual mechanism preventing Putin from attacking NATO.
- Risk-manipulation model — unlike classic arms-control assumptions that actors seek to reduce misperception, Putin actively increases and manipulates risk to create misunderstanding and coercive leverage.
- Arms-control pathway fork — after the war, arms control can move toward a "Renaissance" of new agreements or slide into a "Dark Ages" of collapsed treaties and inspections.
- Three-pronged better path — pursue Russia-focused post-war agreements, build China-focused bilateral groundwork for future verifiable limits, and create broader multilateral behavior-based norms and risk-reduction measures.
- Tactical-use response mapping — because the international response to a Russian tactical nuclear strike is undefined, planners should map likely reactions from China, India, NATO, and global humanitarian and economic actors in advance.
- Post-conflict arms-control opportunity window — wars historically create moments to capture lessons, assess missed off-ramps, and negotiate new limits, but only if the combatants' perspectives drive the process.
- Compliance and partnership audit — before entering new agreements, assess an adversary's cheating record and whether domestic audiences will accept partnering with a regime accused of atrocities.
- Tactical-nuclear inclusion rule — any future Russia follow-on treaty must include Russian tactical nuclear weapons or it will be politically unsalable, despite the quantitative mismatch with U.S. holdings.
- Human-security reframing — move nuclear discourse from abstract mushroom-cloud threats and government officials to impacted people and communities, treating resources, reparations, repair, and dismantlement as security tools.
- Human-in-the-loop norm — multilateral forums such as the P5 can adopt behavioral guardrails like keeping humans in nuclear decision loops and negotiating 21st-century incidents-at-sea-style crisis rules.

## Failure modes named
- Treating arms control as purely about reducing misperception when the adversary seeks to intentionally escalate and manipulate risk.
- Allowing a polarized deterrence community to interpret every event as confirmation of pre-existing pro- or anti-nuclear positions rather than updating beliefs.
- Pursuing unrealistic grand visions such as a single trilateral U.S.-Russia-China treaty when China lacks the track record and conditions are not ripe.
- Shifting reliance from nuclear to conventional forces without recognizing that conventional superiority can be equally or more escalatory.
- Framing nuclear weapons only as a future existential threat, which obscures present harms to test-affected communities and enables coercive bullying today.
- Assuming transparency and dialogue are sufficient while Russia violates or cheats on treaties like INF, CFE, and New START.
- Failing to plan for the humanitarian, economic, and alliance-management consequences of a tactical nuclear weapon use.

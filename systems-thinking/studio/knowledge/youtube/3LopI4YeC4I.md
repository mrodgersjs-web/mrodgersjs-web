# Why Being Delusional is a Superpower

Belief in full personal control is a useful delusion that can increase effort and success, but we must simultaneously recognize the outsized role of luck to remain humble, generous, and grounded in reality.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Use the egocentric-bias lens: you vividly remember your own actions and problems but not others', so you systematically overestimate your share of both contributions and blame.
- Apply the compounding-advantage model: small initial differences in age, resources, or coaching access grow over time because early-selected people get more practice, better training, and further opportunities.
- Model high-stakes selection as a weighted sum of skill and luck: even when luck accounts for only ~5% of an outcome, it can determine most of the winners when many equally talented people compete for a few spots.
- Treat "full control" as a self-fulfilling motivational tool: higher perceived control increases effort, which itself raises the probability of success; lower perceived control reduces effort and lowers success.
- Identify status-legitimation feedback: arbitrary status gains are quickly rationalized as deserved, producing entitlement, worse behavior, and reduced generosity.
- Practice external-attribution gratitude: when reflecting on a good outcome, list the external factors beyond your control; this increases generosity, likability, and happiness.
- Counter survivorship bias among successful people: remember that you rarely see the equally talented and hardworking people who failed, so your personal experience exaggerates how fair the world is.
- Use the two-handed success strategy: first, act as if your destiny is entirely in your own hands; second, if you succeed, remember that luck played a major role and use your fortune to increase others' luck.
- Account for the country-of-birth lottery: birthplace and its inherited infrastructure explain roughly half of global income variance, making it a major exogenous determinant of opportunity.
- Notice entitlement cues in arbitrary privilege: randomly assigned leaders take extra rewards and feel they deserve them, revealing how quickly status becomes self-justifying.

## Failure modes named
- Egocentric bias: underestimating what others do and overestimating your own share of work, blame, and merit.
- Luck blindness: failing to see the fortunate events that enabled success because luck is, by definition, not something you did.
- Skill-only attribution: interpreting success as purely the result of talent and hard work, which devalues external support and increases inequality acceptance.
- Survivorship bias: successful leaders infer the world is fair because they do not experience the equally capable people who failed.
- Entitlement from arbitrary status: random gains in power or wealth are rationalized as deserved, reducing empathy and generosity.
- Defensive resistance to chance: people get offended when told luck matters because it feels like it negates their effort and talent.
- Self-reinforcing inequality: the most powerful people, who set societal rules, are also the most likely to underestimate their luck, making them less inclined to contribute to public goods.
- Gratitude suppression: focusing only on personal qualities when explaining good outcomes lowers charitable giving and social likability.

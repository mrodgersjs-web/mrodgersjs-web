# The Expensive Mistake Most Inventors Make with Product Experts | Adam Tavin

Inventors routinely pay far more in hidden coordination costs than they save on hourly rates when they split product development across isolated specialists rather than using one integrated senior engineering team.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Treat product development as a system-level optimization problem, not a sum of local specialist optimizations.
- Evaluate total project cost, not hourly rates, because fragmented teams add a coordination tax.
- Compare one senior engineer’s ability to see cross-phase constraints against five isolated specialists’ narrow focus.
- Involve senior oversight during CAD so that tooling complexity, material selection, and assembly methods are designed for production from day one.
- Choose materials using both functional requirements and production/supply-chain realities before committing.
- Set tolerances based on real-world manufacturing variation rather than ideal lab conditions.
- Design prototypes with production scaling in mind so validation does not require redesign later.
- Use integrated teams to keep communication, file versioning, quality standards, and change management inside one coherent process.
- Build manufacturing partnerships that provide real-time feedback during design rather than after tooling is committed.
- Account for handoff loss: each transition between specialists risks lost design intent, reversed decisions, and extended timelines.
- Recognize that coordination failures typically inflate total project costs by 40–60%.
- Frame the choice as fragmented chaos versus coordinated execution, where integration reduces waste and total cost.

## Failure modes named
- Optimizing by hourly rate and missing the coordination tax.
- Specialists optimizing locally (clean geometry, speed, unit cost) with no one optimizing the whole system.
- CAD geometry that increases tooling complexity or requires expensive secondary operations.
- Prototypes built with materials or methods that cannot scale to manufacturing.
- Manufacturing changes that break the original design intent.
- Serial handoffs that delay discovery of integration problems until rework is required.
- Version-control chaos, mismatched CAD files, and multiple inboxes across vendors.
- Liability confusion and blame-shifting when defects arise between designer, prototype shop, and manufacturer.
- Tolerance stack-ups failing because no single owner understands how all parts interact.
- Quality-control inconsistency due to differing standards between separate companies.
- Exponential change-management complexity when coordinating modifications across multiple vendors.
- Supply-chain surprises from materials selected without production-scale availability checks.

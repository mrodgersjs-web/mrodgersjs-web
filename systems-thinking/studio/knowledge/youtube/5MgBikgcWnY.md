# The first 20 hours -- how to learn anything | Josh Kaufman | TEDxCSU

Josh Kaufman distinguishes expert-level performance from everyday competence, arguing that roughly 20 hours of focused deliberate practice is enough to go from knowing nothing to being reasonably good at a new skill if you deconstruct, self-correct, remove barriers, and persist through initial frustration.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Distinguish expert performance from basic competence: the 10,000-hour rule predicts the top of ultra-competitive fields, not the time needed to learn something new.
- Use the learning-curve model: early practice yields rapid improvement, competence arrives well before expertise, and later gains require disproportionately more effort.
- Deconstruct the skill: decide the exact outcome you want, break the skill into sub-skills, and prioritize the small set of high-frequency sub-skills that drive most real-world performance.
- Learn just enough to self-correct: gather 3–5 resources, then practice while using those resources to notice mistakes and adjust, not to delay starting.
- Remove barriers to practice: eliminate environmental and digital distractions so the default action is to sit down and do the work.
- Pre-commit to at least 20 hours of focused deliberate practice to push through the initial "grossly incompetent and knowing it" frustration barrier.
- Recognize the primary barrier as emotional, not intellectual: expect to feel stupid at first and treat that feeling as a temporary phase rather than a reason to quit.
- Apply the principle of critical few elements, as in the ukulele example: identify the small set of common chords and songs rather than attempting to master the full vocabulary upfront.

## Failure modes named
- Misinterpreting the 10,000-hour rule as the time required to become reasonably good at a new skill.
- Procrastinating by collecting excessive resources before ever beginning practice.
- Allowing distractions to prevent practice sessions from actually happening.
- Quitting during the early frustration phase because feeling incompetent is emotionally aversive.
- Treating a skill as monolithic instead of deconstructing it into learnable, prioritizable sub-skills.

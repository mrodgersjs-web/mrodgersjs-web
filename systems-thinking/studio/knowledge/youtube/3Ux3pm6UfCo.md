# How to improve your daily decision making: Top 4 cognitive biases you should avoid

This video explains four common cognitive biases that distort everyday judgment—self-serving bias, cognitive fluency/illusion of truth, sunk cost fallacy, and confirmation bias—and offers concrete countermeasures to make better decisions.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Recognize self-serving bias: success is attributed to yourself while failure is blamed on external circumstances, which protects self-esteem but prevents learning.
- Counter self-serving bias by relying on close friends who can call you out and keep your explanations grounded.
- Treat cognitive fluency as a warning signal, not a truth signal: ideas that are easier to process feel more true, but fluency has no logical connection to accuracy.
- Question anything that “sounds about right” before accepting it, because clear fonts, rhymes, and pronounceable names can all inflate perceived validity.
- Apply the sunk-cost rule: money, time, or effort already spent is gone and should not influence whether to continue an investment.
- Decide whether to persist by evaluating only future costs and benefits, not by trying to justify past losses.
- Use the “would I start today?” test to escape sunk-cost traps in projects, movies, relationships, or investments.
- Identify confirmation bias: people naturally search for evidence that supports what they already believe and ignore contradictory evidence.
- Counter confirmation bias by actively seeking disconfirming evidence before making a decision.
- Frame questions neutrally, because asking “Are you happy...?” versus “Are you unhappy...?” activates selective memory retrieval and changes reported outcomes.
- Practice falsification-style thinking: if you believe a stock will rise, first ask why it might fall; if you believe a treatment works, first ask why it might not.

## Failure modes named
- Self-serving bias: taking credit for wins and blaming losses on outside factors, blocking self-improvement.
- Illusion of truth via cognitive fluency: mistaking ease of processing for accuracy.
- Sunk cost fallacy: throwing good time or money after bad because of loss aversion.
- Confirmation bias: only noticing evidence that supports prior beliefs.
- Loss aversion: the emotional pain of waste keeps people stuck in losing situations.
- Selective memory retrieval: question framing prompts recall of only confirming experiences.
- Surface-confidence errors: pronounceable stock tickers or pretty fonts tricking investors into overvaluation.
- Social blind spots: lack of honest feedback allowing biased self-justification to persist.

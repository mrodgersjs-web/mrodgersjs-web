# Understanding media through moisture retention curves

The video explains how to read moisture retention curves—volumetric water content versus tension (matrix potential)—to compare hydroponic growing media, set irrigation thresholds, and avoid overwatering or underwatering.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Read a moisture retention curve as volumetric water content on the y-axis versus tension Φ (negative kilopascals) on the x-axis.
- Compute volumetric water content as the volume of water divided by the total volume of the medium; it ranges from 0 to 1 (0–100%).
- Recognize the leftmost flat region, where tension rises little while water content stays high, as the overwatered/anaerobic zone with limited oxygen.
- Use the steep middle decline as the desirable high-air zone, where plants draw water with little effort.
- Treat the flat tail approaching about −300 kPa as the underwater/wilting zone, where most plants can no longer extract water and the medium can pull water out of roots.
- Compare any curve to the ideal shape: near-complete saturation, a very small overwatered zone, a steep drop, and a long tail before −300 kPa.
- Compare any curve to the worst shape: low saturation, a large overwatered zone, and water only becoming available near −300 kPa.
- Set irrigation trigger points to the medium-specific tension range rather than generic soil values; for example, rockwool is typically irrigated near −7 kPa, not −60 kPa.
- Match tensiometer sensitivity to the medium’s usable tension window; rockwool requires extremely sensitive tensiometers because its active range is only a few kilopascals.
- Use the starting saturation point to estimate watering frequency: media that start lower require more frequent irrigation.
- Interpret particle-size effects: smaller particles increase tension for the same water loss and usually improve watering uniformity; larger particles may channel water despite a better-looking curve.
- Benchmark new media by overlaying their curves on known references such as coco, perlite, rockwool, and peat.
- Do not rely on moisture retention curves alone; also evaluate aeration, porosity, and homogeneous wetting before selecting a medium.

## Failure modes named
- Keeping high-retention media near saturation traps roots in the overwatered zone and causes oxygen starvation.
- Applying soil irrigation thresholds to rockwool lets the medium dry out and become hydrophobic before the tensiometer reading becomes meaningful.
- Using standard tensiometers for rockwool misses the narrow active tension window and gives unreliable irrigation signals.
- Selecting media with very low water retention without increasing irrigation frequency leaves plants water-stressed between irrigations.
- Assuming a good retention curve means a good medium while ignoring channeling or uneven water distribution in coarse-particle substrates.
- Interpreting the long flat tail near −300 kPa as usable water, leading to underwatering and wilting.

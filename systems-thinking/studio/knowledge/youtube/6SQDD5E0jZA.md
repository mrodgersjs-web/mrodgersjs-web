# Epistemology: How Do I Know?  | Episode 1807 | Closer To Truth

A panel-led tour through core epistemological questions, treating knowledge as belief connected to facts in the right way, examining justification, skepticism, contextualism, testimony, holism, and the limits of knowing the divine.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Treat epistemology as the theory of knowledge and justification, not merely knowledge alone.
- Define knowledge as belief connected to the relevant fact in the "right way" through perception, memory, reasoning, testimony, or causal chains.
- Test candidate knowledge against Gettier-style cases where a belief is justified and true yet still not knowledge.
- Map sources of justification onto perception, memory, reasoning, a priori reflection, and testimony.
- Use the causal model to assess future knowledge: evidence that causes both the future event and your belief can ground knowledge, provided the chain is intact.
- Apply contextualism to knowledge attributions: standards for "knowing" shift with context, stakes, and what alternatives are salient.
- Confront skepticism pragmatically by letting it expose hidden biases and unsupported assumptions without trying to live as a global skeptic.
- Distinguish survival/shortcut biases from emotionally loaded biases when diagnosing why you hold a belief.
- Ground scientific knowledge in direct acquaintance and observable data; treat theoretical, unobservable claims probabilistically.
- Accept testimony as a legitimate epistemic source in science, everyday life, and religion.
- Evaluate theories holistically: a single counterexample need not dismantle a theory if auxiliary assumptions can be adjusted.
- Separate cataphatic knowledge of God (positive claims based on divine effects) from apophatic recognition that God infinitely exceeds concepts.
- Treat direct experiential or mystical knowledge as first-person and non-transmittable, not as third-person evidence.

## Failure modes named
- Causal interference, illusion, or hallucination can break the link between fact and belief while leaving the belief intact.
- Justified true belief can still fall short of knowledge.
- Full Cartesian doubt becomes practically paralyzing and can spiral into useless global skepticism.
- Neuroscientific reduction can create circular confidence by doubting direct experience through theoretical claims that are themselves harder to justify.
- Evidentialism overextends scientific evidential standards to domains where testimony, experience, and relational knowledge are primary.
- Dogmatic falsificationism ignores the holism of theories in both science and religion.
- Confusing "knowing God" with "knowing about God" distorts theological epistemology.

# What’s the difference between Loops and Funnels?

The video explains that growth loops are self-reinforcing systems that compound, while funnels are linear paths that decay with scale, and that operationalizing growth as loops means embedding distribution into product strategy, organizing cross-functionally around compounding systems, and running a disciplined experimentation process.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Define growth loops as closed systems where an input goes through a process, generates an output, and that output is reinvested as a new input, producing compounding growth.
- Use the Pinterest content loop as the canonical example: user onboarding and usage generate more content, which feeds search algorithms, which attracts new users, and the cycle repeats.
- Contrast loops with funnels, specifically the AARRR “pirate metrics” funnel: awareness, acquisition, activation, retention, revenue, referral.
- Treat loops as engineered product systems, not merely marketing campaigns, so that product work itself generates distribution.
- Embed distribution strategy into product strategy rather than having a separate product team build and a separate growth team sell.
- Harmonize product, channels, and monetization so that choices in one area reinforce the others.
- Avoid organizing teams around funnel stages, which creates silos; instead use cross-functional teams organized around loops so interplay between steps is visible.
- Stop optimizing for local metric maxima and start seeking compounding results across the whole loop.
- Redefine interim metrics that sit between traditional funnel stages and better map to the company’s North Star.
- Build an institutional habit of experimentation and iteration, supported by a repeatable process rather than ad-hoc heroics.
- Lower the friction for anyone in the organization to submit experiment ideas; the best ideas can come from anywhere.
- Treat the only real failure as not implementing learnings from experiments; make risk-taking safe in the spirit of learning.
- Use process to ensure the team works on the right things and compounds learnings, not to add approval layers or slow execution.
- Require experiment one-pagers that include objective, hypothesis, design/resources, timeline, quantifiable success criteria, and next steps for both success and failure.
- Maintain a public triage list, such as a shared spreadsheet, so anyone can see prioritized experiments, what is running, and past results.
- Triage experiment ideas with the ICE framework—impact, confidence, level of effort—to avoid the test-everything trap and surface high-impact, low-cost bets.

## Failure modes named
- Funnel-based growth trajectories tend to decay with scale, while loop-based systems can compound.
- Funnel org structures create siloed teams that optimize at one another’s expense, e.g., acquisition sending low-quality leads that hurt retention.
- Road-mapping isolated tactics to hit a single metric produces local optima that may harm the broader system.
- A test-everything mindset wastes resources on low-impact experiments without a triage discipline.
- Process aversion among fast-moving teams can prevent the compounding of learnings.
- Lack of predefined success criteria invites revisionist history after experiments run.

# 30. Immunology 1 – Diversity, Specificity, & B cells

This lecture introduces the two-tiered immune system, focusing on how B-cell antibodies achieve extraordinary specificity and diversity through V(D)J recombination and how immunological memory makes vaccination possible.

## Procedure / models distilled
- **Innate versus adaptive immunity**: innate immunity is inborn, immediate, broadly reactive, and unchanged by prior exposure; adaptive immunity is acquired, delayed, highly specific, and improves upon re-exposure.
- **Two branches of adaptive immunity**: humoral immunity is antibody/protein-mediated by B cells, while cell-mediated immunity uses T cells; both arise from a common lymphoid progenitor derived from multipotent hematopoietic stem cells.
- **Antibody/B-cell receptor structure**: an immunoglobulin (Ig) is a Y-shaped membrane or secreted protein made of two identical heavy chains and two light chains, built from modular Ig domains, with identical antigen-binding sites at the tips.
- **T-cell receptor structure**: the TCR is a membrane-bound, alpha–beta heterodimer with fewer Ig domains than an antibody; it only recognizes peptides presented by MHC molecules.
- **Different antigen repertoires**: antibodies can bind proteins, nucleic acids, carbohydrates, and small molecules; TCRs are restricted to peptide–MHC complexes.
- **V(D)J recombination as the diversity generator**: lymphocyte-specific RAG1/RAG2 recombinases join V, (D), and J gene segments on a single chromosome, deleting intervening DNA to create a unique antigen-binding variable region.
- **Junctional imprecision expands diversity**: joins between gene segments can insert or delete nucleotides, but only changes in multiples of three preserve the reading frame and produce functional antibodies.
- **Somatic hypermutation / affinity maturation**: after activation, B cells mutate their Ig variable regions at an elevated rate and select higher-affinity variants; this process depends on T-cell help.
- **Allelic exclusion enforces monoclonality**: a functional rearrangement on one chromosome suppresses recombination of the other allele, so each B cell expresses exactly one antibody.
- **Recombination switches on expression**: V(D)J rearrangement moves an upstream promoter close to a downstream enhancer, turning the Ig gene on only after recombination.
- **CDR3 maps to the V-D-J junction**: the most hypervariable complementarity-determining region (CDR3) lies at the recombination junctions, making it the main antigen-contact surface.
- **Immunological memory**: primary antibody responses take 5–10 days and have affinities around 10⁻⁷ M; secondary responses take 1–3 days, are larger, and produce antibodies with affinities below 10⁻¹¹ M.
- **Memory B cells persist**: because V(D)J recombination is irreversible, long-lived memory B cells retain the encoded antibody even after antigen is gone; effective vaccines must create these cells.
- **Antibody effector functions**: antibodies can neutralize pathogens, opsonize them for phagocytosis, and recruit killer cells such as natural killer cells to destroy antibody-coated targets.
- **Therapeutic antibody example**: Herceptin is a humanized monoclonal antibody retaining mouse complementarity-determining regions that targets HER2 on breast cancer cells, illustrating how antibodies can be engineered into drugs.

## Failure modes named
- **Autoimmune disease** occurs when the adaptive immune system loses specificity and recognizes self-molecules instead of foreign antigens.
- **Ineffective vaccines** fail to generate durable memory B cells, so the body cannot mount a faster, stronger secondary response.
- **Frameshift mutations** arise if insertions or deletions at V(D)J junctions are not multiples of three, producing non-functional antibodies.
- **Confusing recombination types**: V(D)J recombination is intra-chromosomal deletion of segments on one chromosome, not meiotic recombination between homologous chromosomes.

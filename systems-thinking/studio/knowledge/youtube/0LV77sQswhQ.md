# The Toyota Production System as Taught by Toyota - Episode 03

Sakichi and Kiichiro Toyoda's successive loom innovations, culminating in the 1924 Model G non-stop loom, established the principle of jidoka—machines that automatically detect abnormalities and stop to prevent defects and waste.

## Procedure / models distilled
- **Jidoka / autonomation**: build machines that sense their own abnormalities and stop automatically, so quality is built in rather than inspected later.
- **Embedded mechanical sensing**: place simple physical sensors (e.g., thin metal floaters over warp yarns) directly in the process path to detect a specific fault condition instantly.
- **Stop-at-source**: halt production the moment a defect occurs, preventing continuation that would multiply downstream material waste.
- **From vigilance to leverage**: design automation so one operator can oversee many machines rather than being tethered to a single machine.
- **Problem-driven invention**: start by observing real work difficulty (Sakichi watching his mother at a manual loom) and invent to reduce burden and waste.
- **Incremental capability building**: advance through successive generations—hand loom, power loom, high-speed non-stop loom—rather than jumping to a final solution.
- **Waste-as-design-requirement**: treat known waste (fabric ruined by snapped warp yarn) as a constraint the mechanism must explicitly prevent.
- **Appropriate technology**: achieve reliable automatic detection without waiting for advanced electrical or optical systems; use what is robust and available.

## Failure modes named
- Warp yarn snapping unnoticed, ruining fabric and wasting material.
- Operators continuously tied to individual machines because the equipment cannot detect or stop for its own faults.
- Defects propagating through production because the process lacks an automatic stopping mechanism.

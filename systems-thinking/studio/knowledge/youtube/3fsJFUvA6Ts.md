# What Makes The Perfect Business (5 Things)

Hormozi explains the five “S-tier” business advantages—sticky (revenue retention), expensive (high gross margins), expanding (growing market), air (operational scale/low capex), and unique (competitive moat)—and argues founders should prioritize retention first because compounding recurring revenue makes every other growth lever more valuable.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Prioritize *revenue retention* (net revenue retained from a prior customer cohort) over simple logo retention, because a business that keeps revenue is a resale business, not a sales business.
- Distinguish structural churn (unavoidable customer loss) from voluntary churn (customers leave because they think you suck); focus relentlessly on eliminating voluntary churn.
- Qualify customers before selling so the people who enter actually need and can use the ongoing outcome you deliver.
- Build an ascension path that lets cheaper customers spend significantly more over time, enabling >100% net revenue retention even when some logos churn.
- Map the retention curve and concentrate on the first 30 days, the third month, and especially month six, because churn tends to drop to ~2%/month once customers reach six months.
- Choose products or services with high gross margins, since margin funds reinvestment, better pay, faster cash conversion, and ultimately higher net profit.
- De-commoditize your offer through specialization, brand, or unique delivery to escape thin-margin commodity markets.
- Enter growing markets (rising tide) rather than flat or declining industries, because growth makes marketing, sales, and expansion easier and cheaper.
- Engineer operational scale by minimizing the variables required to expand production and keeping capital expenditure low so growth does not depend on endless outside funding.
- Use capex as a deliberate moat only when it produces high return on invested capital (ROIC), network effects, or durable barriers that competitors cannot easily match.
- Create defensible uniqueness through trade secrets, patents, proprietary recipes/processes, specialized skills, or technology that few others can replicate.
- Convert commodity products into moats by building a brand that commands a price premium and increases stickiness.
- Evaluate opportunities as “S-tier vehicles” by combining the five advantages, but if the business lacks all five, fix retention first and then backfill the rest.

## Failure modes named
- Treating customer acquisition as the primary growth lever while ignoring churn, trapping the business in perpetual “sales mode” instead of compounding.
- Voluntary churn caused by selling to poorly fit customers or failing to deliver the ongoing outcome they paid for.
- Competing in low-gross-margin commodity markets such as groceries, farming, or restaurants, where price pressure destroys profitability.
- Operating in declining industries like print media, CDs/DVDs, coal, taxis, or traditional retail, where demand headwinds make growth increasingly expensive.
- The “we’ll make it up in volume” fallacy in capital-heavy businesses that lack true economies of scale or network effects.
- Raising outside capital to scale structurally weak unit economics rather than improving the core offer first.
- Low-barrier-to-entry services that attract unlimited competition and drive prices toward marginal cost.
- Competing without any moat, allowing rivals to copy the offer and commoditize it.

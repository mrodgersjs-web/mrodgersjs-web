# Charlie Munger: How Incentives Shape Behaviour? | University of Michigan 2010【C:C.M Ep.193】

Charlie Munger explains why perverse short-term incentives corrupt behavior across business and politics, and why the antidote is designing systems that minimize temptation rather than layering on regulation.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Define a perverse system as one in which otherwise good people go bad because of how the system is structured.
- Combine short-term incentives with weak moral reticence and expect predictably bad behavior.
- Recognize that human systems usually cannot be fixed until their underlying incentives are fixed.
- Treat organizational sloppiness as an incentive design choice; making theft easy actively creates theft.
- Lower environmental temptations rather than relying on personal willpower or moral superiority.
- Structure ownership, tenure, and capital allocation to make quarterly earnings pressure irrelevant.
- Pay directors little or nothing to sever the reciprocity loop that otherwise drives CEO pay upward.
- Understand that higher director compensation tends to inflate CEO compensation through ordinary social-psychology reciprocity.
- Distinguish official compliance remedies like Sarbanes-Oxley from genuine incentive redesign.
- Accept that wholesale system overhaul is unlikely; instead reform the system piece by piece from within.
- Build personal and institutional defenses against incentives you know would corrupt your own judgment.

## Failure modes named
- Perverse systems that turn good actors into bad ones through structural pressure.
- Short-termism in corporate management and politics driven by quarterly or electoral cycles.
- The backfire effect of paying directors more, which increases CEO pay via reciprocity.
- Writing lengthy compliance rules without changing the underlying incentives that generate misconduct.
- Relying on self-control and moral reticence instead of lowering situational temptation.
- Attempting total system redesign when only incremental, piecemeal reform is politically feasible.

# How to Research Any Topic - Deep-Dive like a PhD Student

A postdoctoral researcher walks through a systematic workflow for deep-diving any topic, from narrowing a question and mapping the literature network, to critically evaluating sources and producing an output.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Start from a general topic and progressively narrow it down to a specific, answerable research question; refine the question as the literature search progresses.
- Begin the literature hunt with either classic papers in the field or a recent review from roughly the last 5–10 years.
- Evaluate journal trustworthiness through peer review, reputation of publishers, and authors whose work you respect, recognizing that this judgment builds with experience.
- Mine the reference list of a good review as a goldmine and import every promising paper into a reference manager such as Zotero or Paperpile.
- Map the knowledge network by identifying the most-cited papers and central authors; use tools like Research Rabbit to visualize citation graphs.
- Backtrace citations from your core papers to understand the foundational work, and forward-trace citations to see who is building on those papers.
- Keep the literature search wide enough to avoid missing main papers from the last few years, while keeping the research question specific enough to prevent the network from exploding.
- Clip quotes, highlights, and key pages into a second-brain system like Notion or Zotero, tagging themes and flagging gaps as you go.
- Read with a critical-thinking hat: check funding sources, tone, argument validity, and whether the data actually support the headline or claim.
- Distinguish correlation from causation and watch for common fallacies, mishandled outliers, and reliance on anecdotes rather than evidence.
- Treat a thoughtful discussion section that names limitations as a positive sign, since good research acknowledges the edges of current knowledge.
- Build a personal research or learning plan with dedicated time and place, starting from the central nodes and moving outward to peripheral papers.
- Maintain a self-study template with a project page, notes database, literature review table, open questions, daily logs, and paper summaries to document everything like a lab book.
- Convert the research into an output such as an essay, video script, blog post, or paper to shift from passive consumption to active learning and consolidation.
- End each session by capturing what you learned, what remains unclear, and the next step to preserve momentum and let curiosity drive the work.

## Failure modes named
- Mistaking passive reading, highlighting, and scrolling for real learning instead of doing active retrieval or production.
- Letting the question stay too broad, causing the literature network to become unmanageable.
- Blindly trusting resources just because they are published; failing to check biases, funding, and argument validity.
- Falling for clickbait-style headlines that overstate findings beyond what the data actually support.
- Confusing correlation with causation, especially in fields where direct manipulation is unethical or impossible.
- Ignoring a paper’s limitations and overclaiming based on limited or biased evidence.
- Failing to document questions and insights early, losing the valuable perspective of a novice.
- Letting the long, uncertain nature of research lead to tedium and self-doubt without a momentum-building routine.

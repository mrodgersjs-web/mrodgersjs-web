# Things about a PhD nobody told you about | Laura Valadez-Martinez | TEDxLoughboroughU

Laura Valadez-Martinez distills seven unspoken challenges of doctoral research and offers practical habits—from protected thinking time to research diaries and deliberate connection—to sustain progress and sanity through a long, open-ended knowledge project.

## Procedure / models distilled
- **Schedule thinking time:** Read, then stop and think; making sense of material is work, not idleness.
- **Practice deliberate exclusion:** Leave most readings and calculations out of the thesis; discarding is progress, not failure.
- **Treat it as a marathon with sprints:** Show up consistently; inspiration follows work.
- **Capture tiny progress:** Switch to a small, completable task (a paragraph, a graph, a tidy table) when stuck, and notice the accomplishment.
- **Get wins outside the thesis:** Bake a cake, take a class, grow a plant—small external completions restore motivation.
- **Keep a research diary:** Write down every decision and the reason for it, because long hours and stress make you forget why you did what you did.
- **Write and preserve syntax/code:** Document quantitative steps so you can spot errors and replicate the analysis later.
- **Never touch the original data:** Version datasets with descriptive names so you can roll back and trace lineage.
- **Read the originals:** Go to primary sources to build confidence, find new angles, and anchor your argument.
- **Stay current and connected:** Read journals, attend conferences, and find peers working on similar topics to confirm you are on track.
- **Cultivate connection:** Talk to colleagues, supervisors, family, and friends; solitude is part of the work, but loneliness is not mandatory.
- **Develop a two-sentence thesis summary:** Explaining your 100,000-word project briefly proves you know what you are doing.
- **Design your own rhythm:** There is no single correct schedule—find the pattern that fits your life and topic.

## Failure modes named
- Getting stuck in multiple ways: ideas, methodology, literature, analysis, or writing.
- Opening endless “doors” of new research and drifting from the original question.
- Loss of motivation because the endpoint feels distant and the work never ends.
- Forgetting earlier decisions due to fatigue, stress, deadlines, and oscillation between detail and generality.
- Doubting relevance, importance, or whether anyone will read the work.
- Loneliness and isolation exacerbating an already difficult process.
- Waiting for inspiration instead of working.
- Failing to document decisions, syntax, or data versions.
- Overwriting original data and losing the ability to reconstruct the analysis.
- Treating the PhD as a purely solitary journey with no outside anchors.

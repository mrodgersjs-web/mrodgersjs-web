# Intro to Game Theory and the Dominant Strategy Equilibrium

An introduction to game theory, focusing on how to identify and solve games using the dominant strategy equilibrium.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Define a game by identifying at least two players (agents), the payoffs each player receives from each outcome, and the rules governing play.
- Recognize that game theorists use multiple solution concepts, including dominant strategy equilibrium, Nash equilibrium, subgame perfect Nash equilibrium, Bayesian equilibrium, and weak perfect Bayesian equilibrium.
- Determine whether one strategy strictly dominates another by checking if it always yields a better payoff for a player no matter what the other players do.
- Identify a strictly dominant strategy as one that strictly dominates every other strategy available to that player.
- Find a dominant strategy equilibrium by identifying when every player is playing a strictly dominant strategy.
- Map the Prisoner's Dilemma payoff structure: mutual quiet yields moderate payoffs, mutual testimony yields worse payoffs, and unilateral testimony rewards the defector and severely punishes the cooperator.
- Apply dominant-strategy reasoning to the Prisoner's Dilemma to conclude that both prisoners will testify because testimony dominates keeping quiet.
- Generalize the Prisoner's Dilemma logic to other strategic settings, such as oligopoly pricing, where each firm has an incentive to undercut rivals.
- Solve more complex normal-form games by checking each player's strategies against all possible opponent moves to locate the dominant strategy equilibrium.

## Failure modes named
- The trap of trusting non-binding promises: players may promise cooperation, but if defection is a strictly dominant strategy, self-interest will lead them to break the promise.
- The cartel/oligopoly breakdown: firms collectively benefit from high prices, but each firm has an individual incentive to undercut, driving the market to a low-price equilibrium.
- The illusion that "keeping quiet" is collectively best guarantees individual cooperation; individual incentives can make the collectively superior outcome unstable.

# Value Props: Create a Product People Will Actually Buy

Michael Skok walks through a three-part framework for defining, evaluating, and crisping a value proposition that solves a valuable problem for a well-defined user segment and delivers an order-of-magnitude gain-over-pain benefit.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Treat value prop work as a three-stage sequence: define the problem/opportunity, evaluate it from the user's point of view, then build a crisp statement.
- Replace "I have an idea" with "I am solving this problem for this person"; ideas only gain meaning when anchored to a problem or opportunity.
- Start the value prop with "For who" and make the target narrow and specific; avoid "everybody" as a customer definition.
- In multi-sided situations, distinguish the user (the person who benefits) from the customer/payer (the person who pays or approves); create pull from the user first, then satisfy the payer.
- Find your minimum viable segment (MVS): a group of users with the same needs so you can sell the same product repeatedly without constant customization; think of MVS as a dance partner for your minimum viable product (MVP).
- Validate the "who" through customer eyes by talking to users before building; ask, don't pitch, until someone says the pain is big enough to engage.
- State the problem clearly before designing the product; "a problem well stated is half solved" and gives the team a shared focus.
- Screen problems against the "4 Us": Unworkable, Unavoidable, Urgent, and Underserved.
- Classify needs on a "must-have vs. nice-to-have" axis: aim for blatant and critical needs rather than latent and aspirational ones, or have a credible path from nice-to-have to mission-critical.
- Build the value prop statement as: "For [who], dissatisfied with [what], due to [unmet need/problem]. You offer [product] that solves that and provides [key benefits]."
- Explain why the solution is disruptive or discontinuous, e.g., analog to digital, offline to online, making something newly accessible.
- Quantify benefits with an order-of-magnitude "gain/pain" ratio; be explicit about what users gain versus the pain of finding, trying, buying, adopting, training on, and trusting your product.
- Account for switching inertia, perceived risk, and startup credibility in the pain side of the ratio; customers default to doing nothing unless the gain is compelling.
- Tailor the gain/pain threshold to the segment; enterprise workflows often need ~10x improvement to justify change, while low-friction consumer apps may need much less.
- Anchor the value prop in founder-market fit: your unique understanding of the problem and a sustainable business model are what make the proposition defensible.

## Failure modes named
- Building a product that is not solving a valuable enough problem, which is the top reason companies fail.
- Targeting "everybody" and boiling the ocean, which makes go-to-market focus impossible.
- Defining the customer as the payer while ignoring the user, producing no real pull for the product.
- Starting to build before the team agrees on who the customer is and what hair-on-fire pain to solve first.
- Treating latent or aspirational needs as sufficient when they remain nice-to-haves instead of becoming must-haves.
- Underestimating adoption pain, inertia, and trust risk, making the gain/pain ratio too weak to compel a switch.
- Believing the value prop is great without evidence that users are pulling it to market.

# Dynamical Systems: Attractive and Chaotic | Prof Peter Giesl

An accessible tour of dynamical systems, showing how phase space, time, and evolution rules combine to produce long-term behaviors ranging from stable equilibria and periodic orbits to chaotic strange attractors.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Define a dynamical system by three ingredients: a phase space (where the state lives), a time variable (discrete steps or continuous flow), and an evolution rule mapping an initial state to its state at any future time.
- Model discrete-time systems by repeatedly applying a rule, such as an infection count doubling each day.
- Identify recurrent behaviors in discrete systems: fixed points that stay put, periodic orbits that return after a fixed number of steps, and basins of attraction that feed initial conditions into a recurrent set.
- Interpret continuous-time systems as vector fields, where the arrow at each point gives the tangent direction (derivative) a solution curve must follow.
- Solve differential equations by tracing a curve that follows the local arrows from a given initial condition, rather than relying on an explicit formula for the state at time t.
- Classify long-term behavior by attractors, which can be equilibrium points, periodic orbits, or more complex attracting sets.
- Use stability and attraction to partition phase space into basins where nearby initial conditions share the same long-term outcome.
- Recognize chaos through sensitive dependence on initial conditions: two arbitrarily close starting points can later diverge dramatically while remaining within the same bounded region.
- Distinguish chaos from mere disorder by noting that chaotic systems contain deterministic structure, including unstable periodic orbits and equilibrium points embedded in the attractor.
- Identify strange attractors as attracting sets with non-integer fractal dimension, where many initial conditions converge even though individual trajectories look chaotic.

## Failure modes named
- Treating a numerical approximation as the true long-term solution when sensitive dependence amplifies tiny errors into completely different trajectories.
- Confusing chaos with unbounded or non-attracting behavior; chaotic motion can remain bounded and be organized by an attractor.
- Assuming chaos and attraction are opposites; strange attractors can be simultaneously chaotic and attractive.
- Misidentifying the dimensionality of complex attractors; strange attractors can occupy a fractal dimension between two and three.

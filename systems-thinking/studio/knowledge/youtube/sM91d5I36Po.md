# Prospect Theory (explained in a minute) - Behavioural Finance

Prospect theory describes how people evaluate outcomes as gains or losses relative to a reference point, and why the pain of losses outweighs the pleasure of equivalent gains.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Use a reference point to classify outcomes as gains or losses rather than as absolute states.
- Recognize that the reference point is often relative and can be shifted by comparison or framing.
- Compare outcomes upward (toward a higher goal) to make current positions feel like losses.
- Compare outcomes downward (toward a worse alternative) to make current positions feel like gains.
- Apply the Olympic-medal heuristic: silver medalists reference gold and feel loss, bronze medalists reference no medal and feel gain.
- Treat monetary outcomes as mental accounting positions, not just net totals.
- Notice that psychological value is attached to the change from a reference point, not the final amount.
- Quantify loss aversion as a rough ratio where the pain of a loss is about two to three times the joy of an equivalent gain.
- Rebalance decision weights by deliberately reframing a net-gain situation as a single gain rather than a gain followed by a loss.

## Failure modes named
- Irrational reaction: silver medalists feel like "first losers" despite being second best in the world.
- Reference-point neglect: judging a result by the wrong comparator instead of its absolute value.
- Asymmetric valuation: valuing a pure $50 gain more favorably than a $100 gain followed by a $50 loss, even though the net outcome is identical.
- Magnified loss aversion: letting the pain of a loss be two to three times stronger than the joy of an equivalent gain.
- Missing the upside: failing to enjoy wins because attention is fixed on what was almost achieved.

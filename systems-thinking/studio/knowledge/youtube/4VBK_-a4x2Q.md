# Prospect theory

An introduction to prospect theory as a descriptive, "as if" model of decision-making under risk, developed by Kahneman and Tversky as an alternative to expected utility theory.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Prospect theory is a descriptive model of risky choice developed as a challenge to expected utility theory.
- Outcomes are evaluated by a value function rather than by final wealth or utility alone.
- The value function is reference-dependent: each outcome is judged relative to a reference point.
- Loss aversion means a loss of a given magnitude creates a larger negative value than an equivalent gain creates positive value.
- The reflection effect means agents tend to be risk-averse in the domain of gains and risk-seeking in the domain of losses.
- Objective probabilities are subjectively transformed by a probability weighting function.
- Small probabilities are overweighted relative to moderate or high probabilities.
- Weighted values are combined to produce an overall evaluation of a prospect.
- Prospect theory is an "as if" model: people behave as though they perform these calculations without actually doing so.
- Because it is descriptive, it predicts how people choose under risk, not how they ought to choose.

## Failure modes named
- Treating prospect theory as a normative prescription for rational choice rather than a descriptive account of behavior.
- Assuming people literally compute weighted values instead of behaving "as if" they do.
- Evaluating outcomes in absolute terms rather than relative to a reference point.

# What Ants Can Teach Us about Resilience | Carmichael Roberts | TEDxBoston

Resilience is not just mental toughness but deliberate design of products, systems, and infrastructure so that critical needs persist through repeated shocks.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Frame climate action as a three-legged stool: mitigation, adaptation, and resiliency, with resiliency as the least-discussed leg.
- Design resiliency into products and infrastructure from the start rather than only responding faster after disasters.
- Treat nature as a pre-programmed resilience model and study its distributed, redundant, self-healing patterns.
- Decentralize critical-resource infrastructure so a single point of failure does not collapse the whole system.
- Source drinking water from atmospheric moisture using modular, solar-like hydropanels that can be placed anywhere and replaced easily.
- Prioritize air-sourced water over centralized land-based freshwater sources that are vulnerable to contamination and storms.
- Replace long supply-chain-dependent fertilizers with locally applied microbial nitrogen fixation in soil.
- Use microbes as on-site nutrient engines that persist underground and continue feeding plants even after floods or supply disruptions.
- Build communities and infrastructure that anticipate hazards, such as routing drainage away from homes and hardening power systems.
- Combine renewable energy with resilient design so essential services stay online during extreme events.
- After a shock, rebuild differently by incorporating lessons from resilient test sites rather than copying the original vulnerable design.
- Recognize repeating disaster patterns early so design changes can be made before the next predictable shock.

## Failure modes named
- Rebuilding the same vulnerable structures after every storm and expecting a different outcome.
- Assuming human learning capacity automatically prevents repeated mistakes.
- Over-relying on complex global supply chains for essential nutrients and resources.
- Centralizing critical infrastructure, such as freshwater reservoirs, in ways that create single points of failure.
- Responding faster to disasters without changing the underlying design of what is being replaced.
- Designing for a pristine, unchanged world instead of anticipating recurring hazards.
- Ignoring natural, decentralized solutions in favor of highly engineered but fragile systems.

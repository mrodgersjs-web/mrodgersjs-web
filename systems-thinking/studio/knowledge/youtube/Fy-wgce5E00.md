# The Pythagorean Theorem Is Not What You Think

The video reframes the Pythagorean theorem as the signature of orthogonality and distance in flat space, explaining why the same structure appears across triangles, coordinate grids, trigonometry, signal processing, and Einsteinian spacetime.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Read a² + b² = c² as an area relation, not a length recipe: the areas of squares built on the two legs equal the area of the square on the hypotenuse.
- Generalize the area principle to any three similar shapes built on the sides of a right triangle; area scaling preserves the same a² + b² = c² relationship.
- Use Bhāskara’s “Behold” proof: four identical right triangles arranged inside a square leave a central gap of area c² in one configuration and two gaps of areas a² and b² in another, with total gap area unchanged.
- Apply Euclid’s similarity proof: drop a perpendicular from the right angle to the hypotenuse, producing two smaller triangles that are similar to the original; because area scales as the square of hypotenuse length, the common factor cancels to yield the theorem.
- Reconstruct Garfield’s trapezoid proof: arrange two congruent right triangles to form a trapezoid enclosing an isosceles right triangle with legs c, compute the trapezoid area two ways, and simplify to obtain a² + b² = c².
- Treat the 2D distance formula and its 3D/nD extensions as the Pythagorean theorem applied to invisible right triangles; it defines Euclidean distance in flat space of any dimension.
- Recognize the theorem as a definition of flatness, not a universal law: on curved surfaces such as a sphere, shortest-path triangles can have angle sums other than 180° and the relation fails structurally.
- See sin²θ + cos²θ = 1 as the theorem on a unit circle, where sine and cosine are perpendicular coordinates and the radius is 1.
- Interpret Parseval’s theorem in signal processing as Pythagorean: orthogonal frequency components in a mathematical space add energies as sums of squares.
- Read the special-relativity spacetime interval s² = c²t² − x² − y² − z² as a modified Pythagorean relation with a minus sign; it replaces spherical with hyperboloid geometry and yields an invariant interval shared by all observers.
- Note that the theorem predates Pythagoras: the Babylonian clay tablet Plimpton 322 records Pythagorean triples more than a millennium before Pythagoras.
- Apply the theorem to an isosceles right triangle with legs of length 1 to discover √2, an irrational number that undermined the Pythagorean belief that every length is a ratio of whole numbers.
- Reframe the equation as the algebra of independence/orthogonality: whenever directions are perpendicular, their magnitudes combine through sums of squares.

## Failure modes named
- Treating a² + b² = c² as a mere numerical recipe about side lengths rather than a statement about areas and flat-space structure.
- Applying the theorem on curved surfaces, where shortest-path triangles violate the flat-space angle-sum rule and the relation becomes structurally wrong rather than slightly inaccurate.
- Presenting the distance formula, sin²θ + cos²θ = 1, or Parseval’s theorem as isolated facts instead of instances of the same orthogonal/flat-space relation.
- Misattributing the theorem to Pythagoras while ignoring earlier Babylonian knowledge recorded on Plimpton 322.
- Assuming all geometric lengths must be rational ratios of whole numbers, a belief the theorem directly contradicts via the hypotenuse of a unit right triangle.

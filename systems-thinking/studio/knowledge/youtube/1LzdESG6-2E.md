# The power of Bayesian reasoning | BBC Ideas

Bayesian reasoning is the practice of holding beliefs as probabilities and systematically updating them as new evidence arrives, with applications from medicine and codebreaking to everyday perception.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Express any belief, hypothesis, or risk as a probability rather than a binary certainty.
- Begin with a prior probability that reflects existing knowledge, base rates, or historical evidence.
- Update the prior with new information to produce a revised posterior probability.
- Treat learning and diagnosis as iterative revision rather than one-shot proof.
- In signal detection, adjust the probability of a category as each new feature appears.
- In medical screening, combine test accuracy with the condition’s base rate to estimate the chance of disease after a positive result.
- Recognize that even accurate tests can yield many false positives when the underlying condition is rare.
- In scientific inference, make underlying judgments and prior evidence explicit instead of pretending to start from nothing.
- Use expert judgment and historical data to design more efficient clinical trials.
- Model perception as a Bayesian brain: prior expectations are continuously updated by incoming sensations.
- Accept uncertainty as the default state and reduce it incrementally through evidence.

## Failure modes named
- Base-rate neglect: judging a test only by its accuracy while ignoring how rare the condition is.
- False-positive overload: rare conditions naturally produce many false positives among all positive screens.
- The blank-slate illusion: believing science or analysis can proceed without prior assumptions or preconceptions.
- Data-speaks-for-itself fallacy: treating each dataset as if it stands entirely on its own, separate from existing knowledge.

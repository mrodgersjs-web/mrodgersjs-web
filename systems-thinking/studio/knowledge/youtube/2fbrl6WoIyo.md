# Lec 1 | MIT 9.00SC Introduction to Psychology, Spring 2011

This introductory lecture reframes psychology as the scientific study of an interpretive mind and its supporting brain, using perception, memory, reasoning, affective forecasting, and social-action experiments to show that experience and behavior are active constructions rather than passive recordings of reality.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Treat psychology as an empirical science of human nature: test ideas about feeling, thinking, and acting with experiments and evidence rather than intuition.
- Study the mind together with the brain: for every mental phenomenon, ask what neural mechanisms support or contribute to it.
- Model perception as constructive inference: the visual system does not copy the world but computes size, length, brightness, and depth from surrounding context and prior assumptions.
- Use visual illusions as diagnostic tools: when a scene violates typical environmental statistics, the same heuristics that usually succeed produce measurable errors and reveal the hidden laws of perception.
- Apply top-down processing to memory: recall of words or stories is shaped by expectations and prior knowledge, not by a literal record of input.
- Map intuitive reasoning failures: compare automatic geographic or probability judgments against formal facts to expose where cognition takes shortcuts.
- Test affective forecasting experimentally: ask people to predict future emotional reactions to events, then compare predictions with later self-reports.
- Expect hedonic adaptation: major positive and negative life changes tend to shift self-reported happiness back toward baseline within months or a couple of years.
- Run staged social experiments with confederates: place participants in realistic but controlled interactions to compare what people say they would feel or do with what they actually feel or do.
- Measure the values-action gap: compare forecasted moral responses with real-time emotional reports and behavioral choices in tense or unexpected social situations.
- Treat inconsistent or failed demonstrations as learning opportunities: when an illusion or experiment does not work as expected, analyze what that reveals about the underlying mechanism or context.

## Failure modes named
- The "video camera" fallacy: assuming the mind records reality objectively and passively.
- Confusing appearance with measurement: treating length, brightness, size, or color as raw sensory facts rather than context-dependent interpretations.
- Affective forecasting error: overestimating how intense and how long future positive or negative events will make us feel.
- Ignoring hedonic adaptation: making major decisions as though happiness or misery from an event will persist unchanged.
- Values-action disconnect: believing that espoused beliefs automatically produce corresponding behavior in ambiguous or stressful social moments.
- Social paralysis under ambiguity: shrinking from acting on one's values when a situation feels weird, provocative, or incompletely understood.

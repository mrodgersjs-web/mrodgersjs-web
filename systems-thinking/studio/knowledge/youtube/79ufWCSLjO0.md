# Transfer of Learning in Motor Control and Learning

Transfer of learning is the influence of prior experience on performing or learning a skill in a new context, producing positive, negative, or zero effects that determine how practice should be sequenced and contextualized.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Define transfer of learning as the influence of previous experiences on performing a skill in a new context or on learning a new skill, with outcomes of positive, negative, or zero transfer.
- Recognize positive transfer as prior experience that facilitates learning or performance in a new environment.
- Apply identical elements theory: positive transfer increases with similarity of component parts, kinematics, required corrections, muscle groups, and coordination patterns.
- Apply transfer-appropriate processing theory: positive transfer increases when the cognitive processing required by two skills is similar, such as fast decision making, problem solving, and attention control.
- Recognize negative transfer as prior experience that interferes with learning, usually temporary and limited to early learning when a similar stimulus now requires a different response.
- Watch for negative transfer especially when spatial locations of movements or the timing structure of movements change.
- Explain negative transfer through perception-action coupling: a prior skill creates a specific memory representation that must be uncoupled and recoupled to produce a new response.
- Explain negative transfer through cognitive confusion: knowing the correct action but being confused about where or when to do it, as with driving on the opposite side of the road or a different keyboard layout.
- Explain negative transfer through intrinsic dynamics: previously learned task-specific coordination tendencies may compete with the new skill and even retroactively interfere with the original skill.
- Use bilateral transfer, also called inter-manual transfer, cross-transfer, or cross-education, to learn a skill with one limb and gain positive transfer to the opposite limb.
- Assume asymmetric bilateral transfer: transfer is generally greater from the preferred/dominant limb to the non-dominant limb, though this remains debated.
- Explain bilateral transfer via cognitive transfer of task knowledge, generalized motor program formation, dynamical-systems abstract coordination representations, and inter-hemispheric brain activation during unilateral practice.

## Failure modes named
- Assuming prior skills will automatically help, while ignoring possible negative or zero transfer.
- Sequencing skills in an order that causes earlier skills to interfere with later ones.
- Treating negative transfer as a permanent deficit rather than a temporary early-learning effect that practice can overcome.
- Designing practice contexts that do not share the kinematic, correction, spatial, timing, or cognitive demands of the target context.
- Training the non-preferred limb first when evidence favors dominant-to-non-dominant asymmetric transfer.
- Treating bilateral transfer as symmetric and missing opportunities to use cross-limb practice.
- Failing to address perception-action decoupling, cognitive confusion, or competing intrinsic dynamics when changing techniques or environments.

## Studio relevance
04 leverage - uses transfer-of-learning principles to sequence skill practice, align practice constraints with target contexts, and exploit bilateral training for faster acquisition.

# How Your Brain Organizes Information

The brain builds flexible, factorized cognitive maps—relational graphs over physical and latent variables, implemented by hippocampal and entorhinal circuits—to generalize knowledge across contexts.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Generalize by stripping away particular sensory context and extracting an abstract procedure or relational structure.
- Treat a cognitive map as a multi-purpose, coherent internal framework that organizes information about the world.
- Use internal spatial maps, not just stimulus-response associations, to explain flexible detour behavior (Tolman’s maze experiments).
- Locate the core cognitive-mapping machinery in the hippocampal formation: the hippocampus plus the entorhinal cortex as its information gateway.
- Decode location with place cells that fire in a context-dependent way for specific positions.
- Read out a stable, periodic coordinate system from grid cells in the entorhinal cortex, which tile space with a hexagonal firing pattern.
- Supplement the coordinate code with specialized cells: object-vector cells, landmark cells, boundary cells, and head-direction cells.
- Assign the entorhinal cortex the job of providing a general coordinate/vector-computation scaffold, while the hippocampus embeds specific locations and landmarks within that scaffold.
- Recognize that the same mapping machinery operates in non-spatial domains, such as sound-frequency spaces and abstract bird-silhouette morph spaces.
- Model any cognitive map as a graph: nodes stand for entities or states, edges stand for relations or transitions, whether spatial, social, taxonomic, or conceptual.
- Perform path integration to keep track of position on a graph: in physical space this accumulates self-motion vectors; on abstract graphs it composes relational rules.
- Expand cognitive maps with latent dimensions—task-relevant variables not directly observable from single sensations, such as left/right trial history or accumulated evidence.
- Factorize experience into a structural component (position on the relational graph) and a sensory component (the particular context), so regularities can be reused and combined.

## Failure modes named
- Explaining flexible behavior purely through stimulus-response associations instead of internal relational maps.
- Assuming a cognitive map is only a literal map of physical space, ignoring abstract and latent dimensions.
- Believing every task-relevant variable is directly observable from immediate sensory cues, missing latent-space inference.
- Storing experiences as unfactorized wholes rather than separating structural relations from sensory context, blocking generalization.

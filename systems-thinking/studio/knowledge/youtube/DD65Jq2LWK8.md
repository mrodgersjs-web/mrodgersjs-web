# How Do We Know What We Know? Philosophy of Science

Philosopher of science Sam Baron explains how philosophy clarifies the assumptions, methods, and inferential gaps behind science, especially as research becomes too abstract for direct observation.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Treat philosophy of science as a meta-discipline that asks questions science cannot answer from within its own paradigm, such as “what is explanation?”
- Map the concept of explanation as a distinct philosophical object so its structure can feed back into scientific method.
- Refine the scientific method by deliberately overriding intuition and unexamined heuristics.
- Use variable-control and statistical techniques to extract causal structure from a sea of correlations.
- Audit observer contributions by separating the idiosyncratic human perspective and perceptual lens from features of the world itself.
- Name and inventory cognitive biases, such as confirmation bias, as explicit risks to examine during inquiry.
- Evaluate inference at each layer in instrument-mediated science, since raw data is several inferential steps removed from the target phenomenon.
- Inspect abductive and inductive arguments for their logical gap: evidence supports but does not guarantee the conclusion.
- Treat causation as a candidate primitive: model reality as a causal ordering from which spacetime structure may be derived.
- Build spacetime from causal structure, as in causal set theory, where discrete causal relations are posited as fundamental and continuous space-time emerges.
- Apply Copernican de-centering as an epistemic move by removing the assumption that the observer occupies a special place in the system.
- Separate science communication from science itself, recognizing that public distrust can arise from poor explanation, cultural commitments, or real philosophical gaps.

## Failure modes named
- Confusing correlation with causation when interpreting statistical patterns.
- Letting unrefined heuristics or intuitions guide scientific reasoning.
- Allowing confirmation bias and other cognitive biases to shape hypothesis testing.
- Conflating observer-dependent perspective with objective world structure.
- Poor science communication and marketing creating openings for counter-narratives.
- Culturally enforced beliefs washing out contrary evidence.
- Exploiting logical gaps in induction and abduction to insert unreasonable skepticism.
- Treating multi-layered inferences from raw instrument data as direct observation.
- Assuming space-time is fundamental rather than possibly emergent from causal relations.

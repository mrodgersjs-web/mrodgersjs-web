# A History of Philosophy | 64 American Pragmatism

This lecture introduces American pragmatism (Peirce, James, Dewey) as a process-oriented, anti-Cartesian movement that prioritizes practical consequences, concrete experience, and experimental belief-fixation over abstract theory and private certainty.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Read pragmatism as a process philosophy: reality is an ongoing, organic whole with internal relations and no hard fact/value split.
- Put practical consequences and concrete experience ahead of abstract theory when judging beliefs.
- Settle philosophical disputes by anticipating the experiential consequences of a belief and checking whether they occur.
- Begin inquiry from an unavoidable background of already-held beliefs and assumptions; universal doubt is impossible because some beliefs are tacit.
- Treat clarity and distinctness as markers of meaningfulness, not automatic marks of truth.
- Build knowledge the way experimental science does: through many converging lines of argument and evidence rather than a single deductive chain.
- Reject "inexplicable ultimates" as explanations; anything posited as ultimate must still be open to further inquiry.
- Recognize that all thought is mediated by signs; there is no private, introspective access to indubitable truths.
- Replace the atomistic "sense datum" model of experience with a holistic stream or continuum of concrete, psychological experience.
- Diagnose worldviews by the felt quality they produce—inner tension versus satisfaction—rather than by abstract propositions alone.
- Test a philosophical or religious claim by its "cash value" or "workability" in lived experience.
- Treat truth as what works or satisfies in experience, while guarding against reducing truth to mere psychological comfort.

## Failure modes named
- Cartesian foundationalism: universal doubt, private clarity as truth, single deductive chain, and unexplained ultimates.
- Appeal to rational proof or tradition/intuition fails because intuitions vary across traditions.
- British empiricism's abstraction of concrete experience into atomistic, passive simple ideas.
- Mechanistic model's reliance on external relations, fact/value split, and pretense of pure objectivity.
- Whitehead's qualitative monism overgeneralizes God, creation, and persons into the same process, producing a thin personal identity and weak ethical accountability.
- Defining truth as workability risks collapsing truth into felt satisfaction.

# Lecture on Measurements and Conceptualization   Validity & Reliability

A lecture covering how to define, operationalize, measure, and evaluate research variables through levels of measurement, reliability types, and validity types.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Define **measurement** as sorting and, when possible, quantifying information consistently and systematically for individual cases.
- **Conceptualize** variables by selecting the most important constructs and specifying their precise dictionary-style definitions (e.g., discrimination, addiction, depression).
- **Operationalize** variables by choosing the specific instruments or methods used to measure each construct in the current study.
- Identify **levels of measurement**: nominal (mutually exclusive categories), ordinal (rank-ordered categories), interval (equal differences), and ratio (absolute zero).
- Check **reliability** by demonstrating that a measure produces consistent results across situations and conditions.
- Apply **test-retest reliability** by administering the same instrument to the same participants twice and comparing paired scores.
- Use **parallel-forms reliability** by comparing alternate but equivalent versions of an instrument to ensure comparable results.
- Estimate **split-half reliability** by dividing an instrument into two equal halves and correlating the total scores of each half.
- Calculate **Cronbach’s alpha / coefficient alpha** as the average inter-item correlation to assess internal consistency.
- Verify **validity** by confirming that a method actually measures what it claims to measure; reliability is necessary but not sufficient for validity.
- Evaluate **content validity** by ensuring the instrument covers all aspects of the target construct, not just a subset.
- Assess **criterion validity** by how well a measure predicts or correlates with another relevant measure or outcome.
- Distinguish **concurrent validity** (high correlation between two measures taken at the same time) and **predictive validity** (ability to predict future performance or behavior).
- Examine **construct validity** by comparing results with theoretical expectations, including **convergent validity** (correlations with related constructs) and **discriminant validity** (lack of correlation with unrelated constructs).
- Reduce measurement error and cultural bias by understanding one’s own values, consulting members of the target group, using back-translation with bilingual translators, and field testing translated instruments.

## Failure modes named
- Measuring only a subset of a construct, producing low **content validity** (e.g., measuring only binging while ignoring purging and self-evaluation in bulimia nervosa).
- Assuming **reliability guarantees validity**; a measure can be consistent yet still measure the wrong thing.
- Confusing related but distinct constructs, undermining **construct validity** (e.g., mistaking aggression for assertiveness or social dominance).
- Treating rank-ordered categories as if they have equal intervals or an absolute zero, which misuses **levels of measurement**.
- Ignoring **cultural bias** in instruments, leading to systematic measurement error for non-source populations.
- Using an instrument without checking what groups it was originally developed and validated on.

# 5 – High Reliability Culture

A high reliability culture is a daily, human-centered default in which every team member feels safe to speak up, problems are anticipated and contained before they cause harm, and inclusive, anti-racist values drive consistent performance toward zero harm.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Define high reliability as performing as intended consistently over time, while anticipating problems and catching them before they snowball into harm.
- Anchor the culture in three universal human principles — "don't harm me, help me, be nice to me" — and apply them in individualized, culturally adapted ways.
- Build psychological safety so anyone can speak up, ask questions, ask for help, or offer ideas without fear of retaliation, judgment, or job loss.
- Foster a just, inclusive, anti-racist culture by inviting all voices to the table and changing structures, policies, practices, and attitudes that produce disparities.
- Treat culture as a default state that is supported, protected, and embodied even when no one is watching, not as a performance turned on for convenience.
- See every employee and patient as human first, respecting the lived experiences each person brings.
- Practice open transparency, teamwork, and collaboration so safety responsibility is widespread rather than siloed.
- Learn from mistakes and treat improvement as continuous, recognizing that "better" is not a final destination.
- Recognize and reward near-miss catches and speak-up behavior to reinforce that such actions save lives.
- Apply cross-industry lessons from commercial aviation and nuclear power, where cultural changes around communication, hierarchy, and error containment produced high reliability.
- Empower every role — from environmental services to physicians — to act on a questioning attitude and escalate anomalies immediately.
- Translate high reliability into a concrete operational rule: get it right for every patient, every interaction, every time.

## Failure modes named
- Culture treated as a performance or switch that can be turned on and off when convenient.
- Fear preventing full participation: fear of speaking up, asking questions, losing a job, being judged, asking for help, getting in the way, or offering ideas.
- Reliance on processes, medicines, and technologies alone without an enabling culture.
- Hierarchical silence where junior or lower-status team members fail to challenge unsafe or errant decisions.
- Lack of teamwork and communication leading to preventable errors.
- Treating universal care principles as one-size-fits-all instead of tailoring care to individualized needs and lived experiences.

# GENERAL CHEMISTRY explained in 19 Minutes

A 19-minute crash course on atoms, the periodic table, chemical bonding, intermolecular forces, states of matter, reactions, stoichiometry, thermodynamics, acids/bases, redox, and quantum electron configurations.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Atomic model: an atom is a nucleus of protons (determines the element), neutrons (determine isotopes), and surrounding electrons; an imbalance of electrons and protons creates ions (cations = positive, anions = negative).
- Use the periodic table as a map: groups share valence-electron count, periods share shell count; metals sit left, nonmetals right, and semimetals lie along the staircase; each cell gives symbol, atomic number (= protons = electrons), and atomic mass (= protons + neutrons).
- Predict chemical similarity from valence electrons: elements in the same group behave similarly because they share the same outer-shell count, and noble gases are inert because their outer shells are already full.
- Apply the “full outer shell” drive: atoms bond to lower potential energy by completing an outer shell (octet, or duet for H/He).
- Classify bonds by electronegativity difference: nonpolar covalent (<0.5), polar covalent (0.5–1.7), ionic (>1.7); use Lewis dot structures to visualize valence electrons and shared pairs.
- Metallic-bond model: a lattice of positive nuclei immersed in a “sea” of delocalized valence electrons, explaining conductivity, heat transfer, and malleability.
- Intermolecular-forces model: permanent dipoles attract each other (hydrogen bonds when H bonds to F, O, or N); transient dipoles create Van der Waals forces; water’s polarity makes it a versatile polar solvent but unable to dissolve nonpolar oils/fats.
- Rank bond and force strengths: ionic > covalent > metallic > hydrogen bonds > Van der Waals forces.
- States-of-matter model: solids have ordered, low-entropy packed particles; liquids flow but keep fixed volume; gases fill volume with high entropy; plasma is ionized gas whose electron energy-level transitions produce an emission spectrum.
- Mixture taxonomy: pure substances vs. mixtures; homogeneous solutions, heterogeneous suspensions, and colloids/emulsions in between (milk is an emulsion, not a solution).
- Balance chemical equations by conserving mass atom-by-atom; convert gram amounts to moles using atomic/molar mass to get exact stoichiometric ratios.
- Distinguish physical change (appearance only) from chemical change (new substances form), signaled by bubbles, smell, or explosions; reactions require activation energy, and catalysts lower that barrier without being consumed.
- Spontaneity model: ΔG = ΔH − TΔS; ΔG < 0 means spontaneous/exergonic, ΔG = 0 means equilibrium, and endothermic reactions can still be spontaneous if entropy increase and temperature are large enough.
- Brønsted-Lowry acid-base model: acids donate protons, bases accept protons; pH = −log[H₃O⁺]; strong acids/bases dissociate almost completely; neutralization yields water + a salt; pH + pOH = 14.
- Redox tracking: assign oxidation numbers (H usually +1, O usually −2, halogens usually −1, elements in pure form 0, total equals the species charge); oxidation loses electrons, reduction gains electrons; the oxidizing agent is reduced, the reducing agent is oxidized.
- Electron-configuration model: four quantum numbers describe shells, subshells (s, p, d, f), orbitals, and spin; each orbital holds up to two electrons with opposite spin; fill subshells via the Aufbau diagonal rule and abbreviate with the preceding noble-gas core; transition-metal valence electrons are those beyond that core.

## Failure modes named
- Treating atoms as miniature solar systems with electrons in fixed circular orbits, rather than probability clouds described by quantum orbitals.
- Assuming a compound inherits the dangerous or familiar properties of its constituent elements.
- Weighing reactants in equal gram amounts instead of converting to moles via atomic/molar mass for stoichiometry.
- Calling any uniform-looking liquid a solution; colloids and emulsions can look homogeneous while still containing undissolved particles.
- Calling the species that loses electrons the “oxidant” and the species that gains electrons the “reductant”; the oxidizing agent is reduced and the reducing agent is oxidized.
- Concluding that endothermic reactions can never be spontaneous while ignoring entropy and temperature effects on Gibbs free energy.

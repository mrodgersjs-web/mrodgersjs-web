# Learn ALL THE MATH IN THE WORLD from START to FINISH

The presenter maps all of mathematics into eight overlapping categories and surveys representative textbooks for each, emphasizing proof writing as the gateway skill and the prerequisite chains that connect foundational algebra to advanced frontiers.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Map the mathematics landscape into eight categories: foundations, algebra & structures, geometry & topology, discrete math & combinatorics, analysis & calculus, probability & statistics, applied math & modeling, and advanced topics & frontiers.
- Treat proof writing as the central gateway skill that unlocks every advanced category.
- Sequence foundations as logic → sets → arithmetic/number theory → pre-algebra → intermediate algebra → college algebra → precalculus/trigonometry.
- Differentiate linear algebra into computational and proof-based approaches depending on your goal.
- Build abstract algebra on groups, rings, fields, and modules only after learning proof writing.
- Sequence analysis from calculus → differential equations → real analysis → complex analysis → functional analysis and Hilbert spaces.
- Study discrete math and combinatorics as the primary bridge between mathematics and computer science.
- Treat probability and statistics as a difficulty spectrum: general-audgebra texts, calculus-based mathematical statistics, and engineering statistics.
- Use applied math and modeling to connect physics, engineering, cryptography, and chaos theory under one umbrella.
- Recognize that the categories overlap and that a subject can reasonably sit in more than one stack.
- Check prerequisites before choosing a text, such as calculus for physics or proof writing for set theory and abstract algebra.
- Judge each book by audience fit: computational versus rigorous, undergraduate versus graduate, and self-study suitability.
- Use a graduate-prep survey text to identify gaps between undergraduate coverage and the knowledge expected in graduate school.

## Failure modes named
- Trying to read advanced proof-based texts without first learning how to write proofs.
- Believing mathematics must be learned in one fixed order instead of flexible category paths.
- Using only the pure-versus-applied taxonomy and missing subject-level organization.
- Underestimating probability and statistics because it seems less glamorous than other fields.
- Choosing a statistics text mismatched to your background, such as mathematical statistics without calculus or proof skills.
- Treating category boundaries as rigid when subjects like algebraic topology or applied math naturally span several stacks.

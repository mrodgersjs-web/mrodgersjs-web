# 02. Nick Pidgeon- Fukushima: a normal accident?

Nick Pidgeon explains how organizational accident theory, the Swiss cheese model, and Perrow’s “normal accident” lens frame Fukushima as a socio-technical, governance-driven failure rather than a natural disaster or isolated operator error.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Treat major accidents as complex socio-technical breakdowns, not purely technical failures or isolated human errors.
- Distinguish individual accidents (frequent, limited causes/consequences, localized) from organizational accidents (rare, many causes, major consequences, long history).
- Map defenses in depth as layers of technical, human, and organizational controls, and expect major failures only when multiple layers align and fail together (Swiss cheese model).
- Search for “disaster incubation”: a long, unnoticed build-up of unsafe conditions, shared assumptions, and degraded defenses before the triggering event.
- Apply Perrow’s “normal accident” lens: systems with interactive complexity and tight coupling can produce catastrophic cascades from a single initiating event.
- Use the Fukushima Diet inquiry report as a template for tracing root causes to governance failure, collusion, and regulatory capture rather than nature or operator error alone.
- Recognize regulatory capture: cosy operator–regulator–government relationships create complacency and suppress questioning of safety assumptions.
- Apply precautionary reasoning: uncertainty about low-probability, high-consequence hazards should strengthen, not weaken, defenses.
- Audit spatial and systemic vulnerabilities in redundancy: backup power, cooling, and controls must survive common-cause failures such as flooding.
- Evaluate safety culture through leadership commitment, rule flexibility, and organizational learning—not just rules and certifications on paper.
- Distinguish “critical trust” (reliance plus healthy skepticism) from blind trust when depending on high-risk institutions.

## Failure modes named
- “Act of God” / unpredictable complexity excuse for accidents.
- Isolated human error framing that deflects from organizational causes.
- Regulatory capture and cosy operator–regulator–government collusion.
- Group insularity and complacency in safety culture.
- Downplaying known external hazards because probability estimates are uncertain.
- Selective use of probability theory to argue against additional countermeasures.
- Common-cause failures wiping out redundancies because backup systems were co-located in vulnerable spots.
- Assumption that total loss of all power was impossible.
- Confusing production goals and maintenance of public confidence with genuine safety assurance.

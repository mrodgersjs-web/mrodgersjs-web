# Asymmetric Warfare: A Symposium | Panel 1: Strategy, Law, and Narrative

Scholars from strategy, law, ethics, and the humanities argue that asymmetry is a constitutive feature of all warfare—spanning capability, legitimacy, narrative, and time—and that just-war principles, international humanitarian law, and political judgment must be reframed through dynamic, context-specific analysis rather than static symmetry.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Treat "asymmetric warfare" not as a special modern category but as a constant of all wars; belligerents routinely seek decisive asymmetries to break stalemates and achieve advantage.
- Analyze asymmetry across multiple registers—material capability, technique and weaponry, authority and legitimacy, legal status, narrative, and spatial-temporal reach—rather than reducing it to size disparity.
- Approach jus ad bellum and jus in bello as dynamic problems when belligerents' objectives and capacities differ; proportionality and distinction must be reevaluated in context.
- Use the practical just-war triad of legitimacy, distinction, and proportionality as a decision procedure for soldiers and commanders, not as abstract axioms that resolve themselves.
- Assign forward-looking responsibility to political leadership to avoid conflicts likely to produce high civilian harm, and backward-looking responsibility to evaluate whether enough was done to protect civilians.
- Apply a leadership-level strategic calculus before using heavy force in densely populated areas: ask whether the civilian-entangled context is a legitimate military target and whether alternative means exist.
- Frame nuclear asymmetry through the "equality of survival" or "equality of injurability" model: a small decision-making minority holds the power to extinguish all others, violating the Hobbesian basis of social contract.
- Recognize that wars exceed their declared spatial and temporal boundaries; account for asynchronicity, traumatic memory, contested territory, and uncontainable collateral effects.
- Treat legal and ethical resolution not as a fixed binary verdict but as iterative negotiation and accommodation, aided by a "third space" of critical or poetic contestation.
- Distinguish between equality of force and moral legitimacy: justice may require the defender to possess greater force than the aggressor, so symmetry of violence is not a self-evident moral good.
- Connect a nation's interpretation of war's rules to its broader political position; dominant powers often view asymmetric challenges as threats to an advantageous international order.
- Evaluate international humanitarian law by its orientation toward short wars and long peace; restrictions on weapons aim to prevent lasting suffering, grief, and revenge in defeated populations.

## Failure modes named
- Treating asymmetric warfare as a discrete, recent phenomenon rather than an enduring feature of armed conflict.
- Relying on linear, progressive narratives of war's causes and consequences that ignore asynchronic, traumatic residues and contested histories.
- Applying proportionality and distinction formulas without attending to the inequality of power, authority, and context in which they are invoked.
- Allowing political privilege to determine when rules of war apply; dominant powers demanding adherence to rules while resisting constraints that challenge their advantage.
- Assuming equality of force is a moral ideal independent of the distinction between aggressor and defender or the aims of the war.
- Permitting heavy aerial or artillery bombardment of civilian-entangled areas without a prior legitimacy calculus and search for alternatives.
- Using the claim that "the enemy hides among civilians" as an automatic justification for collateral harm rather than as a trigger for prevention and harm minimization.
- Treating nuclear arsenals as instruments of state security while ignoring the existential asymmetry between a few decision-makers and the survival of humanity and other life.

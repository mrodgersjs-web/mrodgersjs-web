# A Brief History of Epistemology

A historical tour of how philosophers have defined knowledge, justified belief, and the limits of what we can know.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Start any knowledge inquiry by asking three questions: what is the nature of knowledge, what is its scope, and how reliable are its sources (senses, testimony, social institutions).
- Use thought experiments to stress-test whether true belief counts as knowledge, such as the hologram vase, Plato’s running statues, and the cave of shadows.
- Model knowledge as “tethered true belief”: a true belief that is anchored to something permanent or sufficiently justified, not merely accidentally correct.
- Map claims onto Plato’s divided line, distinguishing sense-based opinion from reason-governed knowledge and authentic forms from mere copies/fakes.
- Practice epistemic ascent: shift attention from changing appearances toward the intelligible structures or principles that underlie them.
- Apply foundationalism by treating some beliefs as bedrock propositions whose justification does not depend on other beliefs.
- Test any foundation with the epistemic regress argument: every chain of reasons ends in infinite regress, circularity, or an arbitrary stopping point.
- Distinguish skeptical stances: Pyrrhonian skepticism suspends judgment for tranquility, while Cartesian skepticism actively denies that we have knowledge.
- Classify knowledge sources into empiricism (sense experience), rationalism (innate ideas), and Cartesian reconstruction from the indubitable self.
- Use Hume’s causal critique to recognize that constant conjunction produces anticipation, not observable necessary connection; induction is therefore epistemically precarious.
- Adopt Kant’s Copernican turn: the mind actively structures phenomenal reality, so scientific knowledge is of appearances, not things-in-themselves.
- Analyze ordinary-language language games to see how bedrock propositions function as rules that make doubt meaningful or meaningless.
- Apply coherentism when foundations fail: justify a belief by its fit within a web of beliefs that can be revised at the edges.
- Naturalize epistemology by studying belief and theory formation as empirical psychological processes.
- Diagnose Gettier cases and facade-barn cases: justified true belief can still fail to be knowledge when truth is causally disconnected or the process is unreliable in the circumstances.

## Failure modes named
- Treating mere true belief or accidental correctness as knowledge.
- Mistaking shadows, images, or copies for the real thing.
- Claiming a foundation while unable to justify the stopping point.
- Running into circular justification or infinite regress.
- Confusing Pyrrhonian suspension of judgment with Cartesian denial of knowledge.
- Inferring necessary causation from repeated correlation alone.
- Projecting mental structures onto unknowable things-in-themselves.
- Trusting testimony or institutional sources without examining their selection mechanisms.
- Gettier-style epistemic luck: true conclusion supported by false or irrelevant evidence.
- Relying on a generally reliable process in circumstances where it is unreliable.

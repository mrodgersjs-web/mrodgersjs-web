# NASA Engineer explains why systems engineering is the best form of engineering

A NASA systems engineer argues that systems engineering is best learned by first mastering one technical domain, then zooming out to integrate components, identify bottlenecks, and take ownership across disciplines.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Define a system as a set of components interacting to achieve a common goal.
- Recognize feedback loops as the distinguishing feature of control systems (e.g., a thermostat).
- Treat systems engineering as the discipline of designing and integrating components so they work together toward the system's goal.
- Build depth in one component first, then zoom out to understand interfaces and the whole system.
- State the system's top-level goal explicitly before analyzing individual components.
- Map all relevant components and their interfaces (electrical, thermal, mechanical, software, orbital, ground, etc.).
- Identify bottlenecks that block the goal, then address each bottleneck as a focused sub-problem.
- Benchmark the system against competing architectures or technologies to make a compelling case.
- Increase system value by repurposing the same hardware for multiple functions (e.g., joint communication and sensing).
- Take full ownership of the integrated system rather than delegating every unfamiliar domain.
- Learn missing domains by reading papers, reading books, and asking experts questions.
- Anchor cross-domain understanding in foundational physics (mechanics, electromagnetics, Maxwell's equations, charges, fields, waves).
- Cultivate second-order skills that classes rarely teach: problem solving, bottleneck identification, and requirements formulation through big "why" questions.
- Follow a career roadmap: pick one area, master it deeply, then branch out into adjacent components until you are "full stack."

## Failure modes named
- Misconception that systems engineers are jacks of all trades with no real depth.
- Trying to become a systems engineer without first mastering at least one technical component.
- Top-down-only thinking that cannot dive deep when a component breaks.
- Solving problems before clearly identifying the bottleneck.
- Avoiding ownership of unfamiliar domains (e.g., "let the mechanical engineer worry about it").
- Neglecting to ask the big "why" questions and define requirements early.
- Building on weak physics fundamentals, making advanced topics harder than necessary.
- Inability to compare or justify the system against competing technologies.

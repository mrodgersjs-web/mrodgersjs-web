# What are Incentives?

Incentives are rewards and punishments that shape behavior, illustrated by the 1787 British reform in which paying sea captains per prisoner who arrived alive rather than per prisoner who embarked raised survival rates from catastrophic to 99%.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Define incentives as any reward or punishment that systematically motivates behavior.
- Map the payoff structure of a system before predicting how actors will act.
- Distinguish input-based metrics (prisoners loaded) from outcome-based metrics (prisoners arriving alive).
- Tie compensation or rewards to the outcome you actually want, not to a proxy that can be gamed.
- Recognize that moral appeals, public outrage, and clergy exhortation are weak when incentives push the other way.
- Apply the convict-shipping case as a canonical principal-agent problem.
- Expect agents to respond quickly to changed payoffs even without changed character or values.
- Use outcome-based payment to make the agent internalize the welfare of the affected party.
- Design policy by asking, “What does this rule make it profitable to do?”
- Check for perverse incentives where the reward function subsidizes the harm it is meant to prevent.
- Treat “economy beat sentiment and benevolence” as a reminder that structure often trumps intention.

## Failure modes named
- Relying on moral suasion or appeals to humanity to override misaligned payoffs.
- Passing regulations requiring better treatment without changing the reward structure.
- Paying for inputs or effort (prisoners embarked) instead of the desired outcome (alive arrival).
- Assuming actors will act benevolently while the incentive structure rewards neglect or abuse.
- Ignoring the gap between stated goals and the actual incentives created by payment rules.

# ACES 102: Systems Thinking Stock and Flows

A short primer on mapping a system’s performance as stocks that change through inflows and outflows, with reinforcing and balancing loops and other influencing variables.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Represent a system’s performance as a stock: an accumulated quantity stored over time, like a storage bin (water in a tub, relationship bank account, completed assignments).
- Model changes in a stock with flows: inflows fill it and outflows drain it; the net balance determines whether the stock rises, stays constant, or falls.
- Use cloud symbols for sources and sinks when the origin or destination of a flow lies outside the boundary you are modeling.
- Choose performance measures that reflect the stocks you care about; sustainability offers many possible metrics across environmental, social, and economic spheres.
- Recognize that measures can conflict in complex systems: improving one indicator often influences another, so trade-offs must be made deliberately.
- Identify reinforcing loops when the amount in a stock influences its own flow, causing the stock to grow or decline over time (e.g., savings-account interest).
- Identify balancing loops when the stock level regulates its own rate of change and pushes the system toward a steady state (e.g., slowing down as completed assignments pile up).
- Connect stocks to each other when the outflow of one stock becomes the inflow of another (e.g., pending assignments becoming completed assignments).
- Map external factors and auxiliary variables that open or throttle flows without being stocks themselves (e.g., coffee, deadlines, interest rates).
- Use the diagram to ask what causes your chosen key-performance-indicator stock to rise or fall and whether those drivers form reinforcing or balancing feedback.
- Distinguish true feedback loops from simple left-to-right relationships; draw a loop only when a stock’s level changes a flow that feeds back into the same stock.
- Treat “fix one problem” interventions cautiously in complex systems because changing one flow or stock can ripple through other connected measures.

## Failure modes named
- Selecting a single sustainability metric without considering how it affects other environmental, social, or economic measures.
- Ignoring the real source or sink of a flow because the “cloud” hides it, missing pollution, resource limits, or downstream consequences.
- Assuming a reinforcing loop is always desirable; unrestrained growth can become unstable or depleting if there is no balancing outflow.
- Confusing a sequential relationship with a feedback loop.
- Letting a balancing loop hide an underlying problem, such as feeling accomplished because a stock is high and then reducing the inflow that sustained it.
- Failing to identify external factors that throttle flows and therefore change stock trajectories.
- Modeling only one stock and missing coupled stocks where one stock’s outflow feeds another’s inflow.

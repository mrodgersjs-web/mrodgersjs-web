# Structural Holes

A low-embeddedness tie between two otherwise disconnected communities creates a structural hole that the spanning node can exploit as a broker.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Define embeddedness of a tie by counting the common friends shared between two people.
- Use high embeddedness as a proxy for trust, because shared contacts raise the social penalty for cheating.
- Identify low-embeddedness ties as potential bridges between otherwise separate communities.
- Map the neighborhoods of both endpoints to check whether their communities overlap or are isolated.
- Recognize a structural hole when two communities lack any alternate path connecting them.
- Treat the person spanning a structural hole as a broker who controls information and transaction flow.
- Analyze whether the broker benefits from preserving the hole, such as by monopolizing access to a market or resource.
- Notice that adding a shared contact across communities creates an alternate route and weakens the broker's monopoly.
- Connect structural holes to Granovetter's weak ties: low-embeddedness ties often span disconnected groups.

## Failure modes named
- Treating low embeddedness as purely risky rather than recognizing its potential bridging value.
- Confusing high embeddedness (trust) with the strategic advantage created by a structural hole.
- Ignoring that brokers may actively block direct connections between communities to protect their position.
- Failing to see that introducing even one cross-community tie can collapse a structural hole and reduce monopoly power.

# Resilience

Resilience is an ecosystem’s capacity to recover from disturbances, and coral reefs maintain it through seed sources, recovery time, and full species diversity—qualities that managers can protect by reducing controllable stressors and safeguarding refuges.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Define resilience as the ability of an ecosystem to recover from disturbances such as hurricanes, sediment, and predatory starfish.
- Recognize that repeated disturbances progressively erode resilience and lengthen recovery time.
- Detect the tipping point where recovery time exceeds the interval between disturbances, producing a state of chronic disturbance.
- Separate manageable local stressors (pollution, overfishing) from unmanageable global drivers (warming-induced temperature swings).
- Identify the need for a seed source of larvae/embryos to recolonize disturbed reefs.
- Map uncertainty around larval origins and connectivity before designing protection.
- Set aside low-disturbance refuge areas to give reefs extra recovery time from unavoidable stressors.
- Protect the full species complement, not just corals, to preserve ecological function.
- Include all ecological players such as herbivores, carnivores, sharks, snails, crabs, and small fish.
- Use biodiversity and ecological complexity as indicators and management goals for resilience.
- Combine local stress reduction with refugia to maximize recovery capacity under global change.

## Failure modes named
- Chronic disturbance trap, where recovery time becomes longer than the time to the next disturbance.
- Resilience erosion from repeated, overlapping shocks.
- Conservation that protects only corals while neglecting the functional species reef ecosystems need.
- Assuming local management alone can offset global warming-driven temperature swings.
- Managing reefs without knowing larval seed-source distances and connectivity.
- Treating resilience as a fixed property rather than a degradable condition.

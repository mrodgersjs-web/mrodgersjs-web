# Words That Win: How To Instantly Influence Anyone (use ethically)

The video teaches seven principles of persuasion—social proof, scarcity, consistency, reciprocity, authority, liking, and risk mitigation—plus an ethical mindset of only influencing people toward choices that genuinely benefit them.

## Procedure / models distilled
- **Social proof**: people use others’ behavior to judge value; create visible demand or activity around yourself, ideas, or offers to make them appear more attractive.
- **Scarcity**: perceived rarity increases value; highlight limited availability or time windows only when real, because false scarcity is a common manipulation tactic.
- **Consistency**: people want to act in line with prior statements and self-image; before changing a mind, show that their past actions already support your view.
- **Reputation to live up to**: give someone a positive label they want and can believe, then let consistency pressure guide their behavior.
- **Reciprocity**: people feel obliged to return favors; offer genuine value first, but watch for gifts given only to extract a larger later request.
- **Rejection-then-retreat**: an extreme opening request followed by a smaller fallback can make the smaller ask feel like a concession you must accept; recognize it and hold your ground.
- **Authority**: credentials, titles, uniforms, and expertise increase persuasiveness; verify incentives and seek second opinions before acting on expert advice.
- **Liking**: people say yes more easily to those they like; charisma, rapport, and goodwill lower resistance.
- **Risk mitigation**: reduce the other person’s perceived downside—free trials, guarantees, small commitments—to overcome loss aversion and fear of regret.
- **Ethical persuasion filter**: only try to persuade someone when you genuinely believe the outcome is good for them; manipulation produces resentment and destroys trust.

## Failure modes named
- Following social proof can make you pursue people you don’t like or ignore people you do, especially in closed social systems like schools or offices.
- Scarcity speech (“almost sold out,” “limited time”) triggers loss aversion and is frequently fabricated to force fast, poor decisions.
- Time scarcity is a hallmark of scams and high-pressure sales.
- Fake laughter or staged approval fails against experienced evaluators because they rely on talent, not crowd cues.
- Reciprocity can trap you into accepting unreasonable favors or concessions out of obligation.
- Rejection-then-retreat backfires when the first request is an obvious lowball, making the other person dislike you.
- Blind trust in authority can lead to bad business or medical advice even from real experts.
- Symbols of authority can be faked, making people overvalue products or people.
- Persuading someone into a bad decision may win short-term compliance but produces long-term resentment and reputational damage.

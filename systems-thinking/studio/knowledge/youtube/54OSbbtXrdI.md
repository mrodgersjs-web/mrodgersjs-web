# How Elon Musk solves problems: First principles thinking explained | Lex Fridman Podcast Clips

Elon Musk explains first-principles thinking as reasoning upward from physics-based axioms, using limit cases and raw-material cost floors to expose whether engineering problems stem from design fundamentals or inherited assumptions.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Treat physics as the unbreakable law and everything else as a recommendation; start any technology problem by checking it does not violate conservation of energy, momentum, or other physical principles.
- Apply first-principles analysis by boiling a problem down to the most fundamental truths you are most confident about, establishing that as the axiomatic base.
- Reason upward from the axiomatic base and then cross-check the conclusion back against those foundational truths.
- Use “thinking in the limit” by scaling a variable—volume, time, number of units—to a very large or very small value to see how behavior changes.
- When diagnosing cost, ask whether a part would still be expensive at a million units per year; if yes, the cause is fundamental design, not low volume.
- Separate economies-of-scale excuses from real design issues by testing the cost at theoretical high volume before accepting “low volume” as the explanation.
- Compute the raw material value of all constituent elements in a product to find the asymptotic cost floor, as if atoms could be waved into shape by magic.
- Recognize that at high volume, manufacturing cost can asymptotically approach the raw material value of the constituents plus any required intellectual-property licensing costs.
- Start design from the platonic ideal—the perfect arrangement of atoms for the best possible product—rather than from the tools and methods already on hand.
- After defining the ideal, determine what new tools, methods, or materials would be needed to move atoms into that desired shape.
- Combine both directions of thought: what can be built with current tools, and what is the theoretical perfect product, then let each inform the other.
- Treat the platonic ideal as a moving target; update it as you learn, since the definition of the perfect product changes with new information.
- Reduce complexity or change materials when a product remains expensive even after volume is assumed to be very high.

## Failure modes named
- Blaming high cost on low unit volume when the real driver is fundamental design complexity or foolishness.
- Starting design from familiar tools, parts, and methods instead of from the ideal arrangement of matter.
- Letting the inertia and momentum of how things have always been done constrain the solution space.
- Defaulting to existing capabilities and therefore producing only what those tools can make, not the best possible product.
- Failing to update the platonic ideal as new knowledge changes what “perfect” looks like.

# Dr. Derek Cabrera reveals that systems thinking is a complex adaptive system | Academic Conferences

Systems thinking is the discipline of aligning our mental models with the VUCA real world through four universal patterns of structuring information: distinctions, part-whole relationships, relationships, and perspectives.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Recognize the VUCA world: reality is volatile, uncertain, complex, and ambiguous, not linear, ordered, or machine-like.
- Identify the central mismatch: wicked problems emerge when our LAME-O thinking (linear, anthropocentric, mechanistic, ordered) collides with how real systems actually work.
- Treat systems thinking as a feedback loop: mental models approximate reality, reality gives feedback, and repeated adaptation improves the approximation.
- Deconstruct mental models into information plus thinking: data/content is structured by cognition to create meaning, knowledge, and models.
- Use distinctions as boundary-making: every idea or thing is defined by what it is and what it is not.
- Apply part-whole thinking recursively: any system can be split into parts or lumped into larger wholes, across horizontal and vertical scales.
- Become a "slumper": combine reductionist splitting with holistic lumping instead of choosing one side.
- Map relationships explicitly: relating two things creates meaning, but hidden relationships in nature are where unintended consequences live.
- Zoom into relationships as systems: a relationship itself is a complex system deserving distinction and part-whole analysis, not a simple link.
- Take multiple perspectives deliberately: changing perspective changes which distinctions, groupings, and relationships you see.
- Use visual mapping as the primary practice: drawing systems is the fastest way to internalize systems thinking beyond abstract concepts.
- Apply the four patterns together: distinctions, part-whole, relationships, and perspectives interact and can be mapped with tools like Plectica.

## Failure modes named
- Reality bias: believing we experience the world directly rather than through a veil of mental models.
- Forcing reality to fit the model: ignoring feedback and making the evidence conform to existing beliefs.
- Linear intervention in nonlinear systems: producing unintended consequences such as the DDT spraying in Borneo that caused roof collapse, rat plagues, and parachuting cats.
- Anthropocentric and mechanistic metaphors: thinking the universe is clockwork or the mind is a computer.
- Faulty grouping: accepting a single hierarchy, category, or boundary as correct when alternative part-whole structures reveal different truths.
- Relating without distinguishing: connecting ideas without clarifying what each thing is, leading to marketing-induced or unfounded associations.
- Single-perspective lock-in: failing to shift distinctions, groupings, and relationships by looking through another point of view.

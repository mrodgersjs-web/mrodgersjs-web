# Lecture: Expected Utility vs. Prospect Theory,  Judgment and Decision Making

This lecture contrasts expected utility theory’s rational, mathematically optimal choice model with Kahneman and Tversky’s prospect theory, which explains how loss aversion, reference dependence, and diminishing sensitivity shape risky decisions.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Use simple gambles as controlled proxies for complex real-world choices to study decision rules.
- Apply expected utility theory by selecting the option that maximizes objective probabilistic value, treating the decision-maker as a rational agent.
- Recognize prospect theory as a descriptive model of actual human risky choice without assuming perfect rationality.
- Evaluate outcomes relative to a reference point rather than in absolute final-state terms.
- Apply loss aversion by weighting potential losses more heavily than equivalent gains.
- Identify diminishing sensitivity by assessing changes as ratios or proportions rather than absolute amounts.
- Compare the psychological value of outcomes to the objective dollar value when predicting decisions.
- Treat current wealth as a reference state and judge gains or losses against prior states.
- Use the stock-sale scenario to detect loss aversion: prefer to sell an asset showing a paper gain over an equivalent asset showing a paper loss.
- Apply the Jack-and-Jill example to model how identical end states can produce different emotional evaluations based on recent change.
- Test whether decision patterns follow logical equivalence or are distorted by emotional response to losses and gains.
- Map prospect theory’s three components—loss aversion, reference dependence, and diminishing sensitivity—onto financial, economic, and everyday risky choices.

## Failure modes named
- Modeling humans as purely rational agents who always compute the mathematically correct answer.
- Evaluating outcomes in isolation or final-state terms instead of relative to a prior reference point.
- Ignoring that mathematically equivalent options can trigger different emotional reactions.
- Treating absolute dollar changes as psychologically equivalent when proportional changes differ.
- Assuming two people with identical current wealth are equally happy without considering recent gains or losses.
- Overlooking how loss aversion can drive suboptimal asset sales, investment holdouts, or risk avoidance.

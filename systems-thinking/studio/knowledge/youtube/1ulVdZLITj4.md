# Phi 101 Lecture 2.1.1: Epistemology - Part 1

An introduction to epistemology that uses skeptical hypotheses, three metaphysical models of reality, and historical responses from Descartes to G.E. Moore to probe how we can claim to know anything.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Test any claim to knowledge by recursively asking "how do you know that you know what you think you know?"
- Verify statistical headlines by checking whether the operation used on subgroup data actually supports the reported aggregate claim
- Map metaphysical positions onto three models of reality: abyss/matterism, matrix/mindism, and cave/dualism
- Use Plato's cave as a dual procedure: distinguish shadow-reality from higher reality, and expect philosophical insight to be both liberating and socially costly
- Classify truth theories into epistemic realism and epistemic relativism before evaluating a knowledge claim
- Apply the skeptical-challenge argument: if a coherent skeptical hypothesis can be raised against P, then the assertion that one knows P is undermined
- Contrast rationalism and empiricism as the two broad classical strategies for grounding knowledge
- Use Cartesian methodic doubt to strip away all beliefs, then rebuild only from what is clear, distinct, and indubitable
- Apply Occam's razor to prefer common-sense realism over more complicated skeptical scenarios such as dreaming or the Matrix
- Deploy a Moorean common-sense proof by starting from indubitable particular claims to refute radical skepticism
- Deploy semantic counter-arguments that show skepticism may presuppose the very knowledge it attempts to deny
- Shift the burden of proof onto the skeptic rather than accepting that a mere possibility of doubt defeats knowledge
- Calibrate degrees of certainty and withhold belief only when a claim is exactly as probable as its negation

## Failure modes named
- Treating the sum of subgroup percentages as a population-wide average or majority
- Assuming reputable sources or generally good thinkers are immune to error
- Holding beliefs groundlessly because of culture, upbringing, or mere feeling
- Conflating "not absolutely certain" with "totally uncertain"
- Treating a skeptical hypothesis as proven rather than as a mere unexcluded possibility
- Confusing a rebuttal with a refutation
- Accepting that the mere ability to doubt a belief automatically defeats knowledge of it
- Failing to distinguish different levels of certainty when deciding whether to believe or withhold belief

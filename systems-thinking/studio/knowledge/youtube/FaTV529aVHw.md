# Correlation vs. Causation: The "Third Variable" Trick You Missed

The video explains that correlation alone never proves causation because of hidden third variables and directionality ambiguity, and it shows how controlled experiments are required to establish true cause-and-effect relationships.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Define correlation as a relationship where two variables change together, not as a causal claim.
- Define causation as a direct cause-and-effect link where one thing makes another happen.
- Use the Pearson correlation coefficient (R) to measure the strength and direction of a linear relationship.
- Interpret R = +1 as perfect positive correlation, R = -1 as perfect negative correlation, and R = 0 as no relationship.
- Read R values in context: real-world perfect scores are rare; ~0.8 is strong, ~0.2 is weak.
- Apply the cardinal rule: correlation does not equal causation.
- Detect the third variable problem by asking whether an unmeasured factor is secretly influencing both observed variables.
- Detect the directionality problem by asking whether A causes B, B causes A, or neither.
- Distinguish correlational research, which observes patterns with low causal confidence, from experimental research, which tests causality with high confidence.
- Design experiments using random assignment to create equivalent groups at the start.
- Include a control group or placebo condition to provide a valid comparison baseline.
- Manipulate only one variable at a time while holding all other conditions constant.
- Treat correlation as a clue or "smoke" that signals where to investigate, never as proof of the "fire."
- When encountering headlines or studies, pause and ask "What is the hidden third variable?" before accepting a causal claim.

## Failure modes named
- Assuming that because two variables move together, one must cause the other.
- Missing the third variable problem and accepting spurious correlations at face value.
- Confusing directionality, i.e., not knowing which variable is cause and which is effect.
- Treating correlational research as if it were experimental evidence of causation.
- Letting sensational headlines drive conclusions without probing for hidden confounders.

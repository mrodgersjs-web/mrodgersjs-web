# WHY & HOW PRICE MOVES + MARKET DYNAMICS FOR BEGINNERS - AUCTION MARKET THEORY (AMT) SIMPLIFIED

Auction Market Theory (AMT) reframes markets as continuous two-sided auctions seeking fair value, where price moves when aggressive market orders overwhelm passive limit orders, producing alternating balance (range/indecision) and imbalance (trend/conviction) regimes.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Use AMT as a conceptual framework, not a strategy, for understanding buyer-seller interaction and price discovery.
- View every market as a continuous two-sided auction whose purpose is to facilitate trades and locate fair value.
- Define fair value as the price zone containing roughly 70% of volume/time, with the point of control marking the peak volume or time price.
- Diagnose the current regime by distinguishing balance (rotational/ranging, indecision, no consensus) from imbalance (trending, aggressive conviction, emerging consensus).
- In a balance regime, expect price to oscillate around central value and repeatedly test the edges of value until one side proves conviction.
- In an imbalance regime, identify which side is aggressively outnumbering the other and follow the directional price discovery.
- Model price movement as market orders (takers/cannonballs) overwhelming limit orders (makers/walls).
- Treat limit orders as liquidity walls that must be eaten through before price can move directionally.
- If price re-enters a balance area, expect mean reversion toward the opposite edge unless there is a strong reaction from the point of control.
- Use the edges of value inside a balance area as defined risk/reward rotation zones until a breakout proves otherwise.
- Once price exits a balance area, expect continuation and imbalance rather than immediate rotation.
- Structure market bias around two core questions: what direction does the market want to go, and how well is it doing at going there?

## Failure modes named
- Believing price moves due to a "lack of buyers or sellers" rather than an imbalance between aggressive and passive orders.
- Treating AMT as a trading strategy instead of an explanatory framework for reading market structure.
- Trading a balance area as if it were a trending market, or chasing an imbalance as if it were a range.
- Ignoring the point of control as the central reference for balance-area rotation and breakout confirmation.

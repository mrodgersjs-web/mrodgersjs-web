# 14) Hyperstructures are a valuable and free coordination headache with Connor McCormick

Connor McCormick argues that public-goods funding is fundamentally a coordination-cost problem, and that blockchain-style consensus mechanisms originally designed for adversarial coordination (e.g., the Byzantine Generals Problem) may offer models for enabling communities to collectively fund valuable shared infrastructure.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Amend definitions by separating the axes of "valuable to own" and "free to use" rather than treating value as a single property.
- Reframe public-goods funding as a coordination-cost problem rather than a revenue-generation problem.
- Map free-rider dynamics (tragedy of the commons, holdout problem, social-cost problem) to rising coordination costs as beneficiary groups scale.
- Use scale analysis: expect coordination costs to rise non-linearly with the number of people who benefit from a shared good.
- Treat the Byzantine Generals Problem as a coordination model, where the challenge is getting many actors to act simultaneously despite adversarial signals.
- Apply Bitcoin's solution to the Byzantine Generals Problem as an analogy for how communities might reach consensus on when and how much to contribute.
- Model collective funding as a consensus problem about timing, amount, and commitment rather than a simple payment transaction.
- Treat Web3 primitives as coordination infrastructure for public goods, not only as financial or ownership instruments.
- Design mechanisms that let a community simultaneously agree on whether to fund a shared good and how much each participant should contribute.
- Distinguish extractive ownership from "ownership as a coordination right" when designing hyperstructures.

## Failure modes named
- Conflating value with monetary price or assuming only paid things are valuable.
- Assuming a hyperstructure can simultaneously be "valuable and free" under standard game-theoretic assumptions.
- Ignoring coordination costs when designing public-goods funding mechanisms.
- Applying small-group funding models directly to large communities without accounting for scale effects.
- The holdout problem, where participants withhold contribution because they expect others to pay.
- The free-rider problem / tragedy of the commons, where individual incentives undermine collective funding.
- Misframing public-goods underprovision as purely an economic problem rather than a coordination problem.

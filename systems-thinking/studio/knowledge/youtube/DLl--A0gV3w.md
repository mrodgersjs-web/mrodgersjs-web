# Game Theory: Introduction

Game theory is the formal study of strategic decision-making among multiple rational agents, showing how the structure of interaction can produce conflict, cooperation, and even ethical norms.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Treat game theory as a theory of rational decision-making in which agents construct plans while anticipating that others are doing the same.
- Begin any analysis by identifying what kind of game is being played, since the appropriate strategy depends on payoff structure and timing.
- Distinguish zero-sum or constant-sum games, where one player's gain is another's loss, from games in which all players can gain or all can lose.
- Use zero-sum framing to model strictly competitive conflict and the distribution of a fixed pie.
- Analyze mutually beneficial or mutually harmful games to see how rational agents can create value together or collude to destroy it.
- Apply Hobbes's framing: ask whether rational self-interest, embedded in the right institutions, can generate norms of cooperation that leave everyone better off.
- Evaluate institutions and incentives by whether they encourage good cooperation and discourage bad cooperation such as cartels, price-fixing, or mafias.
- Scan for third-party effects: a move can impose costs or benefits on people outside the immediate interaction.
- In simultaneous games, choose actions without knowing what other players are currently choosing, as in sealed-bid auctions, stock trades, voting, or play-calling.
- In sequential games, observe prior moves and use that information to respond, as in chess, negotiations, or basketball.
- Recognize that changing the information structure can change advantage: turning a simultaneous game into a sequential one can benefit the player who gains information.
- Understand game theory's limitations: it illuminates strategic behavior and institutional design but does not explain all of ethics or human behavior.

## Failure modes named
- Overextending game theory to explain everything in ethics or human behavior.
- Assuming self-interest and ethics are always opposed rather than potentially mutually supporting.
- Treating all cooperation as good while ignoring destructive cooperation such as cartels, mafias, and price-fixing.
- Failing to classify the game type before choosing a strategy.
- Confusing simultaneous and sequential structure and missing how information timing changes outcomes.
- Revealing predictable patterns that opponents can exploit.
- Neglecting third-party harms or externalities when judging whether an outcome is beneficial.

# Short Story: You don't know which cloud contains rain (The Strength of Weak Ties, Mark Granovetter)

This video explains Mark Granovetter's 1973 study showing that most Boston workers found jobs through weak ties—casual acquaintances—because weak ties bridge separate information pools that strong ties cannot.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Use the weak-tie job-search model: recognize that casual acquaintances, not close friends, are statistically the most common source of new job opportunities.
- Map your network by tie strength: distinguish strong ties (frequent, intimate, high-emotional-investment contact) from weak ties (occasional, casual contact).
- Treat weak ties as information bridges: they connect you to social circles and knowledge pools outside your own.
- Maintain weak ties with low-cost, periodic contact rather than allowing them to decay through neglect.
- Expand your "luck surface area" by deliberately interacting with the wider world beyond your inner circle.
- Audit your close circle for information redundancy: close friends often swim in the same pool of information as you do.
- Recognize that novel opportunities and information travel across structural holes in networks, and weak ties are the primary paths across those holes.
- When seeking opportunities, ask broadly across weak ties rather than only deeply within one tightly knit cluster.
- Be kind to strangers and respectful to casual contacts; you cannot predict which connection will carry the useful opportunity.
- Diversify the topology of your relationships so that non-redundant information can reach you from multiple disconnected groups.

## Failure modes named
- Relying only on close friends for opportunities, trapping yourself in a redundant information pool.
- Neglecting weak ties because they feel distant, unimportant, or unlikely to help.
- Assuming that strong ties are the best source of opportunities because they are trustworthy and familiar.
- Treating strangers or casual contacts poorly, missing unpredictable but valuable opportunities.
- Confusing tie strength with opportunity value: strength does not equal access to novel information.

# Peter Senge: "Systems Thinking for a Better World" - Aalto Systems Forum 2014

Peter Senge argues that systems thinking is an innate, biologically rooted capacity to perceive interdependence, but modern technological and economic systems obscure that interdependence; cultivating systems intelligence therefore requires grounded, emotionally resonant practices that reconnect people with the living world and enable communities to steward shared resources.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Distinguish two layered webs: the innate biological web of life and the human-built economic/technological web placed on top of it.
- Trace mundane acts upstream: follow ordinary behaviors, like plugging in a device, to their hidden energy, material, and ecological dependencies.
- Treat interdependence as the root diagnostic lens: see "interdependence" as the one problem from which many surface problems arise.
- Recognize unintended systemic outcomes: many actors making locally rational choices can produce a collective result that no one wants.
- Avoid splitting the social from the environmental: integrate ecological and human concerns rather than treating them as separate domains.
- Translate systems abstractions into felt, local experience: make interdependence tangible through community-scale projects and sensory engagement.
- Embed the change effort inside the affected community: locate work physically there and begin with what the community values, such as a safe place for children to play.
- Create alternative livelihoods before restricting use: stop extractive practices only alongside viable replacements like organic farming, microfinance, and enterprise development.
- Use community-enforced thresholds: agree on measurable recovery targets and delay harvesting until the system has regenerated sufficiently.
- Rely on community self-regulation rather than external enforcement: durable rules are those the people who use the resource own and enforce themselves.
- Reconnect through emotional relationship: cultivate stewardship by helping people experience love, beauty, and awe for the living world.
- Cultivate innate systems intelligence: provide children and adults with tools, language, and practices, just as musical instruments develop musical intelligence.
- Ground ethics in biology: treat care, compassion, and empathy as inherent human capacities that enable systems intelligence.

## Failure modes named
- Systems ignorance: collectively producing systemic harms that no individual intends.
- Technology dazzle: mistaking human sophistication for independence from ecological limits and ignoring that devices depend on burning former living things.
- Abstraction trap: the word "nature" reinforces a sense that humans are separate from the living world.
- Social–environmental split: treating poverty and ecology as unrelated issue domains.
- Impress-the-teacher communication: writing or speaking to show how much one knows rather than to be understood.
- Enforcement illusion: expecting government regulation alone to stop commons destruction when only the community can enforce restraint.
- Race to the last fish: continuing extraction because no alternative livelihoods exist.
- Undeveloped innate capacity: failing to give children tools and practices that would cultivate their natural systems intelligence.

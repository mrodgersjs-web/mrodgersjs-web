# What Exactly Are Climate Feedback Loops?

This video explains climate feedback loops as cyclical processes in which the output of one climate process becomes the input of another, either amplifying (positive) or dampening (negative) an initial change, using polar examples such as ice-albedo loss and permafrost thaw.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Define a climate feedback loop as a chain reaction in which the climate system responds to itself, amplifying or diminishing an initial change.
- Model the loop structurally: the output of one process becomes the input of the next, creating a continuous cycle.
- Distinguish positive feedback loops, which amplify an initial change, from negative feedback loops, which counteract or reduce it.
- Recognize that positive feedback loops are especially critical because they can accelerate climate change and magnify initial warming.
- Apply the ice-albedo feedback model: warming melts ice and snow, exposing darker surfaces that absorb more sunlight, causing more warming and more melting.
- Apply the permafrost thaw feedback model: thawing permafrost releases methane and carbon dioxide, increasing atmospheric warming and causing further thaw.
- Apply the cloud feedback model: warmer temperatures increase evaporation and cloud cover, and some clouds reflect sunlight back to space, producing a cooling effect.
- Apply the CO₂-fertilization feedback model: higher atmospheric CO₂ can stimulate plant growth, which removes CO₂ from the atmosphere and tempers warming.
- Treat polar regions as critical monitoring sites because they act as early indicators of powerful Earth-system feedbacks.
- Connect local observations, such as melting glaciers or Arctic ecosystem changes, to larger global feedback mechanisms rather than seeing them as isolated events.
- Frame climate action as an intervention in feedback architecture: reducing emissions and protecting natural systems weakens positive loops and strengthens negative ones.

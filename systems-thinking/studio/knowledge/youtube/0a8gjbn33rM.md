# All of Thermodynamics Explained in 11 Minutes

This video explains the core vocabulary, four laws, common processes, and energy-budget relationships that govern how heat, work, and internal energy move and transform in physical systems.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Define a thermodynamic system as the object under observation and the surroundings as everything outside it.
- Classify systems by their exchange boundary: open systems exchange both matter and energy; closed systems exchange only energy; isolated systems exchange neither matter nor energy.
- Use the state-function model for properties that depend only on the current state of a system, not on the path taken to reach it, such as temperature, pressure, and volume.
- Distinguish extensive properties, which scale with the amount of substance, from intensive properties, which do not, such as temperature and pressure.
- Apply the zeroth law to infer thermal equilibrium: if two systems are each in thermal equilibrium with a third system, they are in thermal equilibrium with each other.
- Use the first law, ΔU = Q − W, as an energy-conservation ledger for a system, where an increase in internal energy equals heat added minus work done by the system.
- Apply the second law to determine spontaneous direction: heat flows naturally from hot to cold, and the entropy of an isolated system increases over time.
- Use the third law to anchor the entropy limit: as temperature approaches absolute zero, entropy approaches a minimum value, although absolute zero is practically unattainable.
- Model an isothermal process as one where temperature and therefore internal energy remain constant while pressure and volume change, with heat inflow balancing work output.
- Model an adiabatic process as one with no heat exchange, where work done on or by the system changes its internal energy and therefore its temperature.
- Model an isobaric process as one at constant pressure where heat added causes expansion and heat removed causes contraction.
- Model an isochoric process as one at constant volume where no work is done and added or removed energy changes temperature and pressure.
- Track heat transfer mechanisms by mode: conduction through direct contact, convection through fluids, and radiation through electromagnetic waves.
- Calculate pressure-volume work using W = P ΔV, treating work as positive when the system expands against its surroundings and negative when the surroundings compress the system.
- Use Gibbs free energy, ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, to judge spontaneity: negative ΔG means spontaneous, positive ΔG means non-spontaneous, and zero means equilibrium.

## Failure modes named
- Treating heat as a substance rather than energy in transit driven by a temperature difference.
- Assuming natural processes can run in reverse without external energy input, such as a puddle of water freezing spontaneously at room temperature.
- Believing absolute zero can actually be reached, ignoring the practical impossibility of removing all thermal energy.
- Confusing the signs of work in ΔU = Q − W, where expansion is positive work done by the system and compression is negative work done on the system.
- Misclassifying systems because matter and energy exchange boundaries are not checked separately.
- Mixing extensive and intensive properties when scaling a system up or down.

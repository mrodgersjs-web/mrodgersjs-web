# Forms of Validity in Research and Statistics

The video explains that validity is the accuracy of a measurement, distinct from reliability (consistency), and introduces three core forms of validity—content, criterion, and construct—that underpin trustworthy inferential statistics.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Define validity as the accuracy of a measurement: does it actually capture what it claims to capture?
- Distinguish validity from reliability: reliability is consistency/repeatability, while validity is accuracy.
- Apply the rule of thumb: reliability is necessary but not sufficient for validity; you can have reliability without validity, but not validity without reliability.
- Use the thumb-size example to illustrate a reliable yet invalid measure: thumb size can be measured consistently but does not validly measure intelligence.
- Recognize that an unreliable measure (e.g., an IQ test giving 120 then 94 on retest) cannot be considered valid because inconsistent measurement undermines accuracy.
- Assess content validity by asking whether the sample of test items represents the entire universe of content in a topic.
- Improve content validity by sampling broadly across a domain rather than repeating one narrow item type.
- Assess criterion validity by asking whether a test reflects abilities in a current or future real-world setting.
- Use concurrent criterion validity when the goal is to assess a respondent's current level of ability (e.g., a final exam measuring current knowledge).
- Use predictive validity when the goal is to forecast future performance (e.g., SAT or GRE predicting college/graduate-school success).
- Define a construct as a group of interrelated variables representing an abstract idea (e.g., aggression includes physical, verbal, and relational behaviors).
- Assess construct validity by asking whether a test truly measures the theoretical construct it claims to measure.
- Defend construct validity explicitly when writing research papers, because reviewers will challenge whether you measured the intended construct.
- Quantify construct validity when possible by correlating scale scores with real-world observations or tangible behavioral outcomes.

## Failure modes named
- Treating a reliable measure as automatically valid, leading to false confidence in an inaccurate instrument.
- Declaring a measure valid despite poor test-retest reliability, which undermines any claim to accuracy.
- Building a test with poor content validity by over-sampling a narrow subset of items and missing the broader domain.
- Confusing concurrent and predictive criterion validity, which misaligns the test's purpose with its interpretation.
- Failing to defend construct validity when writing research papers, leaving the core theoretical claim unsupported.
- Treating validity as purely conceptual or argumentative without attempting empirical validation against real-world outcomes.

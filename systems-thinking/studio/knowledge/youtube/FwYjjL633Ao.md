# Tactical Defense vs Strategic Offense: How to Win a Battle in the Middle Ages (1000–1500 AD)

Medieval battle success came from holding tactically defensive positions while pursuing strategic offensive aims, using attacks on honor, sieges, or plunder to force the enemy to attack first.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Treat tactical defense as the stronger form of battle: hold a fixed position and make the enemy come to you.
- Choose the battlefield to maximize defensive advantage, such as hills, bottlenecks, marshes, or behind field fortifications.
- Preserve formation integrity by remaining stationary; only a cohesive, wall-like front can withstand an assault.
- Force the tactical initiative onto the enemy while retaining the strategic initiative yourself.
- Provoke the enemy's honor through invasion, symbolic timing, or threatening letters so that inaction appears cowardly.
- Besiege a symbolically or economically vital city to compel the defender to abandon their strong position and relieve it.
- Use plundering raids (chevauchée) to destroy resources and generate political pressure on the enemy ruler.
- Maneuver or retreat to a chosen killing ground once the enemy is provoked into pursuit.
- Counterattack only after the attacker has been disordered, weakened, or trapped by terrain.
- Endure losses and plundering until the attacker's logistics, politics, or patience force them to attack or withdraw.

## Failure modes named
- Taking the tactical offensive and marching toward a prepared enemy line.
- Allowing a formation to break up on obstacles, mud, terrain, or incoming fire during movement.
- Getting caught in "the press" between the enemy front and one's own rear ranks.
- Accepting the tactical offensive when provoked by honor, despite holding a superior defensive position.
- Failing to generate enough pressure to force the enemy to attack, leaving the strategic offensive stalled.
- Underestimating the cost and duration of a siege when the defender may sacrifice the city rather than fight.

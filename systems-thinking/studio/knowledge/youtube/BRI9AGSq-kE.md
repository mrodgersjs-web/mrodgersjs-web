# Jeff Bezos: 3 Rules to Having an Efficient Meeting | Inc.

Jeff Bezos outlines three rules—two-pizza teams, six-page narrative memos, and silent reading time—to make meetings smaller, deeper, and more decision-effective.

## Procedure / models distilled
- **Two-pizza team rule**: cap meeting size at the number of people two pizzas can feed, keeping groups small enough to limit opinion fragmentation and speed decision-making.
- **Replace PowerPoint with narrative memos**: use a six-page, narratively structured document with real sentences, topic sentences, verbs, and nouns rather than slide decks.
- **Invest heavily in memo quality**: expect the memo to take a week or more, involving multiple drafts, peer review, cooling-off periods, and fresh-mind rewrites.
- **Begin meetings with silent reading**: allocate the first portion of the meeting for everyone to read the memo in silence, like study hall, before discussion begins.
- **Honor the reading time as load-bearing**: treat the half-hour of silent reading as essential, not optional, so all attendees actually consume the document before debate.
- **Shift from presentation performance to shared reading**: move the cognitive work into a written artifact rather than a live, spoken pitch.
- **Design the memo as a decision artifact**: ensure the document is rigorous and complete enough to serve as the foundation for the subsequent discussion.
- **Use small-team dynamics to lower coordination cost**: fewer participants means fewer competing opinions and easier convergence on conclusions.
- **Guard against performative participation**: structure the meeting so executives cannot bluff or pretend to have read the material because everyone reads it together.

## Failure modes named
- **Meeting bloat**: too many attendees create opinion overload and stall decisions.
- **Slide-driven thinking**: PowerPoint invites bullet-point fragments instead of coherent reasoning.
- **Rushed memo writing**: trying to complete a quality memo in one or two days produces weak documents that undermine the meeting.
- **Pretending to be prepared**: busy executives bluff their way through discussions without having read the material, degrading decision quality.
- **Omitting silent reading time**: if the group skips dedicated reading, the document is not actually read before debate begins.

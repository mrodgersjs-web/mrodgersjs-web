# Episode 4: Complexity Economics Explained

Complexity economics studies economies as interacting, adaptive systems—where non-linearity, tipping points, and heterogeneous agents reshape how we understand and act on crises like climate change.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Treat economies as living complex systems rather than static equilibrium systems.
- Model interactions among heterogeneous households, institutions, and governments.
- Incorporate non-linear dynamics where small inputs can produce disproportionate outputs.
- Identify tipping points and positive feedback loops in physical and social systems.
- Use agent-based models with distinct agents that differ in income, well-being, values, and behavior.
- Let agents learn from the past and adapt their preferences and interactions over time.
- Recognize that instability and change can emerge from internal interactions, not only external shocks.
- Map how regime transitions, social innovations, and knowledge diffusion accelerate under specific initial conditions.
- Develop scenario-based policy under conditions of fundamental uncertainty.
- Simulate possible futures to spot risks to avoid and opportunities to cultivate.
- Build interdisciplinary research capacity rather than training narrowly within a single discipline.

## Failure modes named
- Equilibrium fallacy: assuming climate damage is reversible like a standard economic shock.
- Dissection fallacy: studying social systems as dead specimens instead of living, evolving systems.
- Extrapolation fallacy: assuming the past reliably predicts the future during fundamental transitions.
- Linear thinking: missing tipping points by expecting proportional cause-and-effect relationships.
- Disciplinary silos: lacking researchers who can communicate across fields.
- Wait-and-see governance: delaying action until climate impacts are obvious to everyone.

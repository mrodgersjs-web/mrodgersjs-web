# Lecture 03, concept 10: Helmholtz vs. Gibbs free energy

The lecture distinguishes Helmholtz free energy (F = E − TS, for isolated systems at fixed volume) from Gibbs free energy (G = H − TS = E + PV − TS, for systems at fixed pressure that can exchange volume work), explains why the PV term is often negligible for biochemical reactions, and warns against the common notation sloppiness where F is used for Gibbs energy.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Model an isolated system where only heat exchange occurs and volume/particle number are fixed; use Helmholtz free energy F = E − TS.
- Model a laboratory (chemistry) system open to pressure changes where volume can expand or contract; use Gibbs free energy G = E + PV − TS.
- Treat PV work as the mechanical work done by or on the system when its volume changes at constant pressure.
- Bundle internal energy and pressure-volume work into enthalpy: H = E + PV.
- Express Gibbs free energy compactly as G = H − TS.
- Use the naming conventions F or A for Helmholtz free energy and G for Gibbs free energy.
- Recognize that for reactions with negligible volume change—such as protein folding, solvation, or hydrogen-bond breaking—Δ(PV) ≈ 0, so ΔG ≈ ΔF.
- Accept that chemists sometimes write F = H − TS or F = E − TS when they actually mean Gibbs free energy.
- Ask colleagues “do you mean Gibbs or Helmholtz free energy?” whenever a reported free-energy value might involve systems where PV work matters.

## Failure modes named
- Treating F and G as interchangeable without checking whether volume/pressure work matters.
- Assuming standardized notation and reading F as Helmholtz when the author means Gibbs.
- Ignoring the PV term in systems where volume changes are significant.
- Failing to clarify which free-energy definition a colleague or paper is using.

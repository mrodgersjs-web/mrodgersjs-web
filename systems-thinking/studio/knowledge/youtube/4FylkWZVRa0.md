# Mark Granovetter. 1973. The Strength of Weak Ties.

Granovetter’s classic paper argues that weak social ties—casual acquaintances and indirect contacts—act as bridges between densely knit clusters of strong ties, and therefore serve as the main channels through which novel information, opportunities, and coordinated action spread across larger populations.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Measure tie strength by four interaction features: amount of time spent, content/emotional intimacy, reciprocal consequences, and whether people engage each other as “whole persons.”
- Strong ties tend toward triadic closure: if B is strongly tied to both A and D, A and D are very likely to develop a strong tie, completing a local cluster.
- Weak ties lack the pressure for closure, so they can link separate strong-tie clusters without forcing those clusters to merge.
- A bridge is the only path between two clusters; all bridges are weak ties, although not every weak tie is a bridge.
- If a bridging weak tie strengthens, closure pressure creates alternate paths and destroys its bridge function.
- Strong-tie clusters become self-contained echo chambers; novel information must cross weak-tie bridges to reach other clusters.
- Removing a weak-tie bridge isolates an entire strong-tie cluster from the rest of the network, even though the cluster’s internal links remain intact.
- Job-search success depends on weak ties: information chains to new employers usually run through weak ties and are only a few steps long.
- Social structure (marriage, widowhood, life transitions) imposes structural constraints that can convert strong ties into weak ties or sever them.
- Corporate interlocking directorates exemplify weak-tie networks that circulate business information without requiring personal intimacy.
- Support groups built on weak ties can sometimes solve problems that over-enmeshed primary groups create or cannot address.
- Political mobilization requires weak-tie bridges: the West End’s dense families lacked bridges to coordinate resistance to urban renewal.
- Organizations such as churches, civic clubs, and social movements generate the weak ties that connect otherwise isolated local clusters.

## Failure modes named
- Assuming strong personal ties are the best channels for spreading information widely.
- Over-relying on tight, primary-group clusters, which become internally cohesive but globally fragmented.
- Letting weak ties decay—e.g., losing touch with casual acquaintances after a move—thereby cutting off whole clusters from external information.
- Treating weak ties as alienating or useless rather than as integrative bridges.
- Strengthening a bridge tie until alternate paths form and the bridge disappears.
- Ignoring structural constraints (marital status, bereavement, life transitions) that can abruptly restructure available ties.
- Expecting strong local community bonds alone to protect against large impersonal forces.

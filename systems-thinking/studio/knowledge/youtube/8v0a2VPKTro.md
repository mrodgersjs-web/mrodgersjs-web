# Statistical Mechanics | Entropy and Temperature

The video derives entropy and temperature from counting microstates, showing that temperature is the shared inverse-slope of entropy versus energy at equilibrium and that negative temperature arises when adding energy decreases entropy.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Model a system as N identical two-state particles (energy 0 or 1) with a fixed total energy E.
- Count accessible microstates for a given macrostate using combinations: Ω(E) = N! / (E!(N-E)!).
- Recognize that a larger number of microstates at a fixed energy means greater uncertainty about the exact configuration.
- Correct for identical-particle overcounting by dividing by E!, the number of permutations of the energized particles.
- Compare systems of different sizes: the same energy input raises the temperature more in a small system but increases its number of accessible states by a larger relative amount.
- Define entropy as the natural logarithm of the number of accessible states, S ∝ ln Ω, so entropy tracks microstate multiplicity on a logarithmic scale.
- Bring two isolated subsystems into thermal contact while conserving total energy; the combined number of states is a sum over all energy partitions.
- Observe that for large systems the combined microstate count becomes sharply peaked around an equilibrium energy partition.
- Find equilibrium by maximizing the product Ω₁(E₁) Ω₂(E₂) subject to E₁ + E₂ = constant.
- Convert the maximization condition into an equality of logarithmic derivatives: d ln Ω₁/dE₁ = d ln Ω₂/dE₂.
- Identify this shared quantity as proportional to 1/T, so that 1/T is the inverse slope of entropy versus energy.
- Interpret thermal equilibrium as the equalization of entropy-vs-energy slopes between the two systems.
- Predict energy flow direction by comparing slopes: energy moves toward the configuration that maximizes total entropy.
- Explain negative temperature in bounded-energy systems where adding energy decreases entropy (dS/dE < 0), producing ordered high-energy states such as lasers.

## Failure modes named
- Describing entropy only as "disorder" or "uncertainty" without connecting it to the count of accessible microstates.
- Treating negative temperature as simply "below absolute zero" rather than as a state hotter than any positive-temperature state.
- Assuming all configurations or energy values are equally probable when counting microstates.
- Confusing temperature with total energy or with the raw number of states; temperature depends on how Ω changes with energy.
- Neglecting the indistinguishability of identical particles, which leads to overcounting configurations.

# Managing the Negotiation Within: The Internal Family Systems Model

David Hoffman applies Richard Schwartz’s Internal Family Systems (IFS) model to negotiation and mediation, showing how to work with the competing “parts” inside ourselves and parties so that calm, curious “Self-energy” can guide problem-solving.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Treat the mind as a system of “parts”—metaphorical inner voices (optimistic, pessimistic, competitive, conciliatory, etc.)—not fixed brain regions or pathology.
- Recognize three common families of parts: Managers (proactive protectors that prevent harm), Firefighters (reactive protectors that douse emotional pain, often with anger, arrogance, or impulsivity), and Exiles (wounded parts carrying shame, inadequacy, or old hurt).
- Assume every part is trying to help, even when it takes an extreme role; there are no “bad parts,” only parts that can be healed into more productive roles.
- Notice polarities inside people (e.g., a part that wants to fight and a part that wants to settle) and treat them as natural, balancing forces rather than inconsistency or schizophrenia.
- Access “Self-energy”—the calm, curious, compassionate, clear core that remains when extreme parts step back—and invite that energy to lead the process.
- Before working with parties, apply the model to yourself as mediator: identify your own reactive parts, “unblend,” and avoid taking attacks personally.
- Use a “colloquy” or direct questioning to surface a party’s internal negotiation, naming each part and the concern it protects.
- Validate every part that appears before asking any part to relax, so protective parts feel heard rather than banished.
- In caucus, slow down decisions when a conciliatory part (e.g., gratified by an apology or “thank you”) might override a party’s longer-term interests and cause regret.
- Keep the mediation use of IFS distinct from clinical IFS: in mediation, focus on managing managers and firefighters, not excavating exiles.
- Reframe arrogant, puffed-up, or aggressive behavior as a firefighter protecting a deeply wounded exile, and let that compassion calm your own reactive parts.
- Encourage manager-led problem-solving: delay gladiator impulses, postpone confrontation, use written communication later, and keep children or other third parties out of the crossfire.
- Build mediator effectiveness through three lifelong tracks: concrete skills, underlying theory of how people function, and personal development toward a self-led, congruent, non-reactive presence.

## Failure modes named
- Labeling people with diagnostic categories (e.g., “narcissistic”) that box them in and block curiosity.
- Mistaking alternating inner voices for mental illness or inconsistency.
- Letting a party’s conciliatory part accept a low settlement or apology and later suffer buyer’s remorse.
- A mediator taking an aggressive party’s attack personally and letting reactive parts hijack the process.
- Firefighter parts escalating conflict instead of manager parts delaying and structuring the response.
- Failing to validate all parts, which can make protective parts dig in rather than step back.
- Confusing the clinical healing of exiles with the mediation goal of managing protectors and reaching durable agreements.
- Allowing power differentials to be masked by superficial conciliatory gestures without checking the disempowered party’s mixed parts.

# The Immune System: Innate Defenses and Adaptive Defenses

The video explains how the human immune system combines immediate innate defenses (physical barriers, inflammation, and phagocytes) with learned adaptive defenses (antibody-mediated humoral immunity and T-cell-mediated cellular immunity) to recognize, remember, and eliminate pathogens while preserving self-tolerance.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Layered defense model: immunity is split into innate defenses, which are always active, and adaptive defenses, which learn specific invaders; the two systems communicate and cooperate.
- Surface barriers: keratinized epithelial skin and mucosae line openings and cavities; mucosae are acidic, contain lysozymes and defensins, and trap microbes in mucus to block pathogen entry.
- Phagocytosis mechanism: phagocytes engulf pathogens into vesicles that fuse with lysosomes; acid hydrolases digest the contents, and harmless fragments are released by exocytosis.
- Natural killer cells patrol blood and lymph, detect abnormalities in virus-infected or cancerous cells, and trigger apoptosis in those target cells.
- Inflammatory response: tissue injury releases histamine and cytokines, producing vasodilation and increased capillary permeability; redness, swelling, and pain result, but the fluid flush carries foreign material to lymph nodes and delivers clotting proteins for repair.
- Phagocyte recruitment cascade: neutrophils and macrophages leave red bone marrow, marginate along capillary walls at the injury, perform diapedesis to exit the vessel, and follow chemotactic gradients to the infection site.
- Antigen recognition: antigens are foreign molecules that trigger adaptive responses; the antigenic determinant is the specific region that binds to antibodies or lymphocyte receptors like an enzyme-substrate interaction.
- Lymphocyte maturation and self-tolerance: B and T cells originate from hematopoietic stem cells in red bone marrow, become immunocompetent by producing antigen-specific surface receptors, and are tested for self-tolerance; self-reactive cells undergo apoptosis.
- Humoral immunity: B cells bind antigen, proliferate, and differentiate into plasma cells that secrete antibodies; the primary response is slow, but a faster, stronger secondary response occurs upon re-exposure, creating immunological memory.
- Active vs. passive humoral immunity: active immunity follows infection or vaccination, while passive immunity transfers pre-made antibodies via mother’s milk or gamma-globulin injection without generating memory.
- Antibody structure and classes: antibodies are Y-shaped proteins of two heavy and two light chains joined by disulfide bonds; variable regions form antigen-binding sites, and five immunoglobulin classes (IgM, IgA, IgD, IgG, IgE) differ in location and function.
- Cellular immunity: T cells include CD4 helper cells that activate B cells, T cells, and macrophages, and CD8 cytotoxic cells that kill infected or cancerous cells; activation requires antigen presentation plus co-stimulation, followed by proliferation and differentiation.

## Failure modes named
- Broken skin or breached mucosae allow pathogens to bypass the first physical barrier and cause infection.
- Some pathogens inevitably get past surface defenses, requiring internal innate defenses to limit spread.
- The primary adaptive immune response takes several days, so it cannot stop an initial invasion quickly.
- Lymphocytes that fail self-tolerance are eliminated by apoptosis; surviving T cells represent only about two percent of the original pool.
- Inflammation produces pain and swelling, but treating it as purely harmful ignores its favorable role in repair and pathogen clearance.
- Passive immunity provides antibodies directly but does not train the immune system to produce its own memory response.

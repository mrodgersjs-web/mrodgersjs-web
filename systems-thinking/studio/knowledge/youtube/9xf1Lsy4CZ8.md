# What was the Industrial Revolution?

The Industrial Revolution was Britain's gradual shift from small-scale, hand-powered domestic manufacturing to water-and-steam-powered factory production, enabled by coal, steam engines, canals, roads, and railways, which reshaped urbanization, labor, class structure, and politics between the late 18th and mid-19th centuries.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Energy-transition model: an economy escapes prior constraints by moving from biomass, wood, and muscle power to fossil fuels (coal) and steam engines.
- Infrastructure-follows-resource pattern: extracting coal is insufficient; canals, turnpike roads, and railways must be built to move it cheaply and reliably.
- Cost-cascade effect: cheaper transport, such as canals halving coal prices, lowers input costs and enables further industrial expansion.
- Mechanization and economies of scale: one or two engines can power many machines across multiple floors, concentrating production in factories.
- Urbanization feedback loop: factories cluster near transport, labor, housing, and services, drawing people into cities that then attract more industry.
- First-mover sector pattern: the textile industry, especially cotton, industrializes first before steam power spreads to other sectors.
- Labor deskilling/re-skilling split: mechanization creates skilled machine managers while reducing many workers to repetitive, low-skill tasks.
- Class-formation mechanism: a large, precarious industrial workforce develops shared consciousness and organizes for wages, living conditions, education, and suffrage.
- Elite co-option and social mobility: industrial wealth creates a new capitalist class that can merge with or buy into the old political elite.
- Legislative-enablement model: major infrastructure projects such as canals and railways require Acts of Parliament to authorize construction and rights of way.
- Gradual-over-revolutionary framing: industrial change is uneven and slower than the term "revolution" implies; agriculture and domestic service still dominate before 1850.
- Innovation-diffusion pattern: new productive technologies spread unevenly across regions and industries, creating a patchwork rather than a uniform transformation.

## Failure modes named
- Assuming "revolution" means rapid, total transformation of the whole economy by 1850.
- Overstating factory scale: the average cotton mill employed fewer than 20 people, and Britain was the "workshop" not the "factory" of the world.
- Treating coal alone as sufficient, ignoring the canals, roads, and railways needed to move it.
- Romanticizing progress for workers, overlooking de-skilling, repetitive drudgery, and precarious employment.
- Treating industrialization as a uniform national experience rather than a patchy, sector-specific process.

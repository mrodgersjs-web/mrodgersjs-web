# Edge Master Class 2015: A Short Course in Superforecasting, Class I

Philip Tetlock introduces superforecasting as a discipline of making fine-grained, scored probability judgments on hard questions by using well-formed questions, base rates, incremental belief updating, extremized aggregation of diverse signals, and tight feedback loops.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Frame every uncertain claim as an explicit probability estimate, not as a binary yes/no or a coarse "maybe."
- Apply the "clairvoyance test" to every question: it must be so clearly defined that a genuine clairvoyant could answer it without needing later clarification.
- Use the same granular probabilistic reasoning in high-stakes policy domains that people naturally use in low-stakes domains like sports.
- Aggregate forecasts with "extremizing" algorithms rather than simple averaging when the underlying signals are diverse and weakly correlated.
- Distinguish between intellectual clones repeating the same information and independent advisors with genuinely different information sources.
- Start from base rates and prior frequencies before adjusting for the specific details of the case at hand.
- Update beliefs early and often in small, granular increments as new evidence arrives.
- Decompose complex causal questions into conditional question clusters that map a network of if/then probabilities.
- Compare initial probability estimates against external benchmarks such as prediction markets and bookmakers.
- Run repeated forecasting tournaments with explicit scoring and feedback to improve calibration and accuracy over time.
- Track both hit rate and false-positive rate rather than chasing whichever error was punished last.
- Separate the creative work of generating well-formed questions from the analytical work of generating accurate probability estimates.

## Failure modes named
- Coarse uncertainty collapse: treating a spread of probability estimates as "about 50/50" or reducing uncertainty to a few vague categories.
- Accountability ping-pong: oscillating between false negatives and false positives to avoid political blame rather than improving calibration.
- Siloization or false consensus: assuming advisors are either fully independent or fully redundant without modeling the actual correlation of their information.
- Storytelling promotion: rewarding analysts for compelling narratives instead of testable, scored forecasts.
- Refusal to be scored: experts who claim "I don't make predictions" to evade feedback on their accuracy.
- Convenient counterfactuals: inventing untestable control-group histories to defend preferred policies after the fact.
- Single-scenario fixation: focusing on the most likely outcome instead of assigning probabilities across multiple possibilities.
- Feedback desert: environments where no one keeps explicit score, so overconfidence and underconfidence persist.

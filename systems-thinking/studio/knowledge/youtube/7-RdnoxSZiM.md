# W5 academic lecture - Eric Beinhocker: The economy as a complex and evolving system

Eric Beinhocker reframes capitalism not as an equilibrium market machine but as a complex, evolving problem-solving system whose health depends on inclusion, cooperation, and a fair social contract.

## Procedure / models distilled
- View the economy as a complex adaptive, evolving system rather than an equilibrium-seeking mechanism.
- Reframe capitalism as a problem-solving engine whose purpose is to generate and distribute solutions to human problems.
- Define wealth as the accumulated stock of useful solutions, growth as the rate at which new solutions are created and shared, and prosperity as the set of solutions a society can access.
- Evaluate economic activity by whether it solves problems without creating larger problems for others or for future generations.
- Treat markets and the state as an interdependent ecosystem rather than opponents in a left/right contest.
- Build and protect a virtuous cycle: inclusion and fair social contract → reciprocity and trust → cooperation → innovation and rising prosperity.
- Hold firms accountable to all stakeholders and restore their purpose as problem-solving organizations, not shareholder-value maximizers.
- Measure economic success by real increases in problem-solving capability and human prosperity, not by GDP or price.
- Recognize that modern problem-solving depends on vast networks of cooperation and on systems embedded in society and the environment.
- Treat economic ideas as an "intellectual operating system" that authorizes structural power shifts and policy regimes.
- Use historical reform moments as models for system-wide, multi-domain change across education, tax, labor, and governance.

## Failure modes named
- Equilibrium thinking: modeling the economy as a ball settling into a bowl and ignoring path-dependence, power, and evolution.
- Shareholder value revolution: collapsing the firm from a multi-stakeholder institution into a single-stakeholder wealth-extraction vehicle.
- Neoclassical/neoliberal ideology justifying deregulation, financialization, union decline, and tax burdens shifted from capital to labor.
- Extractive capitalism replacing constructive capitalism, with rents captured by elites and used to capture politics.
- GDP and price mistaken for measures of value or well-being, masking harmful or socially useless activity.
- Externalizing costs onto households, society, and the environment, including treating climate change as an "externality."
- Breakdown of the post-war social contract, stoking moral outrage, reciprocity violations, and populist political dysfunction.
- Exclusivity and unfair social contracts leading to rent-seeking, predatory elites, authoritarianism, and long-run innovation decline.

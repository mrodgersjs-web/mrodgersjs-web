The principles of persuasion are&nbsp;
a set of psychological rules to&nbsp;&nbsp;
influence others. Originally there were&nbsp;
six: reciprocity, scarcity, authority,&nbsp;&nbsp;
consistency, liking, and consensus.&nbsp;
Later a 7th principle was added.
The first principle is reciprocity. It&nbsp;
means that if you give something to someone,&nbsp;&nbsp;
it makes them more open to being influenced&nbsp;
by you. This is because we don’t like to&nbsp;&nbsp;
feel that we owe someone and so we want to&nbsp;
return the favor to clear our social debt.
The second is scarcity. When people&nbsp;
perceive something as rare or limited,&nbsp;&nbsp;
they are more susceptible to persuasion. This is&nbsp;
driven by our desire to possess what others value,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and the fear of missing out that&nbsp;
prompts us to make decisions.
The third principle is authority. We&nbsp;
often trust powerful people and their&nbsp;&nbsp;
perceived expertise. We seek their guidance,&nbsp;
particularly in situations of uncertainty,&nbsp;&nbsp;
when we lack direction and need help from someone&nbsp;
else to make a difficult decision on our behalf.
Fourth is consistency. We are more likely&nbsp;
to do something if it's consistent with&nbsp;&nbsp;
past behavior. This is because we align our&nbsp;
actions with expectations. To influence someone,&nbsp;&nbsp;
ask for small commitments&nbsp;
first and larger ones later.
The fifth is liking. People are more willing&nbsp;
to say "yes" to those they are fond of. To&nbsp;&nbsp;
make people like you, find common interests,&nbsp;
be friendly, and give genuine compliments.
The sixth is consensus. Humans are inherently&nbsp;
social. They want to conform to the group.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Social proof can therefore persuade people&nbsp;
to commit to something, they otherwise doubt.
Psychologist Robert Cialdini, who came up with&nbsp;
the 6 rules, later added another one — unity.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Accordingly, people are more influenced by those&nbsp;
who share their beliefs, values, and experiences.
So do you think you understood&nbsp;
the principles? Then meet Jane,&nbsp;&nbsp;
a young entrepreneur who, after learning about&nbsp;
them, established a successful lemonade business.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Follow her story and try to tell us which&nbsp;
principles she uses and in what sequence.
To start her business, Jane first&nbsp;
needs to get funding. To do so,&nbsp;&nbsp;
she offers free samples to potential investors.&nbsp;
When she later presents her business idea,&nbsp;&nbsp;
those who tried her product are&nbsp;
now more likely to hear her out.
On her first week of business, Jane produces just&nbsp;&nbsp;
enough to satisfy some customers but&nbsp;
pretends to run out before everyone&nbsp;&nbsp;
gets what they lined up for. By doing&nbsp;
so, she creates a sense of exclusivity.
Jane then starts to showcase her top&nbsp;
customers, encourages positive reviews,&nbsp;&nbsp;
invites key opinion leaders to visit her shop,&nbsp;
and hosts a local band to play on Sundays.
Once her products are popular, customers can’t get&nbsp;
enough of them. To ensure she’s not losing anyone,&nbsp;&nbsp;
she distributes paper cups to those&nbsp;
in line. Those that take the cup,&nbsp;&nbsp;
are then more likely to&nbsp;
wait for what they came for.
Despite all that success, when Jane tries&nbsp;
to open a savings account, she gets laughed&nbsp;&nbsp;
at. So she partners up with her supplier, to&nbsp;
appear trustworthy enough to get the job done.
To strengthen her business, Jane listens to her&nbsp;
customers and shows interest in the community. As&nbsp;&nbsp;
a result, people will feel more connected to her&nbsp;
company and stay loyal despite the competition.
Now tell us in the comments below which of the&nbsp;
7 principles Jane applied and in what order!
Note that if these ideas aren’t&nbsp;
enough to get the job done,&nbsp;&nbsp;
here is one last piece of Cialdini’s advice:&nbsp;
“When we ask someone to do us a favor we will&nbsp;&nbsp;
be more successful if we provide a reason.&nbsp;
Even if the reason is utterly unreasonable.”

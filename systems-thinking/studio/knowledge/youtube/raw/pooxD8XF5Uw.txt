8. Emergence
According to Wikipedia, emergence is created as a process in which larger entities, patterns, and regularities are woven
through interactions between smaller or simpler entities that themselves are not characterized by the new qualities that emerged. In the
previous section, we discussed how synergistic relationships give rise to the phenomenon in which two or more components that
together produce an output or effect greater than the output or effect of each of the components separately.
This process, in which the connection between components creates something greater than the sum of the parts, is called "emergence."
So when we talked about synergies, we simply noted that the combined effect is greater than the effect of the parts each on its own.
However, the idea of ​​"emergence" implies that what emerges from synergistic relationships
is not only quantitatively different, but actually fundamentally different. In
other words, none of the components that contributed to the emergence of the new phenomenon retain its properties when standing alone.
There are many examples of this, but perhaps the simplest of all is the example of water.
Water is composed of hydrogen and oxygen atoms.  Neither of these two substances that make up the system has the property or quality of "wetness,"
but when we combine them, we get a substance called "water" that possesses the property of wetness.
This property of wetness emerged from the interrelationships of the system's components, and it exists only at the system level.
Another example of an emergence that is often cited is life itself.
Biological systems, such as the plant cell, are composed of groups of abiotic molecules, none
of which individually has the property of "life."
But it is the particular way in which molecules are arranged into structures and processes that enables the emergence of a living system as a real thing.
The world is full of examples of emergence that could be mentioned - from ant colonies to galaxies and civilizations, but these are all types of structures,
while emergence is more the process itself.
These systems are the products of an emergence process that has evolved to produce a system with two completely different levels of quality.
Emergence, therefore, is a process by which a system develops or, in other words, grows
during the process. Various components interact, synchronize to produce synergies,
and from this emerge new phenomena that did not exist before.
In order to produce a new phenomenon that is fundamentally different in quality, the system must undergo a change, which we call a "phase transition." A phase
transition is, generally, a rapid and accelerated period of time in the process of system development in which the
boundaries and definitions with which we defined the system may change quantitatively.
Again, there are many examples of this.  Like the phase transition from solid to liquid when a substance changes when heated,
but perhaps the most dramatic example is the metamorphosis of the butterfly. A phase
transition from caterpillar to adult.
Not only does the morphology of the system change, many of the definitions by which we defined the initial system change,
from before the phase transition to after it, which we actually give a whole new name to.
This illustration helps bring us to another important theme in emergence - and that is the distinction between what is called strong emergence and weak emergence.
Weak emergence describes how the phenomenon can be traced back to the individual components.
That is, we can observe and predict emergence to a higher level only by looking at separate parts of the
object. Strong emergence, also known as "irreducible emergence," states that this phenomenon cannot be broken down into its individual components.
Instead, an attempt to retrace the traces of the emergence phenomenon leads back to the interrelationships between multiple components
so that it is, literally,  Indeed, it is not predictable by looking at the different components separately.
Consciousness is usually mentioned as an example of a strong inference.
It seems that without prior understanding or experience of what exactly consciousness is,
it would be almost impossible to understand the vast and complex system that is human consciousness,
by analyzing the properties of the relatively simple neurons that make up consciousness. The
distinction between strong inference and weak inference can also be formulated using the language of information theory, as
weak inferences are fundamentally computable. In other words,
if we had sufficient information about how the system is composed, we could put it into a simulation,
unlike strong inferences, where no amount of information can predict or formulate the final product of a process that has reached its end. The discussion of
strong and weak inferences leads us to another important idea in systems theory, which is "hierarchy,"
the distinction between micro and macro, and between top-down and bottom-up causality, all of which we will discuss in the lecture.  Next

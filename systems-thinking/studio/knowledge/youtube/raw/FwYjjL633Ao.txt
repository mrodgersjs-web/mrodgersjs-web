Few things puzzled the soldiers and tacticians&nbsp;
of history as much as the question of how to win&nbsp;&nbsp;
a battle. Jean de Bueil, a French soldier&nbsp;
and admiral who lived in the 15th century,&nbsp;&nbsp;
came up with an interesting answer for the Middle&nbsp;
Ages: To win a battle, it was essential to stay&nbsp;&nbsp;
on the defensive because the army that attacked&nbsp;
was always at a disadvantage. Let’s take a closer&nbsp;&nbsp;
look at what he meant by this and consider its&nbsp;
practical implications for warfare in the Middle&nbsp;&nbsp;
Ages. To illustrate, let’s start with an example.
Offense and Defense in the Hundred Years’ War&nbsp;
On October 25, 1415, two large armies faced each&nbsp;
other on a freshly plowed field near the small&nbsp;&nbsp;
town of Agincourt. After a long wait, the English&nbsp;
advanced, dug in about 250 m from the French line,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and broke the tense silence with a hail of&nbsp;
whirring arrows. The French response was swift:&nbsp;&nbsp;
their cavalry advanced and charged the English&nbsp;
line. However, in charging the enemy, they&nbsp;&nbsp;
encountered a row of sharpened stakes the English&nbsp;
archers had driven into the ground. Faced with&nbsp;&nbsp;
fierce resistance from the English, they turned&nbsp;
back, throwing the infantry behind them into&nbsp;&nbsp;
disarray. The advance slowed and descended into&nbsp;
chaos, giving the English archers time to unleash&nbsp;&nbsp;
a deadly barrage of arrows. Shortly after the&nbsp;
French infantry finally reached the English lines,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the two armies came to a standstill. They fought&nbsp;
for the upper hand until the English archers on&nbsp;&nbsp;
the flanks finally threw themselves into close&nbsp;
combat and sealed the fate of the French.&nbsp;
Jean de Bueil attributes the French defeat at&nbsp;
Agincourt to the fact that the English took a&nbsp;&nbsp;
firm position and forced the French to take&nbsp;
the initiative by pelting them with arrows.&nbsp;&nbsp;
They were forced to either retreat or go on the&nbsp;
tactical offensive and undertake the long march&nbsp;&nbsp;
to the English lines. As we have seen, this was&nbsp;
their undoing. Jean de Bueil cites six other&nbsp;&nbsp;
examples in which attacking armies experienced&nbsp;
similar failures, concluding that “A formation&nbsp;&nbsp;
on foot should never march forward, but&nbsp;
should always hold steady and await its&nbsp;&nbsp;
enemies on foot. ... A force which marches before&nbsp;
another force is defeated, unless God grants it&nbsp;&nbsp;
grace. And, therefore, take up a position as&nbsp;
advantageous as possible, and do it early.”&nbsp;
In addition to de Bueil’s examples, numerous&nbsp;
battles confirm that armies that remained on the&nbsp;&nbsp;
defensive had an advantage.Ex Some of the most&nbsp;
striking examples can be found in the Hundred&nbsp;&nbsp;
Years War and the Hussite Wars. The Hussite armies&nbsp;
were masters of the defense. They repeatedly&nbsp;&nbsp;
defeated superior armies of knights thanks to&nbsp;
their wagon forts. They rarely took the offensive,&nbsp;&nbsp;
instead waiting until their opponents attacked,&nbsp;
weakening them, and launching a counterattack&nbsp;&nbsp;
at the first sign of vulnerability. These&nbsp;
examples show that de Bueil was onto something,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which is why most modern historians believe the&nbsp;
defending side did indeed have an advantage.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Clifford Rogers, for example, discussed this&nbsp;
extensively already in a paper back in 1996.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Other tacticians have also recognized this. For&nbsp;
example, Carl von Clausewitz wrote, “As we shall&nbsp;&nbsp;
show … defense is a stronger form of fighting&nbsp;
than attack.” But why was it so advantageous&nbsp;&nbsp;
to remain on the defensive in the first place?
Two factors were decisive. Usually, the defending&nbsp;&nbsp;
side had control of the battlefield.&nbsp;
The defender could await the attacker&nbsp;&nbsp;
in an advantageous position, like on a hill,&nbsp;
behind a bottleneck, or in front of marshy&nbsp;&nbsp;
terrain. The advantage of choosing the battlefield&nbsp;
was sometimes so great that the attackers saw no&nbsp;&nbsp;
point in fighting and instead tried to lure&nbsp;
the defenders into a more balanced situation&nbsp;&nbsp;
or avoided the confrontation altogether.
The second decisive advantage of playing&nbsp;&nbsp;
defense was that it allowed forces to hold their&nbsp;
position. Medieval infantry typically fought in&nbsp;&nbsp;
close formations because presenting a cohesive&nbsp;
front of spears to the enemy gave them the best&nbsp;&nbsp;
chance in battle. The problem was that soldiers&nbsp;
who did not regularly train together found it&nbsp;&nbsp;
difficult to move in formation. Even the slightest&nbsp;
obstacle could become a problem. Rivers, hills, or&nbsp;&nbsp;
field fortifications were almost insurmountable,&nbsp;
and even a bush, a large rock, or incoming fire&nbsp;&nbsp;
could cause disorganization. And the consequences&nbsp;
of a disrupted formation were severe. Christine de&nbsp;&nbsp;
Pisan, one of the first French female writers,&nbsp;
summarizes them as follows: “Two great evils,&nbsp;&nbsp;
[says Vegetius,] can follow from a disordered&nbsp;
formation: one is that the enemies can more easily&nbsp;&nbsp;
break into it; the other is that the formations&nbsp;
may be so compressed that they cannot fight. Thus&nbsp;&nbsp;
it is necessary to keep a formation in ranks,&nbsp;
and tight and joined together like a wall.”&nbsp;
Contemporary reports often attribute defeats&nbsp;
to the collapse of formations, as seen with&nbsp;&nbsp;
the French at the Battle of Agincourt. Both of&nbsp;
Christine de Pisan’s “great evils” occurred there:&nbsp;&nbsp;
First, the muddy ground and the French&nbsp;
cavalry caused the advancing infantry to&nbsp;&nbsp;
become disorganized, making them vulnerable to&nbsp;
English arrows. Then, the 800 or so men fighting&nbsp;&nbsp;
the English on the front line became trapped&nbsp;
between the enemy and their comrades. Standing&nbsp;&nbsp;
on the battlefield today, it's easy to see why the&nbsp;
muddy ground made the French advance so difficult&nbsp;&nbsp;
in 1415. The rain that had come down prior to&nbsp;
the battle collected at the bottom of the slope,&nbsp;&nbsp;
where the English had positioned themselves,&nbsp;
turning the battlefield into a muddy quagmire,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which severely hampered the knights' ability to&nbsp;
maneuver. Seeing the battlefield with your own&nbsp;&nbsp;
eyes really gives you a different understanding&nbsp;
of what happened on that day in 1415. When I&nbsp;&nbsp;

During battles like Agincourt, medieval soldiers&nbsp;&nbsp;
often found themselves trapped between the enemy&nbsp;
and their own comrades. Medieval authors sometimes&nbsp;&nbsp;
refer to this surprisingly common phenomenon&nbsp;
as “the press.” Soldiers caught in the press&nbsp;&nbsp;
struggled to fight due to the immense pressure.&nbsp;
In some cases, they were even crushed to death.&nbsp;
So, the key takeaway from all this is that if&nbsp;
an infantry formation marched toward the enemy,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the chance of it falling into disorder was very&nbsp;
high, whereas an army that remained stationary&nbsp;&nbsp;
could hold its formation easily. If a formation&nbsp;
fell into disorder, it was at a significant&nbsp;&nbsp;
disadvantage compared to an orderly opponent,&nbsp;
and the chances of defeat were high. As a result,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the defending side had a clear advantage.
Of course, the soldiers and tacticians of the&nbsp;&nbsp;
time knew this, too. In practice, this imbalance&nbsp;
between offense and defense led to attempts to&nbsp;&nbsp;
put the enemy in a position where they were forced&nbsp;
to take the initiative while remaining in a safe&nbsp;&nbsp;
defensive position. This raises the question of&nbsp;
why medieval generals would risk an offensive&nbsp;&nbsp;
war at all, considering that the attacker was&nbsp;
at an inherent disadvantage. The answer may&nbsp;&nbsp;
sound simple, but it’s not: You cannot achieve&nbsp;
an offensive aim with a defensive strategy.&nbsp;
To wage a successful offensive war and achieve&nbsp;
their objectives, attackers had to take to the&nbsp;&nbsp;
offensive strategically while ensuring they&nbsp;
held the tactical defensive position during&nbsp;&nbsp;
actual engagements. Or, to put it more simply,&nbsp;
the trick was to march into enemy territory but&nbsp;&nbsp;
get the defender to attack first. The big&nbsp;
question, then, was how to accomplish this,&nbsp;&nbsp;
given that the other side also knew that striking&nbsp;
first would put it at a disadvantage. Medieval&nbsp;&nbsp;
military leaders came up with solutions that&nbsp;
can be simplified into three categories:&nbsp;&nbsp;
[1] provoke the enemy by insulting his honor, [2]&nbsp;
pressure the enemy by attacking an important city,&nbsp;&nbsp;
or [3] pressure the enemy by plundering.
Attack on the enemy’s honor: The first&nbsp;&nbsp;
option was to provoke the enemy by attacking his&nbsp;
honor, for example, by insulting or threatening&nbsp;&nbsp;
him. The simplest and most common form of such a&nbsp;
provocation was to invade foreign territory. This&nbsp;&nbsp;
literal crossing of boundaries was a challenge as&nbsp;
clear as a thrown gauntlet. The territorial lord&nbsp;&nbsp;
of the invaded area had to react to protect&nbsp;
his honor. From a military point of view,&nbsp;&nbsp;
it was often wiser to ignore the challenge,&nbsp;
but this also carried a significant risk.&nbsp;&nbsp;
When Edward III of England led an army&nbsp;
into France for the first time in 1339,&nbsp;&nbsp;
at the beginning of the Hundred Years’ War, Philip&nbsp;
VI refrained from attacking the invading army.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Although this was a sound decision militarily,&nbsp;
many of his compatriots considered it cowardice,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and Philip’s reputation was severely damaged. Many&nbsp;
French knights accused him of acting like a fox&nbsp;&nbsp;
(renardie in French), calling him dishonorable and&nbsp;
deceitful, and publicly showing their disapproval&nbsp;&nbsp;
by wearing caps made of fox fur. They rejected a&nbsp;
ruler who accepted such an insult with impunity.&nbsp;
The effect of a provocation was reinforced&nbsp;
by symbolic gestures. For example, in 1339,&nbsp;&nbsp;
Edward III deliberately entered France on&nbsp;
October 9, the feast day of Saint Denis of&nbsp;&nbsp;
Paris, the patron saint of the French&nbsp;
monarchs. To ensure the enemy could not&nbsp;&nbsp;
pretend ignorance of the challenge, he&nbsp;
was informed by letter simultaneously.&nbsp;&nbsp;
These letters could appear very polite on the&nbsp;
surface while carrying a clear undertone of threat&nbsp;&nbsp;
or insult. Their most important aim was to make it&nbsp;
clear right from the outset that the attacker was&nbsp;&nbsp;
prepared to give the defender an opportunity&nbsp;
to restore his honor through battle. So,&nbsp;&nbsp;
they made it clear that it was up to the offended&nbsp;
party to take the initiative, suggesting that&nbsp;&nbsp;
the lord of the invaded area should abandon&nbsp;
the tactical defense to protect his honor,&nbsp;&nbsp;
despite being in a defensive position. Henry of&nbsp;
Grosmont, the first Duke of Lancaster, formulated&nbsp;&nbsp;
such a message as follows: “My lord [Edward of&nbsp;
Woodstock] answered that he had come into these&nbsp;&nbsp;
parts in order to do certain business, which he&nbsp;
had now well completed, thanks be to God, and was&nbsp;&nbsp;
now returning from where he had done it; and, if&nbsp;
the said King Jean of France wanted to disturb him&nbsp;&nbsp;
on his way, he would be ready to meet him.”
The problem with provocations was that they&nbsp;&nbsp;
only worked if the offended ruler’s sense of&nbsp;
honor caused him to ignore the voice of reason.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Rulers who managed to keep their cool would&nbsp;
usually maintain their advantageous defensive&nbsp;&nbsp;
position. Therefore, the attackers tried to frame&nbsp;
challenges in a way that refusal would cause the&nbsp;&nbsp;
greatest damage to their enemy’s honor, such as&nbsp;
by portraying him as a coward. A cleverly worded&nbsp;&nbsp;
letter left the defender little choice but to&nbsp;
react, as adopting a passive stance could do more&nbsp;&nbsp;
harm than fighting a battle from a poor position.
Siege as leverage: The second option was to&nbsp;&nbsp;
pressure the enemy by besieging a symbolically&nbsp;
or economically important city. The threat of&nbsp;&nbsp;
losing a center of economy and power forced the&nbsp;
defender to go on the offensive. Abandoning the&nbsp;&nbsp;
city or waiting too long to relief it could be&nbsp;
interpreted as a failure to fulfill his duty as a&nbsp;&nbsp;
ruler or simply incompetence. For their part, the&nbsp;
besiegers had all the time in the world and could&nbsp;&nbsp;
wait for the anticipated attack in a position of&nbsp;
their choice. This approach was used by Edward&nbsp;&nbsp;
III when he besieged Berwick in 1333. The siege&nbsp;
forced the Scots to abandon their strong position&nbsp;&nbsp;
and attack his troops at Halidon Hill. They had&nbsp;
to approach the English across a marsh and were&nbsp;&nbsp;
mercilessly shot down by their longbowmen.
Putting pressure on an enemy in this way was&nbsp;&nbsp;
expensive and time-consuming. Important&nbsp;
cities were usually well fortified,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and the maneuver only worked if the attacker&nbsp;
succeeded in bringing the city to its knees.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Only when the garrison’s situation became dire&nbsp;
and the city was on the verge of being conquered&nbsp;&nbsp;
did the pressure to come to its aid intensify.&nbsp;
It was only then that it became worthwhile for a&nbsp;&nbsp;
ruler to take the risk of attacking a besieging&nbsp;
force. Henry V had to besiege the French town&nbsp;&nbsp;
of Rouen for over six months until this point was&nbsp;
reached—six months that cost him a great deal and&nbsp;&nbsp;
were almost as arduous for his men as they were&nbsp;
for the people in the city. Much to his chagrin,&nbsp;&nbsp;
he then discovered that in some cases, a sovereign&nbsp;
preferred to give up a city rather than risk a&nbsp;&nbsp;
battle. The French did raise an army to liberate&nbsp;
Rouen but ultimately decided not to attack.&nbsp;
Pushing an opponent onto the offensive&nbsp;
in this way required a certain amount of&nbsp;&nbsp;
sensitivity. Sometimes, pressure alone was not&nbsp;
enough. When Edward III laid siege to Calais,&nbsp;&nbsp;
Philip VI did not want to risk a battle, even&nbsp;
though the city was strategically critical&nbsp;&nbsp;
and on the verge of capitulation. Edward then&nbsp;
tried to lure him out of cover by offering to&nbsp;&nbsp;
fight on a battlefield chosen by a committee of&nbsp;
knights from both sides. Philip refused because&nbsp;&nbsp;
he knew that even on an open field, he would be&nbsp;
the one under pressure to attack and bring about a&nbsp;&nbsp;
decision. As a result, he would still have had to&nbsp;
take the offensive, leading to an unfair battle.&nbsp;
This example aptly illustrates the interplay&nbsp;
between strategic war aims and tactical necessity.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Strategically, Edward needed a battle to achieve&nbsp;
his goals. Tactically, he wanted to fight it on&nbsp;&nbsp;
the defensive at all costs. To achieve both&nbsp;
objectives, he was even willing to give up his&nbsp;&nbsp;
strong position. Philip, on the other hand, made&nbsp;
the only sensible decision from a tactical point&nbsp;&nbsp;
of view: not accepting the offer.
Plundering as leverage:&nbsp;&nbsp;
The third way to get a defending army to take&nbsp;
the initiative was plundering. One of the most&nbsp;&nbsp;
important methods of warfare in the Middle Ages&nbsp;
was the chevauchée, where mounted units plundered&nbsp;&nbsp;
the enemy’s territory. These raids were very&nbsp;
efficient because they not only destroyed the&nbsp;&nbsp;
enemy’s resources but also appropriated them&nbsp;
for the attacker’s cause. Most importantly,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the population and economy of the area suffered,&nbsp;
which created political pressure on the ruler. At&nbsp;&nbsp;
some point, this pressure was so great that he&nbsp;
either had to give in to the attackers’ demands&nbsp;&nbsp;
or confront them in battle. This was the case, for&nbsp;
example, at the Battle of Crécy. When Edward III’s&nbsp;&nbsp;
troops plundered France in 1346, Philip VI marched&nbsp;
against them. The English retreated to the north,&nbsp;&nbsp;
bypassed a blockade at the river Somme, and&nbsp;
awaited their pursuers on a battlefield where&nbsp;&nbsp;
they had a clear advantage. Philip attacked&nbsp;
anyway, and the English longbowmen shot&nbsp;&nbsp;
several waves of his Genoese crossbowmen and&nbsp;
French knights to pieces. Edward had managed to&nbsp;&nbsp;
get the French to take the tactical initiative.&nbsp;
They went on the offensive and lost the battle.&nbsp;
Of course, the side on the defensive did not&nbsp;
always win—there were numerous other decisive&nbsp;&nbsp;
factors. However, the advantage of the tactical&nbsp;
defense was so great that it was an important part&nbsp;&nbsp;
of medieval warfare to try to put the other side&nbsp;
in an offensive position. The attacker aimed to&nbsp;&nbsp;
create a situation where the defender was forced&nbsp;
to act by violating his honor, threatening a city,&nbsp;&nbsp;
or inflicting lasting damage to the economy and&nbsp;
reputation of the sovereign through plundering.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The defender sought to prevent this: he wanted&nbsp;
the attacker to take the offensive tactically&nbsp;&nbsp;
to achieve his goals. In many cases, this was&nbsp;
the more challenging task because the defender&nbsp;&nbsp;
had to endure plundering and losses until&nbsp;
the attacker gave up his plan or took the&nbsp;&nbsp;
tactical initiative. Invaders usually only did&nbsp;
this if they ran out of supplies or something&nbsp;&nbsp;
else forced their hand. If a defender held out&nbsp;
long enough, every invader would eventually&nbsp;&nbsp;
defeat himself, but holding out wasn’t easy.
The back and forth between offense and defense&nbsp;&nbsp;
was a core element of medieval warfare. Therefore,&nbsp;
Jean de Bueil was at least partly right: putting&nbsp;&nbsp;
yourself in a tactically defensive position or&nbsp;
forcing your opponent in an offensive position was&nbsp;&nbsp;
decisive for your chances in battle—and not only&nbsp;
in the Hundred Years’ War. The imbalance between&nbsp;&nbsp;
offense and defense played an essential role in&nbsp;
the thinking of great tacticians (like Carl von&nbsp;&nbsp;
Clausewitz) throughout history. The Chinese&nbsp;
strategist Sun Tsu wrote as early as 500 BC:&nbsp;&nbsp;
“Invincibility lies in the defense; the&nbsp;
possibility of victory in the attack.”

Imagine you need surgery. So you compare
two doctors in your area. Dr. Jones has
an 86% success rate and Dr. Smith only
has about 79%. Easy choice, right? Well,
not necessarily. Let's say you go with
Dr. Jones. You're being rational and
you're following the numbers, but
something doesn't sit right. So you ask
the hospital for more detail. What if
their outcomes differ by the type of
patient they treat? When you look at
just the high-risisk patients, Dr. Smith
actually has a 75% success rate compared
to Dr. Jones at 70%.
Okay, so Smith's better at complicated
cases. Then you check the low-risk
patients. Smith is at 95% and Jones is
at 90%. So Smith's better there, too.
Now hold on a second. Dr. Smith is
better for high-risisk patients but then
also better for low-risk patients but
somehow has a worse overall record. How
is that even possible? Here's what's
hiding in the numbers. Dr. Smith is a
specialist. She's the surgeon they call
when things get really complicated. So
out of her last 100 patients, 80 of them
were high-risisk cases and only 20 were
routine. Let's do the math on that.
Those 20 low-risk patients at 95%
success gives her 19 survivors. Those 80
high-risisk patients at 75% gives her 60
more. Add them together and you get 79
out of 100.
Now, Dr. Jones handles mostly
straightforward surgeries. Out of his
100 patients, 80 were low risk and only
20 were high risk. So those 80 lowrisk
cases at 90% gets him 72 survivors.
The 20 high-risisk cases at 70% adds 14
more. Total that up and you get 86 out
of 100. See what happened there? Dr.
Jones looks better overall, not because
he's a better surgeon, but because he's
operating on easier cases. Dr. Smith
looks worse overall, not because she's
less skilled, but because she's the one
taking on the really difficult patients.
This phenomenon is known as Simpsons
paradox. The same set of data appears to
show opposite trends depending on how
you group it. In fact, you can have
variables that are positively correlated
within multiple subgroups, but
negatively correlated when you combine
them all together.
Each group shows the same pattern, but
the aggregate flips it completely.
On first glance, the overall numbers
told you to choose Dr. Jones, but when
you break it down, Dr. Smith is actually
the better choice no matter what kind of
patient you are. And this isn't just a
mathematical curiosity. You see it in
hospital rankings where major referral
centers look worse on paper because they
treat the sickest patients until you
adjust for risk. You see it in clinical
trial data where a treatment looks
weaker overall but performs better once
you account for disease severity or
baseline risk. And you see it in
university admissions like the famous UC
Berkeley case where the aggregate
numbers told a completely different
story than the departmental breakdowns.
The problem is a lurking variable, a
hidden factor that completely changes
what the data is telling you. In our
case, it's the proportion of high-risk
versus low-risk patients each surgeon
treats. Because sometimes the better
choice looks worse on paper.

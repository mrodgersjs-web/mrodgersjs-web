The Roman Empire seemed unbeatable at&nbsp;
its peak - one of the largest empires&nbsp;&nbsp;
the world has ever seen. It fended off&nbsp;
all of its military foes. It built a&nbsp;&nbsp;
complex administrative state that ruled&nbsp;
regions from Egypt, to Spain, to England,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and as far east as modern-day Iraq. At its&nbsp;
height, it seemed like it would rule forever.
And today, it’s a distant memory.
Why did the Roman Empire ultimately fall?&nbsp;
Did it meet an enemy too powerful to fend&nbsp;&nbsp;
off? Was it undone by internal conflict? Or did&nbsp;
it simply stretch itself too thin? The answer&nbsp;&nbsp;
isn’t any one of those - and the empire’s&nbsp;
downfall happened over hundreds of years.
To find the truth of the Roman Empire’s fall,&nbsp;
we have to go back to how it became an empire&nbsp;&nbsp;
in the first place - because initially, it was a&nbsp;
Republic with far less territory. The Republic had&nbsp;&nbsp;
taken over from the previously flawed monarchy,&nbsp;
but the people seemed to be calling out for a&nbsp;&nbsp;
more unified government. One man was more than&nbsp;
willing to answer that call - Julius Caesar,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the man appointed perpetual dictator&nbsp;
of Rome in the mid-first century BCE.
He started consolidating power, becoming&nbsp;
a popular and effective ruler who won many&nbsp;&nbsp;
military campaigns - and many of his rivals&nbsp;
realized he had no intention of giving up&nbsp;&nbsp;
his newfound power. The elites of Rome&nbsp;
assassinated him, setting off one of the&nbsp;&nbsp;
biggest political crises in Roman history.&nbsp;
Civil wars kicked off as many people sought&nbsp;&nbsp;
to claim power - and in the end the wars saw&nbsp;
Octavian, the adopted son of Caesar, take power.
He continued his father’s legacy, conquering&nbsp;
nearby territories including Egypt - ending&nbsp;&nbsp;
that country’s Hellenistic era. Soon, he&nbsp;
was more powerful than his father ever was,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and the Roman senate responded accordingly.&nbsp;
They granted him the title of Augustus,&nbsp;&nbsp;
allowed him to rule in perpetuity, and soon no one&nbsp;
would dare challenge his power. The plotters who&nbsp;&nbsp;
feared Caesar’s role as dictator had accidentally&nbsp;
crafted the rise of Rome’s first Emperor.
And he wouldn’t be the last.
From then, Rome would not only become&nbsp;
another monarchy ruled by a series of&nbsp;&nbsp;
hereditary leaders both good and bad, but&nbsp;
it would develop a hunger for conquest that&nbsp;&nbsp;
would envelop a large part of Europe and&nbsp;
parts of Asia and Africa. While Italy would&nbsp;&nbsp;
continue to serve as the center of the&nbsp;
empire, the other territories would be&nbsp;&nbsp;
organized into provinces - each administered&nbsp;
by a local governor with drastically different&nbsp;&nbsp;
approaches to leadership. The empire would&nbsp;
soon be so large that it would be impossible&nbsp;&nbsp;
for one leader to maintain full control over every&nbsp;
territory, which meant the provinces would still&nbsp;&nbsp;
maintain some level of autonomy - as long as they&nbsp;
paid their taxes and didn’t cause any problems&nbsp;&nbsp;
that would arouse the ire of the Emperor.&nbsp;
And the system seemed to be working - the&nbsp;&nbsp;
empire was stable enough that the next two&nbsp;
centuries became largely known as “Pax Romana”.
But under the surface, trouble was brewing.
During the first century CE, religious conflict&nbsp;
started brewing under the surface. The Roman&nbsp;&nbsp;
Empire largely worshipped the traditional pantheon&nbsp;
- led by Jupiter and heavily tied to the Greek&nbsp;&nbsp;
pantheon led by Zeus. This polytheistic faith&nbsp;
was characterized by ornate temples dedicated&nbsp;&nbsp;
to the individual Gods and a wild lore that&nbsp;
made the Gods seem rather petty and human.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Jupiter’s dalliances with human women, and gods&nbsp;
taking revenge for the most petty grievances,&nbsp;&nbsp;
were both common staples of the faith in those&nbsp;
days - and then came Christianity. Spinning&nbsp;&nbsp;
out of the teachings of the Jewish Rabbi&nbsp;
known as Jesus and his eventual crucifixion&nbsp;&nbsp;
by the Roman authorities, this new Abrahamic&nbsp;
faith was based of the often-persecuted faith&nbsp;&nbsp;
of Judaism - and largely received the same&nbsp;
treatment in the early days. While some of&nbsp;&nbsp;
the more dramatic stories like early Christians&nbsp;
being fed to lions have no historical backing,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the Roman Empire saw new faiths&nbsp;
as a threat to the establishment.
But religions aren’t easy to kill.
The conflict stayed under the radar and&nbsp;
Christianity was mostly a small faith&nbsp;&nbsp;
during those early years, and a wise emperor&nbsp;
would have likely focused on maintaining the&nbsp;&nbsp;
empire’s stability rather than persecuting&nbsp;
heathens. But when you have a monarchy,&nbsp;&nbsp;
you’re often relying on the whims of birth&nbsp;
order and genetics - and in the year 177,&nbsp;&nbsp;
Rome’s luck ran out. The new Emperor, Commodus,&nbsp;
would soon become infamous for this exact reason.
The youngest Emperor ever at the age of&nbsp;
fifteen, his rule was dictated by his&nbsp;&nbsp;
emotions which led to recklessness. A teenager&nbsp;
making bad decisions when given too much power?&nbsp;&nbsp;
Perish the thought! As her was inheriting&nbsp;
the Empire built by many of his predecessors,&nbsp;&nbsp;
it was hard for him to screw up the larger things&nbsp;
- but back in Rome, he wasted no time throwing&nbsp;&nbsp;
things into chaos. He was known for his fickle and&nbsp;
dictatorial leadership style, as well as his love&nbsp;&nbsp;
of the gladiator matches in the Colosseum. He&nbsp;
would even participate in duels himself - which&nbsp;&nbsp;
largely meant anyone who faced off against him&nbsp;
would probably be expected to die quietly. He&nbsp;&nbsp;
built a cult of personality around himself,&nbsp;
and his reign was so chaotic that he would&nbsp;&nbsp;
eventually be immortalized by Joaquin Phoenix as&nbsp;
a villain in the Oscar-winning movie Gladiator.
And his reign ended as it began - with chaos.
Commodus was assassinated by a wrestler in&nbsp;
the Roman baths after fifteen years in charge,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and things would only get more chaotic&nbsp;
from there. The Roman Senate declared&nbsp;&nbsp;
him a public enemy and tore down&nbsp;
his statues, and he was succeeded&nbsp;&nbsp;
by a new Emperor - in what would become&nbsp;
known as the Year of the Five Emperors.
It was 193 CE, and civil war ensued. The next&nbsp;
emperor would be assassinated only three months&nbsp;&nbsp;
after taking office, his successor would&nbsp;
essentially pay off the guards to let him&nbsp;&nbsp;
become Emperor, and he would be ousted and&nbsp;
executed only weeks later. The chaos would&nbsp;&nbsp;
only die down when two emperors, Septimus Severus&nbsp;
and Clodius Albinus, ruled simultaneously despite&nbsp;&nbsp;
both considering the other to be a threat.&nbsp;
Rome’s political climate was in complete chaos.
And soon, people outside would start noticing.
Many of the provinces of Rome had been&nbsp;
semi-autonomous for a long time and&nbsp;&nbsp;
had developed their own cultures. So as the&nbsp;
Roman state spiraled into chaos, they started&nbsp;&nbsp;
to wonder - why not just make it official? As&nbsp;
the Romans struggled through a series of weak,&nbsp;&nbsp;
crazy, and inconsequential Emperors -&nbsp;
often in quick succession - territories&nbsp;&nbsp;
started to break away at an increasing rate.&nbsp;
First came the self-declared Gallic Empire,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which took most of the territory of&nbsp;
modern-day France and England with it.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Then came the Palmyrene Empire, which was led&nbsp;
by Queen Zenobia and took over much of Egypt&nbsp;&nbsp;
and the eastern coast of the Mediterranean -&nbsp;
including the many key sites in the Holy Land.
It was the biggest crisis in Rome’s history&nbsp;
- but it would bring many changes with it.
A series of three Emperors in the third&nbsp;
century would bring this crisis to the&nbsp;&nbsp;
end - but the Roman Empire that emerged from&nbsp;
it would not be the same. First was Aurelian,&nbsp;&nbsp;
who only reigned for five years&nbsp;
but had a huge impact. At the time,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the diminished Roman military was under attack&nbsp;
by Barbarian tribes and internal strife,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and it seemed like the Empire’s days might be&nbsp;
nearing an end. But Aurelian had risen through&nbsp;&nbsp;
the ranks of the military and knew its workings&nbsp;
well, and under his reign he fended off the&nbsp;&nbsp;
attacking tribes and conquered both the Palmyrene&nbsp;
Empire and the Gallic Empire. He surrounded Rome&nbsp;&nbsp;
with mighty walls, abandoned provinces that&nbsp;
were too much trouble to keep, and improved&nbsp;&nbsp;
Rome’s economy. Under his short tenure, Rome&nbsp;
started to look more like it did at its peak.
His successors would build on his work.
Diocletian would reign for twenty years and was&nbsp;
also a military veteran, but most of the military&nbsp;&nbsp;
work had been completed already. He would focus&nbsp;
his efforts on improving the empire’s stability,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which included creating a system of regional&nbsp;
courts that would give the empire more control&nbsp;&nbsp;
over its far-flung provinces. While the empire&nbsp;
had reclaimed the territory lost to the breakaway&nbsp;&nbsp;
provinces, Diocletian started expanding once&nbsp;
again - and by the end of his twenty years,&nbsp;&nbsp;
he became the first Roman emperor to abdicate&nbsp;
his position voluntarily, retiring to his palace.
But his successor might have&nbsp;
had an even bigger impact.
The thirty-one year run of Constantine the&nbsp;
Great would be among the most successful in&nbsp;&nbsp;
the empire’s history. He introduced a new&nbsp;
currency, expanded the empire’s territory,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and reorganized the army to be more effective.&nbsp;
But his most significant change was his conversion&nbsp;&nbsp;
to Christianity - reportedly spurred on by him&nbsp;
seeing a cross in the sky. As the Roman Emperor&nbsp;&nbsp;
converted, so did many of the citizens of Rome,&nbsp;
igniting Christianity’s rise to becoming a world&nbsp;&nbsp;
power religion. At the end of his reign,&nbsp;
the empire’s territory was near its peak,&nbsp;&nbsp;
expanding as far east as Byzantium. After his&nbsp;
death, Constantine left the empire to his sons,&nbsp;&nbsp;
starting a new dynasty which would rule over&nbsp;
Rome. The Roman Empire looked as healthy as ever,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and many people assumed that this was&nbsp;
the beginning of a new golden age.
So how could things go so very wrong?
The year was 337 CE when Constantine passed&nbsp;
away, and while Constantine’s reign had been&nbsp;&nbsp;
full of successes, there was trouble lurking&nbsp;
under the surface. His embrace of Christianity&nbsp;&nbsp;
had given the Church massive power in Rome, and&nbsp;
although many supported him in this decision,&nbsp;&nbsp;
there were some who didn’t wish for the empire to&nbsp;
fall under the grasps of Christianity. Enemies of&nbsp;&nbsp;
the state were executed, and their estates&nbsp;
were given to those he favored. The military&nbsp;&nbsp;
was paid well, but soon the soldiers seemed&nbsp;
to be engaging in blatant corruption. But the&nbsp;&nbsp;
empire was still the greatest power in the world,&nbsp;
and it was hard to imagine anyone challenging it.
But empire’s aren’t immune from internal strife.
In the aftermath of the death of Constantine,&nbsp;
the throne of the empire was once again open&nbsp;&nbsp;
for contest, and this era led to many conflicts&nbsp;
over who would take control. His sons initially&nbsp;&nbsp;
ruled jointly, but chaos would follow and&nbsp;
Rome would be plunged into civil war. Many&nbsp;&nbsp;
territories were taken over by local leaders,&nbsp;
with territories going back and forth at the&nbsp;&nbsp;
whims of military victories. Despite this,&nbsp;
the core of the empire was unchallenged,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and by 379 a new emperor named Theodosius had&nbsp;
risen. A proven general, he would become one&nbsp;&nbsp;
of Rome’s greatest military leaders and&nbsp;
would put an end to this era of strife.
But far greater threats were&nbsp;
brewing beyond the borders of Rome.
As far-reaching as Rome’s borders were at the&nbsp;
time, it was hard to believe that a nomadic people&nbsp;&nbsp;
from a continent away would throw the whole thing&nbsp;
into chaos. But the Huns were no ordinary nomads.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Fierce warriors, they had been making inroads into&nbsp;
Asia and Eastern Europe - and where they went,&nbsp;&nbsp;
people fled. Their mass takeover of territory led&nbsp;
the local Gothic tribes to flee towards the Roman&nbsp;&nbsp;
Empire. Suddenly, the Romans had a huge influx&nbsp;
of barbarian warriors fleeing across their border&nbsp;&nbsp;
- and Rome’s lack of management in their border&nbsp;
states came back to haunt them. A well-organized&nbsp;&nbsp;
government may have been able to resettle these&nbsp;
new residents and incorporate them into the&nbsp;&nbsp;
Roman state - even using their warrior smarts&nbsp;
to reinvigorate the military. Unfortunately,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the corrupt local officials instead chose to&nbsp;
exploit them, assuming they weren’t a threat.
They would be proven very wrong.
Now the Romans had a large number of outside&nbsp;
warriors within their borders. These guests&nbsp;&nbsp;
had seen Roman hospitality was lacking, and&nbsp;
they quickly took up arms. They were joined&nbsp;&nbsp;
by other tribesmen heading over the&nbsp;
border, and what started as a refugee&nbsp;&nbsp;
crisis quickly turned into a guerilla war. While&nbsp;
Rome had a superior military force, it was also&nbsp;&nbsp;
spread out and concentrated in the cities, and the&nbsp;
forces that were sent to confront the Goths found&nbsp;&nbsp;
the invaders fought much harder than expected. The&nbsp;
elites felt very little of this crisis - they were&nbsp;&nbsp;
kept safe in their walled cities - but across&nbsp;
the massive expanses of the Roman countryside,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the Goths quickly gained territory and claimed the&nbsp;
land of former Roman subjects across the Balkans.
And Theodosius may have neglected&nbsp;
this problem a bit too much.
The Emperor may have restored Rome’s&nbsp;
military in some ways, but the coffers&nbsp;&nbsp;
were still looking kind of bare after decades&nbsp;
of patronage under his predecessors. He raised&nbsp;&nbsp;
taxes to confront the barbarians - and was met&nbsp;
with rebellions from the people he ruled over.&nbsp;&nbsp;
He also cracked down on other faiths, doubling&nbsp;
down on Constantine’s embrace of Christianity,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and even persecuted believers of other&nbsp;
variants of Christianity. Soon he was&nbsp;&nbsp;
facing new rebellions in the west seeking to&nbsp;
end his rule, with rebel leader Magnus Maximus&nbsp;&nbsp;
declaring himself emperor in 383 and taking over&nbsp;
the territory of Gaul. This led to significant&nbsp;&nbsp;
losses in territory and troops until Maximus&nbsp;
made a failed attempt at defeating Rome itself&nbsp;&nbsp;
and was ultimately defeated in 388. Rome once&nbsp;
again became the greatest power on the planet.
But soon this would all change.
By 395 CE, Theodosius I would die, leaving behind&nbsp;
him a weakened army and no clear successor. His&nbsp;&nbsp;
two sons would each claim the empire, but neither&nbsp;
was a capable leader - leaving their ministers to&nbsp;&nbsp;
essentially take charge of the situation. Rather&nbsp;
than go to another brutal civil war only a decade&nbsp;&nbsp;
after the last, the two halves of the empire&nbsp;
essentially went their own separate ways - one&nbsp;&nbsp;
emperor, Arcadius, took the eastern Roman Empire,&nbsp;
which would become known as the Byzantine Empire&nbsp;&nbsp;
in the future, while the other son, Honorius,&nbsp;
would take the western part including Rome itself.&nbsp;&nbsp;
It was the biggest loss of territory for Rome yet&nbsp;
- and this time, there would be no going back.
The Byzantine Empire would endure, lasting&nbsp;
for another thousand years with a power&nbsp;&nbsp;
base in the city of Constantinople - but&nbsp;
it would never reach the world-defining&nbsp;&nbsp;
power of the Roman Empire. As for Rome, it&nbsp;
still ruled much of Europe - but its days&nbsp;&nbsp;
were drawing short. Without the massive&nbsp;
territory now owned by the Byzantines,&nbsp;&nbsp;
administering the empire was much more&nbsp;
manageable - but it also meant the massive&nbsp;&nbsp;
barbarian invasions were harder to control. The&nbsp;
leader of the Huns, Attila, was one of the most&nbsp;&nbsp;
feared military leaders of all time - and now&nbsp;
he placed his full attention on taking Rome.
It would be a death of a thousand&nbsp;
cuts for the Roman Empire.
Over the next eighty years, the Roman Empire dealt&nbsp;
with brutal attacks from the Huns on their fringes&nbsp;&nbsp;
and the Gothic population throughout the empire.&nbsp;
The Roman military was a shadow of what it used&nbsp;&nbsp;
to be, and it frequently found itself in retreat.&nbsp;
The territory of the empire shrunk piece by piece,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and Rome started to look less like a powerful&nbsp;
empire and more like a city in decline. Some&nbsp;&nbsp;
leaders would manage to restore the military’s&nbsp;
strength, but none would get it back to the level&nbsp;&nbsp;
where it could reclaim significant amounts of&nbsp;
territory. The empire would limp along until 476,&nbsp;&nbsp;
where one ambitious king decided it&nbsp;
was time to strike the final blow.
His name was Odoacer - and the&nbsp;
mostly-forgotten figure may have&nbsp;&nbsp;
been one of the most important people in history.
The last Emperor of Rome, Romulus&nbsp;
Augustulus, had a mighty name but&nbsp;&nbsp;
was only a sixteen year old boy when Odoacer&nbsp;
invaded. The barbarian king, surprisingly,&nbsp;&nbsp;
dealt kindly with the young king as he sacked&nbsp;
Rome - accepting his surrender and allowing the&nbsp;&nbsp;
boy to remain alive. He sent him away to live&nbsp;
with relatives, even granting him a pension,&nbsp;&nbsp;
before declaring himself the ruler of Italy.&nbsp;
Probably the most dramatic firing from a first&nbsp;&nbsp;
job a teenager had ever experienced! But now, the&nbsp;
Roman Empire was over - or was it? That depends on&nbsp;&nbsp;
how you define it, because Odoacer now ruled what&nbsp;
was left of it - but he was considered a foreign&nbsp;&nbsp;
occupier. The Roman Senate didn’t consider him a&nbsp;
legitimate Roman ruler and instead transferred the&nbsp;&nbsp;
seal of Rome to the eastern empire. And so the&nbsp;
greatest empire in history passed into legend.
Or did it?
The Roman empire as a government was no more,&nbsp;
but its greatest institution still remained - the&nbsp;&nbsp;
Roman Catholic Church. It was still in its&nbsp;
infancy, less than five hundred years old,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but its faith had spread very quickly and would&nbsp;
soon grow its roots in most of Europe. As the&nbsp;&nbsp;
Church spread, so did its power - and for&nbsp;
the best part of the next thousand years,&nbsp;&nbsp;
it would be the de facto government in much&nbsp;
of Europe. Even kings would answer to it,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and few would challenge the Pope’s&nbsp;
power until Henry VIII would start&nbsp;&nbsp;
his own religion when the Church&nbsp;
wouldn’t let him get divorced. So,&nbsp;&nbsp;
while the Roman Empire did not control Europe&nbsp;
anymore, the Church’s legacy far outlive it.
But why did the empire actually fall?
There is no one reason why the Roman Empire&nbsp;
crumbled, but many factors played a role - few&nbsp;&nbsp;
bigger than the collapse of its military. Rome’s&nbsp;
army was once the most powerful in the world,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and it attracted the best with good pay. But as&nbsp;
the empire’s coffers declined, it soon struggled&nbsp;&nbsp;
to recruit new troops effectively. This led&nbsp;
to Emperors filing out the ranks with foreign&nbsp;&nbsp;
mercenaries, who neither had the skills nor the&nbsp;
loyalty of previous armies. Many even came from&nbsp;&nbsp;
the invading armies they were fighting, and the&nbsp;
effectiveness of their fighting forces suffered.
They tried to make up for the personnel&nbsp;
problem - but that just made things worse.
Many emperors consistently overspent on&nbsp;
the military, but that didn’t make it any&nbsp;&nbsp;
easier to manage. The huge territory they&nbsp;
had to govern made it near impossible to&nbsp;&nbsp;
communicate effectively, and with no e-mail&nbsp;
or teams groups to share important memos,&nbsp;&nbsp;
it was often a matter of weeks or months before&nbsp;
key changes in policy were communicated. As the&nbsp;&nbsp;
empire got larger and larger, this became a&nbsp;
bigger problem - and soon the empire was more&nbsp;&nbsp;
focused on protecting its key cities than&nbsp;
effectively managing its whole territory.
But inside Rome itself, things&nbsp;
weren’t doing much better.
One of the biggest problems with Rome in its last&nbsp;
days was the lack of strong leadership - right up&nbsp;&nbsp;
to the top. Being Roman Emperor was not a&nbsp;
great job. Given that it was an appointed&nbsp;&nbsp;
position that came with near-unlimited power,&nbsp;
a lot of people wanted it - and that meant that&nbsp;&nbsp;
getting the job came with massive risks. At one&nbsp;
point between the second and third centuries,&nbsp;&nbsp;
over twenty men held the title in less than&nbsp;
75 years - and the most common way to be fired&nbsp;&nbsp;
from this job was with a knife between&nbsp;
your ribs. Turns out Julius Caesar was a&nbsp;&nbsp;
trendsetter. When an Emperor did manage&nbsp;
to hold onto power for years, there was&nbsp;&nbsp;
a good chance they would essentially turn the&nbsp;
empire into their own personal patronage mill,&nbsp;&nbsp;
appointing their allies to powerful positions. And&nbsp;
the Emperor’s bodyguards, the Praetorian Guard,&nbsp;&nbsp;
would often take matters into their own hands and&nbsp;
assassinate emperors when they became more trouble&nbsp;&nbsp;
than they were worth. With a government like this,&nbsp;
is it any wonder that the empire began crumbling?
All of these factors came together,&nbsp;
spelling the end of the Roman Empire.
For centuries, the Roman Empire had&nbsp;
been unchallenged on a military level.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Their expeditions would face little to no&nbsp;
opposition, and their army could largely&nbsp;&nbsp;
sweep into a territory and take it over as&nbsp;
they pleased. Conquering new territories often&nbsp;&nbsp;
meant having to build new infrastructure that&nbsp;
would increase quality of life in the region&nbsp;&nbsp;
in some way as well as enforcing the rule&nbsp;
of the Roman Empire on its newly acquired&nbsp;&nbsp;
citizens. So the army wound up being more of&nbsp;
an enforcement arm of the Roman government,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and it was no longer prepared to deal with&nbsp;
major military conflict. So when outsiders&nbsp;&nbsp;
such as the Goths came calling after being&nbsp;
pushed out of their territory by the Huns,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the Roman military wasn’t forimdable&nbsp;
enough to maintain its borders.
And it all came down to one fatal mistake.
If the military was well-prepared for this&nbsp;
invasion, the odds are they could have defeated&nbsp;&nbsp;
the Goth armies and defended their territory. They&nbsp;
were better trained and had superior weaponry than&nbsp;&nbsp;
the Goths. But once the Goths had managed to&nbsp;
enter the fringes of the empire, it was going to&nbsp;&nbsp;
be much harder to displace them without a major&nbsp;
military operation. The Romans ultimately chose&nbsp;&nbsp;
the worst option of all - they didn’t aggressively&nbsp;
repel the Goth invaders and defend their borders,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but they also didn’t welcome them into the empire&nbsp;
and take advantage of this new manpower like they&nbsp;&nbsp;
could have. Instead, they exploited them and hired&nbsp;
them while never letting them develop any loyalty&nbsp;&nbsp;
to the empire. This led to constant civil wars&nbsp;
and small military skirmishes, growing tension,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and a slow but sure loss of territory. And&nbsp;
it all could have been avoided if the Empire&nbsp;&nbsp;
coordinate a better response to the Goths seeking&nbsp;
refuge within the borders of the Roman Empire.

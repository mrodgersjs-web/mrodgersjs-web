Welcome to causation versus correlation,
the autism effect. Unfortunately, this
is a voice over because I didn't realize
my mic was on. So, here we go. We live
in a world where nearly everything
interacts with our biology. Every
chemical, every medication, every
environmental factor, our bodies,
they're constantly absorbing,
processing, and responding to these
inputs constantly. And while patterns
and health outcomes appear, isolating a
single cause is nearly impossible.
Biology doesn't operate in a straight
line, and it doesn't wait for us to
fully understand it before it acts.
Correlations emerge when we look
closely. Some are subtle, some are
striking, but they persist enough to
demand attention. It's tempting to
dismiss correlations because they aren't
proof of causation. But biology is
constantly making choices, responding to
countless influences, and shaping
outcomes in ways we may not fully
measure. Imagine a child exposed to
multiple factors: environmental
chemicals, medications, diet, and
inherited genetics. Each factor
interacts, amplifies others, and even
blocks certain pathways. You cannot
point to a single trigger because the
biology has already been influenced by
layers of exposures, some visible and
some hidden. Yet the patterns we observe
are real, clear enough to study,
understand, and act on.
Our history gives us painful examples.
Decades ago, a drug calledomide
was released and caused babies to be
born with missing limbs. At the time it
seemed like an isolated tragedy, but it
revealed something fundamental.
Chemicals can act on biology in ways
that we yet don't understand. And a
single compound can disrupt development
of the human body profoundly.
Today, I think we face a similar
situation, but exponentially more
complex. We have countless
pharmaceuticals, over-the-counter
medications, and synthetic chemicals.
Many developed before the full
consequences were understood. All
interacting with human physiology
simultaneously.
Recognizing the complexity of causation
does not mean we absolve responsibility.
Just because biology is multiffactorial,
just because exposures are layered, it
doesn't mean companies, corporations, or
regulators should continue producing
these compounds without accountability.
Ignoring the patterns, ignoring the
correlations, and allowing widespread
exposure to continue is not a path
toward safety. It's a path toward
jeopardizing entire generations.
Observing correlations doesn't excuse in
action. If anything, it demands more
careful scrutiny, more transparency, and
more accountability. Our biology adapts.
Our children are affected, and the
societal consequences are profound. This
is not theoretical. As a parent, seeing
the patterns firsthand is mind-boggling.
The knowledge exists. There is no way
those in power are unaware of the
potential harm. But history shows us
that in action allows harm to compound.
We need to respect the complexity of
biology while also holding systems
accountable. Patterns may not give
perfect causation, but they give
evidence enough to demand change.
Waiting for absolute proof means waiting
while exposure continues, while pathways
are disrupted, and while future
generations are being left to bear the
consequences. Biology may be complex,
but responsibility is simple. Awareness
about truth without action is
unacceptable.

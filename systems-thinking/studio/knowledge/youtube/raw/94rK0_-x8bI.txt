Today, we live in a world where we can access
more information than ever before. But, how
do you know what is true and what’s not?
How do you know that you even know something?
For that matter, what even is knowledge? These
are questions concerning Epistemology, the
branch of philosophy concerned with knowledge
that forms the very foundation of human understanding.
Epistemology is the study of the nature, origin,
and scope of knowledge and epistemic justification.
In this video, I’ll do my best to clearly
and easily introduce the subject and cover
significant developments and concepts from
past to present-day theory. Make sure to pay
attention because after all, without understanding
knowledge and what is it, how can you coherently
argue for and know for sure what it is you
believe is true?
The term “epistemology” comes from the
Greek words “episteme” and “logos”.
“Episteme” can be translated as “knowledge”
while “logos” can be translated as “argument”
or “reason.”
All the questions and tasks of epistemology
can be sorted into two main categories which
we’ll cover in-depth.
#1. Determining the nature of knowledge. What
is it? How do we distinguish between someone
who knows something and someone who fails
to know something?
#2. Determining the extent of what we can
know. How do we acquire knowledge, and are
there limits to what can be known?
So, what is knowledge?
Knowledge, simply put, is a type of cognitive
success. More specifically, it’s a familiarity,
awareness, or understanding of someone or
something, which may include objects (called
acquaintance knowledge or ‘knowing by acquaintance’)
skills (called procedural knowledge or ‘knowing-how)
and facts (called propositional knowledge
or ‘knowing-that’).
Philosophers mainly focus on propositional
knowledge as it deals with facts about the
world such as ‘He knows that Seattle is
in California’, ‘Cats are mammals,’
or ‘2+2=71’. (A proposition can be of
course, true or false.)
Possible ways we may gain knowledge include
our perception, introspection, memory, the
testimony of someone else, and reason.
The philosopher Emmanual Kant detailed in
his work A Critique of Pure Reason that knowledge
can be categorized as ‘a priori’ or ‘a
posteriori’. A priori knowledge means knowledge
prior to any experience using only reason
i.e.: I know that all bachelors are unmarried.
A posteriori is knowledge posterior to, or
following sense experiences in addition to
reason. For example, I know that the snow
is white.
Philosophers called empiricists believe that
all knowledge is ultimately grounded upon
experience while philosophers called rationalists
believe all knowledge is ultimately grounded
upon reason.
Since we’re mainly concerned with propositional
knowledge, the scope is so broad that we need
a general characterization of knowledge we
can apply to any situation.
Traditional epistemologists generally agree
that propositional knowledge has three core
parts: belief, truth, and justification.
The first main ingredient, if you will, of
knowledge is belief, an acceptance that a
statement is true or that something exists.
For example, to accept the truth of the proposition
“the sky is blue,” one must first believe
that the sky is blue. If one has no beliefs
about a particular matter, one cannot have
knowledge about it.
Next, is truth, or the idea that one’s beliefs
are in accordance with reality or objective
facts. To acquire true knowledge is to increase
one’s true beliefs and decrease false beliefs.
If one can’t acquire truths at all, then
one has no knowledge.
However, not all true beliefs constitute knowledge.
For example, the philosopher Duncan Pritchard
argued we could have simply had epistemic
luck.
Take this example. Imagine John believes he
has a fatal illness because he’s a hypochondriac
and can’t help but believe such things.
However, it turns out that upon going to a
doctor, he’s informed he does have a fatal
illness. He was right, but it was simply a
matter of luck.
So, knowledge needs a third element, justification
or why one has good reason for holding their
beliefs. Only true beliefs arrived at in the
right way can be considered real knowledge.
Therefore, many philosophers up until the
20th century believed that justified true
belief, or JTB, as a fully accurate description
of the nature of knowledge. Unfortunately,
that came tumbling down when philosopher Edmond
Gettier in his influential 1963 paper entitled
"Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?" argued
that luck still played a role in knowledge
despite one having JTB.
Here’s an example of what’s now called
a ‘Gettier case’ that seemly shows JTB
is not true knowledge:
Suppose a normally well-working clock on-campus
stopped working at 11:59 pm last night and
is frozen. Twelve hours later, as I’m on
my way to class, I glance at the clock and
form the belief that it’s 11:59. My belief
is true since the time is indeed 11:59, and
my belief is justified. There’s never been
any reason to doubt the clock is not working,
and I cannot be blamed for basing beliefs
about the time on what the clock says. However,
it seems evident that I do not know that the
time is 11:59. If I walked past the clock
earlier in the day, I would have ended up
with a false belief rather than a true one
since the clock is frozen.
To solve Gettier’s issues, we need to understand
what justification is, specifically its nature
and structure. So, let’s dive into that
now.
One of the most important aspects of deciding
the nature of justification is to consider
if it should depend on factors solely in one’s
mind or consider external factors.
Internalism is the belief that justification
depends solely on factors internal to the
believer’s mind.
Internalism is contrasted with externalism,
which states factors outside of just psychological
ones, such as perception, can be conditions
of justification.
The most significant externalist theory of
justification is called reliabilism, put forth
by philosopher Alvin Goldman in the 1960s.
According to reliabilism a true belief counts
as knowledge only if a reliable belief-forming
process produces it. This may include standard
perceptual processes, remembering, good reasoning,
and introspection. So if we have 20/20 vision
that seems to line up with others' reports,
that’s a good belief-forming process.
However, philosopher Keith Lehrer provides
this example which seems to upend reliability
and externalism called Mr. Truetemp that goes
along the lines of this:
A man named Mr. Truetemp had a device called
a tempucomp implanted into his brain during
surgery which he was unaware of. This device
accurately reads the temperature and causes
a spontaneous belief about the temperature.
Mr. Truetemp now has many true beliefs about
temperature but doesn’t know their source
or why he has them.
His belief-forming process is reliable, but
his beliefs are unjustified. The argument
shows just because a process is reliable doesn’t
mean it’s enough to justify beliefs formed
during that process.
But, what about internalism? If justification
depends just on factors in one’s mind, this
raises the question of how these factors,
and knowledge, are structured?
The two main theories of justification are
called coherentism and foundationalism.
Coherentism is the theory that justification
is a relation of mutual support among many
beliefs. Think of coherentism as a spider
web where each belief is a section of the
web, and it’s strength of staying in tack
depends on the strength of the surrounding
areas in the web.
The problem with coherentism is that a system
of facts can be coherent, like facts about
a secret wizard underworld that fit together
but be wrong. Coherentism is based on circular
logic.
Foundationalism, on the other hand, states
that certain beliefs can be based on other
beliefs. Think of a skyscraper where one belief
is based on another below it.
An example of foundationalism is Aristotle
is deducing that Socrates is mortal. There’s
a logical real that states all humans are
mortal. Socrates is a human and humans are
mortal. Therefore, Socrates must be mortal.
However, there is skepticism and issues with
foundationalism, too. In fact, much of epistemological
study throughout the centuries has been just
to address epistemological skepticism solely.
Epistemological skepticism is the view that
we cannot know anything, or at least that
we do not know as much as we think we do.
Skepticism about the extent of what we can
know is not a new idea, but began during the
Hellenistic period. Pyrrhonism associated
with the philosopher Pyrrho involves skepticism
that states we can’t know anything, including
if we can know anything, and Academic skepticism
dating from around 266 BC associated by Carneades
that states that the only thing we know is
that we know nothing.
A significant argument against foundationalism
and in fact, the ability to provide a complete
logical foundation for human knowledge as
a whole is called the Regress Problem, also
known as Agrippa’s Trilemma, attributed
to the ancient Pyrronist skeptic Agrippa.
The argument essentially states that any proposition
supported by a belief can be endlessly (infinitely)
questioned, resulting in an infinite regress
of beliefs.
John Pollock states the argument as such:
... to justify a belief one must appeal to
a further justified belief. This means that
one of two things can be the case. Either
there are some beliefs that we can be justified
for holding, without being able to justify
them on the basis of any other belief, or
else for each justified belief there is an
infinite regress of (potential) justification
[the nebula theory]. On this theory there
is no rock bottom of justification. Justification
just meanders in and out through our network
of beliefs, stopping nowhere.
So in the case of Socrates being mortal, one
could ask what makes one human? We’d have
to find another belief like all born from
females are human. But then that leads to
another question; how do we know all born
from humans are human? And so on ad infinitum.
Epistemological skepticism isn't’ just about
just asking why for every premise. It also
involves full detailed arguments to support
its own premises.
I’ll cover two notable skeptical theories.
The first being Cartesian skepticism which
philosopher Rene Descarte outlined in the
first of his Meditations. The argument states
that our perceptions are often inaccurate;
for example, we sometimes mistake being in
a dream for real life. What if hypothetical,
an evil demon is tricking us into believing
we’re in the accurate word, when things
are far different. The modern version of this
is that we are perhaps a brain in a vat fed
data that makes it seem we have hands, legs,
and live in reality.
Of course, the scenario is far-fetched but
illustrates that our senses can tell us how
things appear, but not how they are. Descarte
attempted to refute his original argument
but with his famous “Cogito, ergo sum”
or “I think, therefore I am” statement.
However, the philosopher David Hume showed
with Humean Skepticism how if one were to
show a way to trust our perceptions clearly,
it still wouldn’t be enough to defeat epistemological
skepticism.
First is the issue of numerical vs. qualitative
identity.
For the most part, we all think the external
world is stable, and we believe various things
we see persist throughout the day. If there’s
a tree in my yard I see each day, I assume
it’s still there if I’m not looking at
it.
This can be outlined as the following:
I have had two sense-experiences of a tree:
one this morning and one in the evening.
The two sense-experiences were (more or less)
identical.
Therefore, it is likely that the objects that
caused them are identical.
Therefore, a single object – the tree – has
been in my yard all day.
However, Hume thinks arguments like this one
suffer from an equivocation fallacy, an informal
fallacy resulting from using a particular
word/expression in multiple senses within
an argument. Mainly the first use of the word
identical refers to qualitative identity while
the second is numerical identity.
When we say that the objects that caused the
two sense experiences are identical, we mean
that there is one object, rather than two,
that is responsible for both of them, which
renders the argument fallacious.
This problem goes deeper than that.
Not only is there no reason to believe objects
we observe can persist day-to-day, but we
have no reason to assume similar objects like
other trees will persist either.
We can outline the argument like this…
The tree has always persisted in the past.
Nature is roughly uniform across time and
space and thus the future will be roughly
like the past. (We might call this second
statement the principle of the uniformity
of nature or PUN.)
Therefore, that tree will persist into the
future.
However, because this is an inductive argument,
Hume states that we need to provide some additional
proof that PUN is true. This is referred to
as the ‘ problem of induction.’
So we might make this argument to show PUN
is true:
PUN has always been true in the past.
space (and thus the future will be roughly
like the past).
Therefore, PUN will be true in the future.
However, this argument’s second premise
is just PUN restated. Thus, it’s circular,
meaning we haven’t solved the problem of
induction, and thus skepticism about knowledge
remains.
At this point you’re probably wondering
if humanity has made any progress in knowing
anything since the original Hellenistic skeptics.
Could it really be that we truly know nothing
despite centuries of writing, thinking, and
arguing?
Hume did offer a way out of the woods of global
epistemological skepticism with the idea of
mitigated skepticism.
Mitigated skepticism admits that we will never
know anything for 100% certainty, but perhaps
we can have partial knowledge of things while
still remaining open minded and skeptical.
One of the most promising responses today
to global epistemological skepticism is called
Bayesian Epistemology which uses this idea
of partial knowledge.
Based on Thomas Bayes' work in probability
theory, instead of having fixed beliefs about
propositions, with Bayesian Epistemology we
are constantly updating our beliefs as probabilities
depending on evidence and context.
In Bayesian Epistemology, belief is seen as
a continuous phenomenon of degrees called
credences.
Credences we already have about reality before
we begin to gather new evidence or information
are what’s referred to as priors.
Knowledge then is a combination of new information
that updates our credences plus our already
established priors.
Yes, the idea of knowing something 100% is
fully impossible, but we can have degrees
of belief, some greater than others. We can
know some things less reliably than others
to form a framework of reality and truth.
Of course, Bayesian Epistemology has its critics
and skepticism, but this perhaps the most
promising theory that helps us understand
what truth and real knowledge are as of recording
this video.
In conclusion, the study of epistemology is
long from over and is both a blessing and
a curse to humanity. We’re the only species
that strives to understand our place in the
universe, why we’re here, and what our purpose
is, if there’s one at all. Cognition has
helped us come to dominate the planet but
may also lead to the end of us if we don't’
continue to pursue and try to understand truth
and knowledge.

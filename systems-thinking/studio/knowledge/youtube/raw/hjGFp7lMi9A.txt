Critical phenomena arise at transitions.
The idea is that when the system is&nbsp;
just at this edge of order and disorder,&nbsp;&nbsp;
interesting complex dynamics can arise.
As a physicist, critical phenomena is&nbsp;
extremely appealing because it appears&nbsp;&nbsp;
in many phenomena — from the evolution of the&nbsp;
universe to the properties of superconductors,&nbsp;&nbsp;
flocks of starlings, networks of brain cells,&nbsp;
tectonic plates, social interactions among humans,&nbsp;&nbsp;
all these types of things. Any time I can&nbsp;
see one equation apply to lots and lots of&nbsp;&nbsp;
different things, I think that’s beautiful. It’s&nbsp;
economical. It’s insightful. Which raises a really&nbsp;&nbsp;
profound question: Why? Why are so many things&nbsp;
in nature operating near the critical point?
When physical systems go through phase&nbsp;
transitions, such as when water transitions&nbsp;&nbsp;
from a liquid into a vapor because of a&nbsp;
change in temperature, the system moves&nbsp;&nbsp;
through what’s known as the critical point&nbsp;
– a fleeting moment of transition from one&nbsp;&nbsp;
phase to another characterized by exotic emergent&nbsp;
properties that have long intrigued scientists.
Critical systems have this property&nbsp;
of changing phase. Small changes in&nbsp;&nbsp;
some critical environmental variable lead&nbsp;
to drastic changes — almost discontinuous&nbsp;&nbsp;
changes — in the function. And it’s that&nbsp;
kind of observation that leads us to believe&nbsp;&nbsp;
that the study of critical&nbsp;
transitions is valuable.
Critical dynamics are best demonstrated in a&nbsp;
simplified system known as the Ising model,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which visualizes the individual iron atoms making&nbsp;&nbsp;
up a magnet with arrows to indicate&nbsp;
the direction of each atom’s spin.
You can imagine a lattice. And on this lattice you&nbsp;&nbsp;
get all these little spins that&nbsp;
can point either up or down.
And when this lattice is really cold,&nbsp;
what will happen is all the spins will&nbsp;&nbsp;
line up together. So the nearest&nbsp;
neighbor interactions will cause&nbsp;&nbsp;
them all to point in the same direction.&nbsp;
This piece of iron — BING! It would stick&nbsp;&nbsp;
on your refrigerator because all the&nbsp;
bar magnets are in the same direction.
But now if you heat this up — if you took&nbsp;
a little Bic lighter and you put it under&nbsp;&nbsp;
it — what would happen is these little&nbsp;
spins would start moving. They start&nbsp;&nbsp;
going in different directions. And then they&nbsp;
would eventually cancel. Some of them would&nbsp;&nbsp;
point up and some of them would point down, and&nbsp;
then it would fall off of your refrigerator.
So you get a phase transition from being&nbsp;
very ordered to being totally disordered.
As it passes from order to disorder, the system&nbsp;
moves through the critical point and clusters of&nbsp;&nbsp;
similarly oriented spins form throughout the&nbsp;
lattice. If you were to measure the sizes of&nbsp;&nbsp;
these clusters at various scales, the data would&nbsp;
reveal what’s known as a power law, where dynamics&nbsp;&nbsp;
at one scale mirror the dynamics at other scales.&nbsp;
This phenomenon is also known as scale invariance.
Scale invariance is another way of saying that&nbsp;
there is self-similarity or fractality.&nbsp;These&nbsp;&nbsp;
kinds of properties are spectacular because indeed&nbsp;
everything simplifies at the critical point.
When a system reaches the critical point,&nbsp;&nbsp;
it displays a telltale peak in what is known&nbsp;
as the correlation length — an indication of&nbsp;&nbsp;
how sensitive the system as a whole is to&nbsp;
the activity of any one of its components.
What happens is the system behaves in ways that&nbsp;&nbsp;
allow fluctuations to occur over&nbsp;
the scale of the entire system.
If it was too cold, you’d have no correlation&nbsp;
because they’re just pointing. They’re not&nbsp;&nbsp;
moving. And when it’s too hot, they’re&nbsp;
moving a lot, but they’re not correlated.
So only at that sweet spot right in the&nbsp;
middle do you have interactions at all scales.
Now, what that means is something very weird.&nbsp;
That means that the distance over which these&nbsp;&nbsp;
spins might interact is technically infinite.&nbsp;
I could take a spin over here and flip it,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and there’s some nonzero probability that&nbsp;
another spin very, very far away would flip&nbsp;&nbsp;
also as a result of it. So in other words, I&nbsp;
can initiate a cascade of events that would&nbsp;&nbsp;
propagate through the system, and it would have&nbsp;
some nonzero probability of affecting that.
In 1987, the physicist Per Bak wondered if&nbsp;
many different types of complex systems in&nbsp;&nbsp;
the natural world might self-organize&nbsp;
around critical points. To illustrate&nbsp;&nbsp;
his theory of “self-organized criticality,”&nbsp;
Bak used the familiar example of a sandpile.
As the pile gains mass, friction can no&nbsp;
longer hold the grains of sand in place,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and a single grain added to the pile&nbsp;
will trigger an outsized effect,&nbsp;&nbsp;
sending avalanches cascading down its sides.
And it turns out that if you look at the&nbsp;
distribution of avalanche sizes — the big ones,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the small ones, the intermediate&nbsp;
ones — they follow power laws.
And so Per Bak’s idea was that, hey, here’s&nbsp;
a natural system that self-organizes to the&nbsp;&nbsp;
critical point. You don’t need to tune it&nbsp;
there. You don’t need to get just the right&nbsp;&nbsp;
control temperature to put the Ising&nbsp;
model. It will evolve into that state.
And so when he came out with this&nbsp;
concept of self-organized criticality,&nbsp;&nbsp;
he was claiming that many natural&nbsp;
systems fall into that category,&nbsp;&nbsp;
like earthquakes, like stock&nbsp;
market crashes, like piles of sand.
Now, he was a pioneer, and it&nbsp;
was amazing that he did that,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and it inspired many people from&nbsp;
other areas to enter into the&nbsp;&nbsp;
field of criticality and to take a look at&nbsp;
this concept and apply it more generally.
And I would say basically it’s had&nbsp;
huge amounts of traction. However,&nbsp;&nbsp;
there have been people who&nbsp;
have been quite skeptical.
Bak’s equations only account for one grain of&nbsp;
sand hitting the pile at a time. In nature,&nbsp;&nbsp;
things are more complicated, and researchers have&nbsp;
found it difficult to simulate true criticality.
This is a general problem of mathematical models&nbsp;
just to be always aware of: At what point have you&nbsp;&nbsp;
overextended that simple abstraction and&nbsp;
applied it in a way that’s inadmissible?
And so SOC is just one mechanism for tuning to&nbsp;
critical points. It’s a very interesting one,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but perhaps it will turn out to be a rare one.
Despite the criticism, Bak’s work&nbsp;
inspired interest in criticality&nbsp;&nbsp;
throughout the 1990s and into the early&nbsp;
2000s, when neuroscientists began to probe&nbsp;&nbsp;
a new question: whether brains might&nbsp;
exhibit self-organized criticality.
Per Bak’s work opened up the concept that&nbsp;
criticality could apply to many different things,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and that made me think: We’ve got lots of&nbsp;
neurons that are interacting in this network,&nbsp;&nbsp;
so, hey, why not? So we just started&nbsp;
to apply that framework to the data.
The idea that the brain is at the&nbsp;
transition point — for example,&nbsp;&nbsp;
at criticality, at the transition between order&nbsp;
and chaos — has been around for a while. I think&nbsp;&nbsp;
the real avalanche of criticality research was&nbsp;
triggered by John Beggs and Dietmar Plenz in 2003.
We isolated the gray matter. The cortex has a&nbsp;
piece of tissue. When it was young, we grew it on&nbsp;&nbsp;
a microelectrode array in a dish. We let it grow&nbsp;
for about four weeks and we measured the activity,&nbsp;&nbsp;
how these cells would interact with each other.&nbsp;
And we found that in layers II, III, they start to&nbsp;&nbsp;
form groups like just these cascades that were&nbsp;
predicted by the Per Bak sandpile model. And&nbsp;&nbsp;
plotting avalanche science distributions&nbsp;
and sure enough, they were power laws.
It was the first paper that claimed that the brain&nbsp;
was probably functioning at a critical point.
The question for scientists then became:&nbsp;&nbsp;
Why? Why might functioning at a&nbsp;
critical point be helpful for brains?
Can you show that operating near the&nbsp;
critical point actually increases&nbsp;&nbsp;
behavioral performance? And when you’re&nbsp;
not near the critical point, it doesn’t?
So why would being at the critical&nbsp;
point be to your evolutionary advantage?
So let’s say you’re at the side of a river and&nbsp;
there’s a bunch of reeds and they’re blowing in&nbsp;&nbsp;
the wind. And then you notice that, hey, this is&nbsp;
different from yesterday. I think there’s a tiger.&nbsp;&nbsp;
So you want to be very sensitive to inputs. The&nbsp;
system is most susceptible to slight changes&nbsp;&nbsp;
in inputs when it’s near the critical point. It&nbsp;
has these large fluctuations that can take off.
According to the critical brain hypothesis,&nbsp;
when the network is right at criticality,&nbsp;&nbsp;
it’s perfectly balanced between two extreme&nbsp;
states: super-criticality, in which networks&nbsp;&nbsp;
of neurons display the highly ordered runaway&nbsp;
excitations seen in epilepsy, and sub-criticality,&nbsp;&nbsp;
in which signals fail to trigger larger cascades&nbsp;
and stall out, as seen in comatose states.
By hovering near the critical point,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the theory goes, networks of neurons would&nbsp;
be optimized for information transmission.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Just like in the Ising model, tiny inputs could&nbsp;
result in big, complex behaviors in the network.&nbsp;
Proving that such a measurement&nbsp;
of optimal brain activity exists&nbsp;&nbsp;
would give researchers a new scale to&nbsp;
interpret just about everything brains do.
When we first got our results back from&nbsp;
the 2003 paper, I was just enamored with&nbsp;&nbsp;
the idea of criticality. I was in love&nbsp;
with it. I’d go to bed thinking, “Oh,&nbsp;&nbsp;
it’s optimal information transmission. We get&nbsp;
the — just the right exponents. It’s all cool.”
And then over time, people started&nbsp;
to question this in various ways.
Just as with Per Bak’s sandpile model,&nbsp;&nbsp;
scientists began to question whether the&nbsp;
physics of criticality could neatly apply&nbsp;&nbsp;
to such a chaotic biological system with&nbsp;
so many variables interacting all at once.
In simpler systems like the Ising model, a&nbsp;
single variable like temperature can be adjusted&nbsp;&nbsp;
to bring the network right to the critical&nbsp;
point. But in complex biological systems,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the prospect of tuning to the exact point&nbsp;
of criticality would be much more difficult.
The brain is constantly receiving inputs from&nbsp;
outside that could, you know, blow it off of&nbsp;&nbsp;
the critical point. So for those reasons alone, it&nbsp;
can’t really be exactly critical. Then what is it?
One of the options of many on the&nbsp;
menu about how the brain is actually&nbsp;&nbsp;
operating is that it’s slightly&nbsp;
sub-critical, and that it doesn’t&nbsp;&nbsp;
really get to the critical point&nbsp;
because that might be dangerous.
Another plausible idea is that it’s quasicritical.&nbsp;&nbsp;
And what that means is that it gets as close to&nbsp;
the critical point as it can. But then there’s&nbsp;&nbsp;
this activity that’s basically going to push&nbsp;
it away from being right at the critical point.
As research continues to reveal&nbsp;
tantalizing signatures of criticality,&nbsp;&nbsp;
what was once a fringe theory has begun to&nbsp;
attract more mainstream attention in the field,&nbsp;&nbsp;
with researchers now hunting for what kinds of&nbsp;&nbsp;
mechanisms might be responsible for&nbsp;
tuning brains to the critical point.
The big question that is unanswered so far is&nbsp;
what is the homeostatic mechanism bringing back&nbsp;&nbsp;
the brain to this quasi-criticality region?
That’s a big question — a big open question.&nbsp;
That’s the million-dollar question.
Neuroscience has been and continues to be very&nbsp;
hesitant and reluctant to agree on a theoretical&nbsp;&nbsp;
idea of the kind that criticality offers. Most&nbsp;
neuroscientists are very hard-nosed empiricists.&nbsp;&nbsp;
They don’t believe that there is an overarching&nbsp;
theory that explains most — or, you know, god&nbsp;&nbsp;
forbid — all of what the brain is doing in&nbsp;
one handy concept such as critical state.
I personally think that what does not&nbsp;
play well with neuroscientists is if&nbsp;&nbsp;
criticality is portrayed as the answer to&nbsp;
everything. I think that is overselling it.
And yet I have no doubt believing&nbsp;
that a system like the brain almost&nbsp;&nbsp;
requires us to be in a critical state for&nbsp;
it to function well or optimally, even.
There might also be one equation that explains how&nbsp;
the whole thing works. That’s the idealized dream.&nbsp;&nbsp;
We may never, ever get there, but the hope is&nbsp;
that there might be some general principles&nbsp;&nbsp;
that really explain how intelligence&nbsp;
functions in this world that we live in.
The field wasn’t there 20 years ago when we had&nbsp;
just one idea, a sandpile model or an Ising model,&nbsp;&nbsp;
that would guide us. We are way beyond that. And&nbsp;
we are at the point now where the technological&nbsp;&nbsp;
advance in neuroscience to record the individual&nbsp;
spiking activity for many, many thousands of&nbsp;&nbsp;
neurons…. These are the precision tools that we&nbsp;
need in order to test new ideas on criticality.
How is the collective coming together to&nbsp;
produce outcomes that are way beyond what&nbsp;&nbsp;
an individual could do? And I think this is&nbsp;
how our society is organized. This is how&nbsp;&nbsp;
our brain, our body is organized. And&nbsp;
any understanding of the richness that&nbsp;&nbsp;
we gain when we operate as a collective, I&nbsp;
think, is just beautiful scientific insight.

What if the moment a financial metric
becomes important, it actually becomes
less reliable? This idea helps explain
why economic indicators, performance
statistics, and policy targets sometimes
stop reflecting reality. It's called
Goodart's law. The idea was first
discussed by the British economist
Charles Goodhart in the 1970s while
studying monetary policy. He observed
that once policymakers begin targeting a
specific economic indicator, the
statistical relationship that indicator
had with the broader economy can change
or even break down. In simple terms,
when a measure becomes a target, it can
stop being a good measure. Why does this
happen? Because people respond to
incentives. When a number determines
rewards, policy decisions, or financial
outcomes, people begin adjusting their
behavior to improve that number. Not
necessarily to improve the underlying
system, but to improve the metric
itself. You can think of it like
measuring the performance of a company.
If a company is evaluated purely by
quarterly profits, managers may start
focusing only on short-term results,
even if that hurts the company in the
long run. The number improves, but the
underlying system does not. Economic
history contains several examples of
this pattern. One well-known example
comes from the fight against inflation
in the United States in the late 1970s
and early 1980s. At the time, economists
and policymakers used different measures
of the money supply known as monetary
aggregates. Two commonly discussed ones
were M1 and M2. M1 is a narrow measure
of money. It includes physical cash and
money held in checking accounts. In
other words, money that can be spent
immediately. M2 is a broader measure. It
includes everything in M1, but also
savings accounts and certain other
deposits that are slightly less liquid.
During this period, many economists
believe that inflation was closely
related to how quickly the money supply
was growing. Because of this idea, the
Federal Reserve began placing greater
emphasis on the growth of certain
monetary aggregates, especially M1, as
an indicator when conducting monetary
policy. The reasoning was
straightforward. If the growth of M1
slowed, the amount of money immediately
available for spending would grow more
slowly and inflationary pressure might
eventually decline. But once M1 became
an important policy focus, financial
markets began to adapt. Banks and
investors were responding to economic
incentives, not trying to manipulate the
statistics themselves. They were mainly
trying to offer competitive returns and
respond to regulatory limits on interest
rates. During a period of high
inflation, financial institutions began
developing new financial products that
allowed people to hold cash-like assets
without keeping their money in checking
accounts that were counted in M1. One
important example was the rapid growth
of money market funds in the early
1980s. Money market funds allowed
investors to hold highly liquid
short-term financial assets that behaved
in many ways like cash. However, these
funds were not included in M1 because
they were not traditional checking
deposits. As a result, people could move
money out of checking accounts, which
were counted in M1, and into money
market funds or similar instruments that
were treated differently in the money
supply statistics. From the perspective
of the statistics, M1 could appear to
grow slowly or even decline. But in
reality, people still held large amounts
of highly liquid money-like assets. So,
policymakers were still watching the
growth of M1. But financial innovation
had made M1 a much less reliable
indicator of the liquidity in the
economy. In other words, the metric
policymakers were focusing on no longer
reflected the underlying reality they
were trying to understand. This is
exactly the type of situation Goodart's
law describes. Once a metric becomes a
target, people begin adjusting their
behavior in ways that make that metric
less useful for measuring what is
actually happening in the system. And
sometimes these incentive effects can
lead to very unexpected outcomes. In
fact, there is a famous historical case
where a government tried to reduce the
number of dangerous cobras and
accidentally ended up creating more of
them. Economists call this the cobra
effect, and we'll look at that story in

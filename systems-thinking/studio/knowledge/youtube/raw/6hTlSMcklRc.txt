Have you ever used a product and
thought, "This feels like it was built
by five different teams that barely talk
to each other." Or perhaps this feature
set is so disjointed. What if I told you
there's a fundamental law that explains
why this happens?
It's called Conway's law and it states
organizations which design systems are
constrained to produce designs which are
copies of the communication structures
of these organizations.
In simpler terms, your company's
organizational chart secretly designs
its products.
First articulated by computer programmer
Melvin Conway in 1968, this observation
highlights a profound truth about how
human organization impacts technological
output. He wasn't just talking about
software. His insights apply to any
complex system an organization creates.
The core principle is deceptively
simple. If four teams are working on a
compiler, they will produce a fourpass
compiler. If they can't communicate
effectively, their individual components
won't integrate smoothly. The product
reflects the communication paths or lack
thereof within the organization.
Imagine building a house. If the
plumbing team never talks to the
electrical team, you might end up with
pipes running where wires should be or
switches placed inconveniently far from
outlets. The houses's design ends up
reflecting the fragmented communication
between the construction teams.
In software, this is often seen in
monolithic applications. A large,
tightly coupled system where different
teams are responsible for different
features, but they all push code into
one giant codebase. Without strong,
intentional communication, the result
can be a product that feels like a
collection of disperate features rather
than a cohesive hole.
Why does this happen? It's largely due
to communication overhead. When teams
are structured in silos, information
flow becomes constrained. Each team
optimizes for its own component or
function, often without full visibility
or understanding of how their part
impacts the whole.
Information struggles to cross
organizational boundaries. Decisions
made in one department might have
unforeseen consequences in another
simply because the communication
channels aren't designed to facilitate
seamless collaboration.
One of the most common consequences is
integration challenges. When different
parts of a product are built by teams
that don't communicate effectively,
those parts often don't fit together
well, leading to bugs, rework, and a
frustrating user experience.
Another outcome is redundancy. Different
teams might unknowingly build similar
functionalities or components because
they aren't aware of what other teams
are doing. This wastes resources,
increases complexity, and slows down
development.
The product itself can suffer from
feature bloat or a lack of cohesion. It
might have many functionalities, but
they don't feel like they belong
together or the user experience is
inconsistent across different parts of
the system.
Conway's law can also stifle innovation.
When new ideas require approval or
coordination across many different and
disconnected departments, the pace of
development slows to a crawl and agile
responses to market changes become
nearly impossible.
While often discussed in software
development, Conway's law applies
universally. Think about the design of a
car, a government service, or even the
patient journey in a hospital. The way
these systems are organized often
mirrors the departmental structure of
the organizations that created them.
The good news is Conway's law isn't just
a descriptive observation. It's a
powerful tool. Understanding it allows
us to perform what's known as the
inverse Conway maneuver.
Instead of letting your organizational
structure dictate your product design,
you intentionally design your
organization to achieve the product
architecture you desire. You align your
teams to the components or services you
want to build.
A prime example is the shift towards
microservices architecture. Instead of
one large team working on a monolith,
smaller autonomous teams are each
responsible for a specific micros
service. This organizational structure
directly supports a distributed modular
product architecture.
Agile methodologies with their emphasis
on small cross- functional teams and
frequent communication are inherently
designed to counteract the negative
effects of Conway's law. By bringing
diverse skills together, they foster
better internal communication within the
team, leading to more cohesive product
increments.
Similarly, the DevOps movement aims to
break down the traditional wall between
development and operations teams by
fostering collaboration and shared
responsibility. It ensures that the
products operational aspects are
considered from the very beginning,
leading to more robust and deployable
systems.
Beyond structure, organizations can
actively facilitate communication
pathways, tools, shared workspaces,
regular cross team meetings, and even
informal social events can help build
the relationships necessary for
effective collaboration.
Empowering teams with autonomy over
their specific product areas coupled
with clear boundaries and interfaces
allows them to innovate quickly without
constant external dependencies. This
fosters a sense of ownership and
accountability.
Ultimately, leadership plays a critical
role in shaping the organizational
structure. Leaders must understand
Conway's law and intentionally design
teams, reporting lines, and
communication channels to support the
desired product outcomes.
The ability to adapt and reconfigure
teams as product needs evolve is key.
Organizational agility, not just
technical agility, becomes paramount in
ensuring that your structure continues
to serve your product goals.
So, how do you spot Conway's law at play
in your own organization? Look for
products that feel disjointed, features
that are hard to integrate, or
persistent communication breakdowns
between teams working on related
components. These are often symptoms of
an underlying organizational structure
that's inadvertently shaping your
product.
Conway's law isn't about blaming
individuals. It's about understanding
systemic forces. By becoming aware of
how your organizational structure
influences your product, you gain the
power to intentionally design for better
outcomes.
Take a moment to consider your own
company's org chart. What does it say
about the products you're building? By
aligning your teams with your product
vision, you can unlock greater cohesion,
efficiency, and innovation.

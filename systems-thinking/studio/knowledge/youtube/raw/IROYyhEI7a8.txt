The complexity of the political
and economic changes
that we need to make to tackle
the climate crisis can feel overwhelming.
One way to bring that complexity
within reach of practical action
is to adopt a different lens
to view the situation
- the lens of systems -
which is really another way of saying
the science of patterns
and their underlying rules.
Every natural system
and every human system
can be thought
of as a set of relationships
that produce
repeating patterns of behaviour
in the natural world, all living things
are reliant on, and continued by,
their relationships with each other
like the rabbits
living in a field eating the grass,
and the foxes that prey on them.
While individual creatures come and go,
the pattern of eating and
predation continue over time.
In our human world,
we also live in patterns of everyday life
that persist over time.
Take energy:
You can trace the pattern of our reliance
on fossil fuels
all the way back to the early 1700s
when we started to make use of coal
to fuel the industrial revolution.
Now we have to
leave that pattern behind
and move to a new
pattern based on clean power.
This way of looking at
the world reveals
both what keeps
a pattern in place
and how new
patterns can get going.
At the heart of this way of thinking
is the simple concept of a causal loop.
This helps us
see how patterns behave over time,
through cycles of cause and effect,
whether a pattern strengthens
or weakens
caused by positive or negative feedback.
As a population of rabbits increases,
they reproduce more,
raising the population, reproducing more,
and so on.
That's positive feedback,
and we call it a reinforcing loop.
But as the population
increases, the predators turn up
and start to reduce the number,
limiting the growth.
That's negative feedback,
and we represent it with a balancing loop.
New technologies
usually benefit from positive feedbacks
or learning by doing, in which
the more we make something,
the better we get at making it
so we can improve performance
and reduce the price.
This turns into a reinforcing loop.
The better the performance, the more
the demand from the market goes up.
And the more
we can invest to improve the performance.
And so the demand goes up.
And so it goes on in a reinforcing loop.
This is common in technology development.
In fact, improvement of this sort
in products is so common
that we almost take it for granted.
Once a new approach gets into a loop
like this, it can improve
very quickly
and soon replace the old pattern.
Think of the big leap we made from horse
drawn to motorised transport,
something previous generations
couldn't have imagined.
We need to make changes at this pace and
scale to the patterns of our world today.
Policy can play a big part in getting
this sort of dynamic of change going
for the new solutions we need,
creating a reinforcing loop of ambition.
For example,
when businesses in a sector commit
to zero carbon targets and start
investing and innovating,
they embolden policymakers,
and when policymakers
set the regulatory framework
to drive towards zero carbon,
they incentivise the private sector.
This ambition loop can draw in resources,
raise ambition, and accelerate change.
As well as reinforcing loops
that drive things along
we need to pay attention
to the balancing loops
of negative feedback that hold them back.
Nothing can grow forever, so there is
always something that creates a pushback.
That might be saturation of the market
or the limits of technology,
but it can also be unintended
aspects of policy
that tend to slow down or prevent change.
For example, carbon pricing is a tool
for driving down carbon emissions,
but some approaches are
less effective than others
because of negative feedback
effects from balancing loops.
This example shows the
importance of system thinking
to effective policy.
In classical economics,
there is no difference
between the two main
approaches to carbon pricing,
carbon taxes or cap-and-trade,
but modelling with causal loops shows
they have very different effects.
The cap-and-trade approach
sets a cap on the allowable emissions
in a sector
which can be lowered over time.
Permits for emitting carbon
are available up to the total of the cap,
and businesses have to buy enough permits
to cover their emissions.
So that is an attractive way to link
policy to targets to reduce emissions,
because the cap on available
permits controls the allowable emissions.
There is a market in the permits
and when a business lowers
its emissions it can sell its permits
to someone else who needs them.
But when
they do this, they are also lowering
the demand for permits overall
and the price will drop.
So the innovation by one player lowers
the incentive to change for the others.
Looking at the situation
dynamically with causal loops
reveals this sort of self-limiting
negative feedback effect,
and can help point to
alternative approaches
that are likely to be more effective.
For example, a fixed carbon
tax does not create a negative feedback,
and so is likely to create change faster
than a cap-and-trade scheme.
A subsidy for renewables
also creates many positive feedbacks,
and so is likely
to be even more effective.
In any part of the economy or society
that we are trying to change.
There are usually many positive
and negative feedback loops
all interacting with each other.
Together
these can produce all kinds of behaviour:
bubbles, crashes, stability,
oscillations, growth, collapse,
and transitions from
one pattern to another.
Causal loops can be used
to explore these relationships,
help us avoid unhelpful negative effects,
and capture the power
of positive feedback.

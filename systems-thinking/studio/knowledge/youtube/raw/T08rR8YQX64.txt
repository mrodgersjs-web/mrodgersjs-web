Have you ever looked at a giant company,
one filled with the absolute smartest
people, and just wondered why it takes
them forever to get anything done? It's
really tempting to point the finger at
bad managers or maybe unmotivated
employees. But the real reason is often
much more fundamental. The problem isn't
always the people. It's the sheer
physics of being big. You know the
feeling. A project gets bogged down. A
decision takes months. And our first
instinct is to assume someone somewhere
is dropping the ball. We think maybe the
leadership just doesn't get it or that
someone isn't doing their job. But
here's the strange part. These same
slowmoving companies are the ones that
attract top tier talent. They're full of
brilliant, driven people who really want
to make an impact. So if the people are
great, what's actually going on? Well,
this points to a completely different
idea. What if the slowness isn't a flaw,
but an unavoidable side effect of
getting bigger? What if the very act of
growing creates a kind of organizational
gravity that naturally slows everything
down? To really wrap our heads around
this, we can turn to a core principle
from economics. It perfectly explains
what happens when a company's growth
goes past a certain sweet spot. And that
principle is called diseconomies of
scale. Now, you've probably heard of
economics of scale, where getting bigger
makes you more efficient. This is the
exact opposite. It's the tipping point
where adding more people and more
resources actually starts to make you
less efficient and your average costs
start to go up. This chart shows it

It hits this perfect point of efficiency
right at the bottom, QST star. But if it

diseconomies of scale. So that feeling
of slowness, the frustrating red tape,
the projects that seem to cost way more
than they should, those are all just
symptoms of a company operating on that
rising right-hand side of the curve.
It's literally grown past its most
efficient size. So why does this happen?
What causes this inefficiency? Well, one
of the biggest culprits is something we
deal with every day, communication. Just
think about it this way. On a tiny team
of three people, you only have three
lines of communication between them.
It's simple. But if you just double that
team to six people, the communication
channels don't double to six, they
explode to 15. The growth isn't linear,
it's exponential. And this gets out of
hand incredibly fast. With just 10
people on a team, you're already
juggling 45 different communication
channels. By the time you get to a group
of 50, that number has skyrocketed to
over,200. Every new person you add
creates a huge new web of connections.
All of this creates what we can call
communication overhead. It's like an
invisible task that gets applied to
every single task. It means more
meetings, more emails, more status
updates, and a much higher chance for
misunderstandings. All of that creates
friction and that friction slows the
entire machine down. Now, communication
is one big piece of the puzzle. The
second major factor is the sheer weight
of the formal structure a company has to
build just to manage all those people.
See, as a company gets bigger, it's just
impossible for a handful of leaders to
keep an eye on everything. So, what do
they do? To maintain some control and
make sure everyone is on the same page,
they add layers of management. It's a
totally logical move, but every single
layer you add brings more administrative
cost and slows down the whole
decision-making process. And over time,
this can lead to something called
bureaucratic inertia. This is when the
company's own rules and procedures take
on a life of their own. The organization
just keeps doing things the way we've
always done them, even if those ways
don't make sense anymore. And the signs
of this are probably familiar. You see
things like duplication of effort, where
two different teams are accidentally
solving the same problem. Office
politics can start to flare up as people
compete for resources and the people
making the big decisions can become
isolated from what's happening on the
front lines. The end result is a company
that just can't react as quickly to
changes in the market. A classic real
world example of this happened at
General Motors. At one point, they had
two different divisions that
independently developed their own very
similar design software. Because the
organization was so huge and
coordination was so difficult, they
basically paid for the same thing twice
and then had to spend a ton of money to
merge the two systems later on. When you
have all these layers of management, the
people at the very top who are making
the big strategic calls, they become
insulated. They're just further away
from the real world impact of their
choices, which over time can lead to
some pretty poor decisions. So, all of
this sounds pretty negative, right? It
raises a really important question. If
this is the case, why do these systems
even exist? I mean, if all these layers
and processes make companies slow and
inefficient, why do basically all large
successful companies have them? And
here's the key. These structures are not
a mistake. They are a deliberate and you
could even say necessary solution to the
enormous challenges that come with
operating at a massive scale. You have
to think about what all this bureaucracy
actually achieves. It's what allows a
company to manage incredible complexity.
It reduces risk by creating standards so
you don't have thousands of employees
just making things up as they go. It's
what ensures the quality of the product
is predictable and it creates the
stability needed to comply with a
mountain of legal requirements. This
really gets to the heart of it. It's all
about a fundamental trade-off. A small
company can be incredibly agile and move
super fast, but it's also high risk. One
bad mistake could be the end of it. A
large company, on the other hand, trades
that speed and agility for stability. It
moves a lot slower, sure, but it's also
much more resilient and predict. So, the
next time you see a huge company moving
at a snail's pace, just remember that
it's not always because the people there
are incompetent. More often than not,
that slowness is the unaffordable price
they pay for the very systems that
create the stability and reduce the risk
that allowed them to become so big in
the first place.

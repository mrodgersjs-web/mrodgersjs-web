Now before diving into the components of the&nbsp;
immune system itself, any study of immunology&nbsp;&nbsp;
will benefit from an overview of its history for&nbsp;
appropriate context. To do this properly, let’s&nbsp;&nbsp;
go back in time a couple thousand years. Before&nbsp;
we knew what the immune system was, and before&nbsp;&nbsp;
we knew that pathogenic microbes exist, people&nbsp;
were still able to recognize the fundamental&nbsp;&nbsp;
principles of immunity. In his description of&nbsp;
a terrible plague that swept Athens in 430 BCE,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the Greek historian Thucydides wrote, “Yet it&nbsp;
was with those who had recovered from the disease&nbsp;&nbsp;
that the sick and the dying found most compassion.&nbsp;
These knew what it was from experience, and had&nbsp;&nbsp;
now no fear for themselves; for the same man was&nbsp;
never attacked twice – never at least fatally”.&nbsp;
It wouldn’t be until centuries later that&nbsp;
scientists like Robert Koch and Louis Pasteur&nbsp;&nbsp;
demonstrated that diseases such as smallpox and&nbsp;
plague are caused by infectious microorganisms.&nbsp;&nbsp;
However, Thucydides’ observation that&nbsp;
people who had previously been infected&nbsp;&nbsp;
were protected from subsequent infection is an&nbsp;
excellent example of what we call adaptive or&nbsp;&nbsp;
acquired immunity. This principle is the basis&nbsp;
of how vaccines work, which we will discuss in&nbsp;&nbsp;
considerable depth later in the series.
Although Edward Jenner has largely been&nbsp;&nbsp;
credited with the development of the first&nbsp;
vaccine, which was against smallpox in 1796,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the principles of immunization, or intentional&nbsp;
exposure to a pathogen to avoid future infection,&nbsp;&nbsp;
had already been in practice across the world&nbsp;
for centuries. In the 9th century CE, the&nbsp;&nbsp;
Persian physician Al Rhazes noticed that smallpox&nbsp;
survivors were no longer susceptible to subsequent&nbsp;&nbsp;
bouts of the disease. In China around 1000 CE,&nbsp;
people learned that inhaling the powdered crusts&nbsp;&nbsp;
of smallpox scabs would protect them against&nbsp;
future cases of smallpox. Then in the early 1700s,&nbsp;&nbsp;
an African slave in Boston named Onesimus&nbsp;
described how he had been inoculated against&nbsp;&nbsp;
smallpox, which was a common practice in southern&nbsp;
Libya. Around the same time in Constantinople,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the British poet Lady Mary Wortley Montagu&nbsp;
learned about Turkish women who performed&nbsp;&nbsp;
operations to inoculate people against smallpox.&nbsp;
After having her son inoculated in Turkey,&nbsp;&nbsp;
she brought this knowledge back to England. It was&nbsp;
only after all of this activity that Edward Jenner&nbsp;&nbsp;
introduced the concept of vaccination by using&nbsp;
a weaker, less dangerous version of the virus&nbsp;&nbsp;
that causes smallpox in order to immunize people,&nbsp;
instead of using smallpox itself. This process was&nbsp;&nbsp;
safer and easier to regulate, leading to the&nbsp;
widespread adoption of the smallpox vaccine.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Much of this information is discussed from a&nbsp;
strictly historical perspective in the history&nbsp;&nbsp;
of drugs series, so be sure to head over there&nbsp;
to learn more about the development of this&nbsp;&nbsp;
and other medical innovations.
But moving forward from here,&nbsp;&nbsp;
even with Jenner’s astounding breakthrough,&nbsp;
immunology didn’t really take off as a discipline&nbsp;&nbsp;
until scientists began using experiments to&nbsp;
uncover the major components of the immune system.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The Russian zoologist Elie Metchnikoff discovered&nbsp;
phagocytes, which are cells that consume things&nbsp;&nbsp;
like other cells. In a landmark experiment in&nbsp;
1883, Metchnikoff pushed a rose thorn into a&nbsp;&nbsp;
transparent starfish larva. After a few hours, he&nbsp;
noticed that cells had rushed in and surrounded&nbsp;&nbsp;
the thorn, and he correctly hypothesized&nbsp;
that these cells could respond similarly&nbsp;&nbsp;
to an infectious threat. His theory became known&nbsp;
as the “cellular theory of immunity,” which states&nbsp;&nbsp;
that immune protection from foreign invaders is&nbsp;
primarily carried out by cells like phagocytes.&nbsp;
A few years later, in 1890, Emil von Behring&nbsp;
and Shibasaburo Kitasato discovered another&nbsp;&nbsp;
element of the immune system. They realized that&nbsp;
after an animal had been infected with diphtheria&nbsp;&nbsp;
or tetanus, their serum contained a powerful&nbsp;
antitoxin, that, if transferred to a new animal,&nbsp;&nbsp;
could protect it from future infection. This&nbsp;
phenomenon is called “passive immunity.”&nbsp;&nbsp;
Although they didn’t know this at the time,&nbsp;
the “antitoxin” they discovered was antibodies,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which are specialized proteins made by the immune&nbsp;
system that bind to specific targets, keeping&nbsp;&nbsp;
them from being able to enter cells, while also&nbsp;
flagging them for destruction by immune cells.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Their discovery led to a rival theory,&nbsp;
the “humoral theory of immunity,”&nbsp;&nbsp;
which said that the protective elements of the&nbsp;
immune system could be found in bodily fluids,&nbsp;&nbsp;
sometimes referred to as humors, such as serum.
Whether immunity was carried out by cells or by&nbsp;&nbsp;
humors was the subject of intense debate for&nbsp;
many years. As technology developed, we began&nbsp;&nbsp;
to understand that the immune system is made up of&nbsp;
both cellular components and humoral components,&nbsp;&nbsp;
or as we now call them, soluble components, and&nbsp;
that these work together to provide immunity.&nbsp;
During this series, we will study the tissues,&nbsp;
cells, and molecules that make up the immune&nbsp;&nbsp;
system. We will work to understand what the&nbsp;
major components of the immune system are,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and precisely how they work together&nbsp;

In doing this, we will talk about how barrier&nbsp;
surfaces like the skin, stomach lining,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and even tears are the first line of defense&nbsp;

lymph nodes and the spleen, where white&nbsp;
blood cells can sense if there’s an infection&nbsp;&nbsp;
in a different part of the body. We will perform&nbsp;
an in depth analysis of the different kinds of&nbsp;&nbsp;
immune cells, which have highly specialized roles&nbsp;
for detecting and destroying different kinds of&nbsp;&nbsp;
threats. We will learn about how white blood cells&nbsp;

During this series, we will also talk about&nbsp;

And finally, throughout this series, we will&nbsp;

We want to understand what happens when&nbsp;

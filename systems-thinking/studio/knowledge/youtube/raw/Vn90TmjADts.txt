One of the problems with climate change
is&nbsp;that the worse it gets the worse it gets.
Let's find out why and how nature&nbsp;
could help reverse this trend.
Earth's carbon is stored in three main&nbsp;pools:
land, the oceans, and the atmosphere.
The total amount of carbon doesn't change&nbsp;
but it does move around between these pools.&nbsp;
That's called the carbon cycle. For instance&nbsp;
plants absorb carbon from the atmosphere and use&nbsp;&nbsp;
it to make leaves, stems, and roots. And when these&nbsp;
plants die all of that carbon gets released back&nbsp;&nbsp;
into the atmosphere but some of the carbon&nbsp;
from decaying plants and animals actually&nbsp;&nbsp;
gets locked in the ground and it can stay&nbsp;
there for hundreds or even thousands of years.&nbsp;
With enough time they can even form substances like oil.
Humans have introduced a big change into the carbon cycle.
Burning fossil fuels moves carbon&nbsp;that was
stored underground into the atmosphere.
That extra carbon traps heat in our atmosphere,&nbsp;
setting new cycles into motion, leading to what&nbsp;&nbsp;
scientists call feedback loops. These can either&nbsp;
accelerate or slow the rate of climate change.
For instance, when frozen soil thaws&nbsp;,
dormant microorganisms become active.
They decompose thawed plant material and release&nbsp;
carbon from the soil into the atmosphere which&nbsp;&nbsp;
then traps even more heat and accelerates warming.&nbsp;
That's an example of a positive feedback loop: a&nbsp;&nbsp;
vicious cycle that increases warming. That's why&nbsp;
the worse climate change gets the worse it gets.
Even our own behavior is caught&nbsp;
in a positive feedback loop&nbsp;
Burning fossil fuels for energy has transformed&nbsp;
human life by making us more productive.
The more productive we are, the more&nbsp;
our economies and populations grow,&nbsp;&nbsp;
causing us to burn even more fossil fuels.&nbsp;
So, how do we get out of these vicious cycles?
Luckily, negative feedback loops are virtuous&nbsp;
cycles that can help cool the climate.
For example, higher temperatures and more&nbsp;
CO2 in the atmosphere can actually fuel&nbsp;
plant growth in some areas. This means&nbsp;
that healthy diverse ecosystems have the&nbsp;&nbsp;
potential to absorb some of that excess&nbsp;
carbon and help re-stabilize the climate.
These negative feedback loops aren't&nbsp;
strong enough to solve climate change&nbsp;&nbsp;
but they can help slow global warming while&nbsp;
we transition to fossil fuel free economies.
If we work with powerful helpful feedback&nbsp;
loops, we can prevent climate change from&nbsp;
spiraling out of control. First we must cut&nbsp;
back on fossil fuels and protect diverse&nbsp;
ecosystems that can draw down excess carbon,&nbsp;
reducing how much heat the atmosphere traps.&nbsp;
Those actions will help re-stabilize the&nbsp;
carbon cycle and ultimately, the climate.

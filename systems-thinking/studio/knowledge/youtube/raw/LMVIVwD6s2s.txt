The word thermodynamics has its origin
in two Greek words. The word thermy
means heat and the word dynamis means
motion or force or power. So if we
combine both, thermodynamics literally
means the study of motion or power or
force due to heat. But in science,
thermodynamics is much more than just
heat. In simple words, thermodynamics
studies how energy flows in or out of
any system when it underos some physical
or chemical change. It tries to explain
what happens to energy when something
gets heated, cooled, expanded,
compressed, or even reacts chemically.
Think about a cup of hot coffee. When it
is left on a table, after some time it
becomes cooler. Why? Because heat from
the coffee flows to the surrounding air.
The study of this heat flow, how much
heat goes out, why it goes out, and what
changes happen in both the coffee and
the air is actually what thermodynamics
is all about. Similarly, when a car
engine runs, it burns fuel to release
heat, which is then used to do work like
moving the car forward. Thermodynamics
helps us understand how efficiently that
heat is turned into motion or why some
energy is always lost in the process of
converting it into work. Now to study
thermodynamics properly, we need to
define a few key terms. A system in
thermodynamics is the specific part of
the universe that we want to focus on.
For example, if we are studying the
boiling of water in a pot, the water
inside the pot is our system. The
surroundings are everything outside the
system which can affect it or be
affected by it. In this case, the air
around the pot, the stove, the table,
and so on. The boundary is what
separates the system from the
surroundings. In our boiling water
example, the walls of the pot and the
surface of the water act as boundaries.
Sometimes this boundary is real and
visible like the pot wall. Other times
it can be imaginary like if we imagine a
bubble of air in a room as our system.
The boundary can also have different
properties. It can be rigid or flexible
like a balloon surface that can expand.
It can be permeable meaning matter can
pass through it or non-permeable where
matter cannot cross. It can also be
adiabatic meaning no heat passes through
it like a thermos flask or nonadiabatic
where heat can pass easily. Finally when
we combine both the system and the
surroundings we get the universe. So the
universe is simply the system plus the
surroundings together. Now depending on
how a system interacts with its
surroundings, it can be classified into
three main types. Open, closed, and
isolated systems. An open system is one
which can exchange both matter and
energy with its surroundings. A very
simple example is a boiling pot of water
without a lid. Steam goes out, that's
the exchange of matter, and heat flows
in or out. That's the exchange of
energy. A closed system can exchange
only energy, not matter. For example, if
you have a sealed metal container of
gas, heat can flow in or out of it, but
the gas itself stays inside. Lastly, an
isolated system can neither exchange
matter nor energy with its surroundings.
A perfect thermos flask is an example of
an isolated system because ideally no
heat enters or leaves it and no matter
crosses its boundary noise. Now there is
another way to classify systems and that
is based on their composition. In this
way systems can be either homogeneous or
heterogeneous. A homogeneous system is
one that is uniform throughout, which
means it has the same composition and
properties in every part. A single solid
like a block of copper, a pure liquid
like distilled water, or a uniform
mixture of gases like air are all
homogeneous systems. A heterogeneous
system, on the other hand, has more than
one phase and is not uniform throughout.
For example, ice floating in water is a
heterogeneous system because there are
two distinct phases, solid ice and
liquid water. The chemical composition
may be the same, but the physical states
are different. Another example is oil
and water in the same container. They do
not mix and form two separate layers,
making it a heterogeneous system. Once
we know what a system is, the next step
is to describe it completely. To do
this, we use thermodynamic properties.
These properties are measurable
quantities that define the condition or
state of the system. They are like the
fingerprints of a system because once
you know them, you can tell exactly what
state the system is in. Examples of such
properties include pressure, volume,
temperature, internal energy, and
enalpy.
Pressure, volume, and temperature are
the three most fundamental properties
used to describe the state of any
thermodynamic system, especially gases.
Thermodynamic properties are divided
into two main types, intensive
properties and extensive properties. An
intensive property is one that does not
depend on the amount of substance
present. It depends only on the nature
of the material. For example,
temperature, pressure, density, color,
viscosity, refractive index, and
specific heat capacity are all intensive
properties. Imagine a small cup of pure
water and a large bucket of pure water.
Even though the amounts are different,
the temperature, density, color, and
refractive index will all be the same.
So these properties do not change with
size or quantity. On the other hand,
extensive properties depend on the
amount of substance present. The more
material you have, the greater the value
of the property. Examples include mass,
volume, energy, enthalpy, internal
energy, and heat capacity. If you double
the amount of a substance, its mass,
volume, and total internal energy also
double. Thus, extensive properties are
additive. Next, we come to the idea of
the state of a system. The state of a
thermodynamic system means its exact
condition at a specific time. This
condition can be completely described by
certain measurable quantities called
state variables or state parameters.
These variables include pressure,
volume, temperature, and composition.
For example, if we have a gas in a
cylinder with a movable piston, its
condition can be described by its
pressure, volume, and temperature. If
the gas is at a certain pressure P1,
volume V1, and temperature T1, we say it
is in state one. Now if we heat the gas
or compress it, its pressure, volume and
temperature will change to new values
say P2, V2 and T2 and it will be in
state two. Thus a change in any state
variable means a change in the state of
the system. The properties that define
the state of a system are known as state
variables and the relationship between
them is described by an equation of
state. For example, for an ideal gas,
the relationship between pressure,
volume, and temperature can be expressed
by the ideal gas equation. But we will
come to that later. For now, just
understand that these variables together
tell us how the system is behaving. Now,
the next important concept in
thermodynamics is the difference between
a state function and a path function. A
state function is a property whose value
depends only on the current state of the
system, not on how it reached that
state. In other words, it depends only
on where you are, not on the route you
took to get there. Imagine climbing a
mountain. Whether you take a steep path
or a winding one, your altitude at the
top is the same. Altitude depends only
on your position, not on the path.
Therefore, altitude is like a state
function. Similarly, in thermodynamics,
quantities like pressure, volume,
temperature, internal energy, enthalpy,
and entropy are all state functions
because they depend only on the state of
the system. A path function on the other
hand depends on the specific path or
process by which the system changes from
one state to another. A simple analogy
is the distance you travel. Suppose
there are two cities A and B. The
straight road between them is 100 km
long. But there might be another route
that goes around hills and is 150 km
long or another that is 200 km long. The
distance you travel depends on the path
you choose even though your start and
end points are fixed. Similarly, in
thermodynamics, quantities like heat and
work are path functions because they
depend on how the process happens, not
just on the initial and final states.
For example, if you compress a gas
slowly or quickly, the amount of work
done and heat transferred will differ
even if the starting and ending
pressures and volumes are the same. In
thermodynamics, it is often said that
heat and work are not properties of the
system. They are modes of energy
transfer. Once the process is over, the
system may have more or less internal
energy, but we cannot say how much heat
it contains. Heat and work only exist
while energy is being transferred. And
they depend on the path of transfer. Let
us now connect these ideas to real life.
Every time you heat food, run an air
conditioner, drive a car, or even drink
cold water, you are observing
thermodynamics in action. When ice
melts, it absorbs heat from its
surroundings. When you compress air into
a tire, work is done on the gas and its
temperature rises. When you burn fuel in
an engine, chemical energy is converted
into heat and then into mechanical work.
Each of these changes involves the flow
or transformation of energy. Exactly
what thermodynamics studies.
Thermodynamics does not deal with
individual molecules. It deals with
large groups of them looking at their
overall behavior. That is why we call it
a macroscopic science. It studies bulk
properties like pressure and temperature
rather than the motion of each molecule.

One must imagine Sisyphus&nbsp;
happy. This is the final line of writer and&nbsp;&nbsp;
philosopher Albert Camus’s famous work, The Myth&nbsp;
of Sisyphus. You've likely seen or heard it before&nbsp;&nbsp;
even if you’re not familiar with his work, as this&nbsp;
line has transcended its origins and can now be&nbsp;&nbsp;
found in comment sections and memes all across the&nbsp;
internet, pasted into different contexts, images,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and conversations. But what exactly does this&nbsp;
line mean? What exactly was Camus trying to say?&nbsp;&nbsp;
Sisyphus is a Greek king condemned by the gods&nbsp;
to roll a boulder up a hill, only for it to roll&nbsp;&nbsp;
back down as soon as he approaches the top,&nbsp;
over and over, for eternity. Why would we&nbsp;&nbsp;
imagine such a person happy? For what reasons&nbsp;
would he be? Is Camus wrong? Is Sisyphus dumb?&nbsp;
At its core, the line is a sort of hymn for&nbsp;
the school of thought known as absurdism—a&nbsp;&nbsp;
philosophy that Camus is largely credited with&nbsp;
defining. The philosophy can essentially be&nbsp;&nbsp;
summed up with the following: Humanity has&nbsp;
an innate desire for meaning and truth—a&nbsp;&nbsp;
rational understanding of everything—yet the&nbsp;
universe appears entirely devoid of these things.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The Absurd is found in the conflict&nbsp;
between humanity’s desire and what is—our&nbsp;&nbsp;
yearning for rationality meeting the universe’s&nbsp;
irrationality, our pleading for meaning&nbsp;&nbsp;
and the universe’s cold silence. Camus writes:
In a universe suddenly divested of illusions&nbsp;&nbsp;
and lights, man feels an alien, a&nbsp;
stranger. His exile is without remedy&nbsp;&nbsp;
since he is deprived of the memory of a&nbsp;
lost home or the hope of a promised land.
Camus chooses Sisyphus as the quintessential&nbsp;
allegorical embodiment of this absurd condition.&nbsp;&nbsp;
He uses the myth to explore&nbsp;
and arrive at what he argues&nbsp;&nbsp;
is the resolution to humanity’s&nbsp;
confrontation with the Absurd.&nbsp;&nbsp;
To better understand where, how, and what&nbsp;
Camus arrives at—and whether it suffices—let’s&nbsp;&nbsp;
start with a brief summary of Sisyphus’s story.
***&nbsp;
In Greek mythology, Sisyphus was the founder&nbsp;
and king of Ephyra, now known as Corinth.&nbsp;&nbsp;
He was an extremely clever but&nbsp;
tyrannical king, often killing visitors&nbsp;&nbsp;
and inappropriately seducing women. His hubris was&nbsp;
so immense, he also often meddled with the gods.&nbsp;
In one case, he undermined the sky and thunder&nbsp;
god Zeus by conspiring with the river god Asopus.&nbsp;&nbsp;
This didn’t go over very well. Sisyphus’s behavior&nbsp;
would eventually lead Zeus to having him taken&nbsp;&nbsp;
to the underworld. Sisyphus, however, being&nbsp;
as clever as he was, fooled the god of death&nbsp;&nbsp;
Thanatos by convincing him to demonstrate&nbsp;
how the chains for his punishment worked.&nbsp;&nbsp;
In the process, Sisyphus bound the chains&nbsp;
around Thanatos and escaped the underworld.&nbsp;
After much commotion and chaos as a result of&nbsp;
Sisyphus’s actions, trapping the god of death,&nbsp;&nbsp;
he knew that his escape would eventually come&nbsp;
to a head once Thanatos was freed. And so,&nbsp;&nbsp;
Sisyphus conceived of another plan to avoid facing&nbsp;
any consequences. He ordered his wife Merope to&nbsp;&nbsp;
toss his body into the public square once he died&nbsp;
of natural causes. His wife did as instructed.&nbsp;
Once back in the underworld, Sisyphus informed&nbsp;
the Queen Persephone that he had been disrespected&nbsp;&nbsp;
by not receiving a proper burial. His plan&nbsp;
worked, and Persephone allowed Sisyphus to&nbsp;&nbsp;
return to the land of the living to punish his&nbsp;
wife, so long as he immediately returned after.&nbsp;
Sisyphus did not return.
He believed he had triumphed over death&nbsp;&nbsp;
and outsmarted the gods yet again, but Sisyphus&nbsp;
would soon realize the gods can be tricked but&nbsp;&nbsp;
not beaten. Zeus would catch Sisyphus and&nbsp;
punish him once and for all, where now,&nbsp;&nbsp;
he could no longer escape or outsmart anything.&nbsp;
Enforced by divine authority, his punishment—the&nbsp;&nbsp;
absurd condition the modern world has come to&nbsp;
know him for: rolling a massive boulder up a hill,&nbsp;&nbsp;
where every time he approaches the top, it rolls&nbsp;
back down to the bottom, and he must start again,&nbsp;&nbsp;
over and over, forever.
Sisyphus is a striking choice for Camus to&nbsp;
embody the condition humanity finds itself&nbsp;&nbsp;
in—particularly in the modern world. So much of&nbsp;
life is comprised of tiny boulders reminiscent&nbsp;&nbsp;
of Sisyphus’s plight—from cleaning the house and&nbsp;
paying the bills, to day-to-day routines and work,&nbsp;&nbsp;
to career benchmarks, social achievements, and&nbsp;
relationships, to humanity’s technological and&nbsp;&nbsp;
scientific progress. These all have a kind looping&nbsp;
futility to them. They fail, end, or lead to new&nbsp;&nbsp;
problems. Even if we accomplish them, we find&nbsp;
ourselves essentially back where we started:&nbsp;&nbsp;
bored, confused, and desiring of something&nbsp;
we can’t quite satiate or put our finger on.&nbsp;&nbsp;
And of course, all will ultimately be crushed&nbsp;
under the weight of the one giant boulder that&nbsp;&nbsp;
is life itself. Everything we do and accomplish,&nbsp;
everyone we love, everything we learn, obtain,&nbsp;&nbsp;
or think we’ve made sense of, will turn to dust&nbsp;
underneath the boulder of our mortal transience.&nbsp;&nbsp;
And if we see no obvious afterlife, no obvious&nbsp;
order in the universe, no ultimate answers&nbsp;&nbsp;
to our why questions, it all seems to go&nbsp;
nowhere—back down the hill. Camus writes:&nbsp;
To work and create “for nothing,” to sculpture in&nbsp;
clay, to know that one’s creation has no future,&nbsp;&nbsp;
to see one’s work destroyed in a day while&nbsp;
being aware that fundamentally this has no more&nbsp;&nbsp;
importance than building for centuries—this is the&nbsp;
difficult wisdom that absurd thought sanctions.
&nbsp;
So back to the question: If this is Sisyphus’s&nbsp;
story and eternal condition, and this is&nbsp;&nbsp;
what Camus is paralleling to humanity’s&nbsp;
condition, why must one imagine him happy?&nbsp;&nbsp;
What about this condition gives any reason&nbsp;
to believe in the possibility of happiness?&nbsp;
There are, of course, many interpretations one&nbsp;
can make about Sisyphus’s psychosocial state and&nbsp;&nbsp;
Camus’s philosophical intention. It’s helpful to&nbsp;
first ground those interpretations in the cultural&nbsp;&nbsp;
and historical context in which Camus was writing&nbsp;
in. Born in French Algeria in 1913, he grew up&nbsp;&nbsp;
during a period of immense cultural volatility and&nbsp;
change. Between the World Wars and the increasing&nbsp;&nbsp;
collapse of societal structures, Camus existed&nbsp;
in a world comprising heavy hearts and unstable&nbsp;&nbsp;
ground. In adulthood, he had become a major&nbsp;
figure a part of the Existentialist movement,&nbsp;&nbsp;
helping form its shape and popularity alongside&nbsp;
the likes of philosophers like Jean-Paul Sartre,&nbsp;&nbsp;
Simone de Beauvoir, and Martin Heidegger.&nbsp;
Fundamentally, the existentialist view is&nbsp;&nbsp;
that life has no inherent meaning, and meaning&nbsp;
can only be forged subjectively through human&nbsp;&nbsp;
choice and action, rather than discovered as an&nbsp;
external truth. In the words of Jean-Paul Sarte,&nbsp;&nbsp;
“In life, a man commits himself, draws his own&nbsp;
portrait and there is nothing but that portrait.”&nbsp;
During this time, humanity began to find&nbsp;
itself increasingly consumed by a shadow&nbsp;&nbsp;
cast by the carcasses of progress and religion.&nbsp;
Nihilism was taking the form of this shadow,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and existentialism and subsequently absurdism&nbsp;
were trying to find the edges of light.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Afterall, it’s one thing to endure the pains and&nbsp;
hardships of existence when you believe there is a&nbsp;&nbsp;
grand purpose to everything, when a heaven awaits,&nbsp;
when meaning is there for you in your first breath&nbsp;&nbsp;
and carrying you softly unto your last. But&nbsp;
when the fabric tears, dropping you into the&nbsp;&nbsp;
abyss of uncertainty and unintelligibility,&nbsp;
what are you left to do but fall into despair?&nbsp;&nbsp;
The things you can’t understand or do,&nbsp;
the struggles and terrors of existence,&nbsp;&nbsp;
all retain their pain but lose their meaning.
Camus’s attempt to deal with this fall—to&nbsp;&nbsp;
weave a kind of non-lethal safety&nbsp;
net—was through absurdism. He writes:&nbsp;
The realization that life is absurd&nbsp;
cannot be an end, but only a beginning.&nbsp;&nbsp;
This is a truth nearly all great minds&nbsp;
have taken as their starting point. It is&nbsp;&nbsp;
not this discovery that is interesting, but the&nbsp;
consequences and rules of action drawn from it.
Camus’s broader argument with Sisyphus is that&nbsp;
rather than despairing over his condition,&nbsp;&nbsp;
if Sisyphus acknowledges it with lucidity,&nbsp;
recognizing and embracing it’s total absurdity,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and refuses to depend on grand illusions about&nbsp;
some better tomorrow in which the boulder will&nbsp;&nbsp;
hold or a new life will come, and continues&nbsp;
on anyway, he triumphs over his condition.&nbsp;&nbsp;
He revolts against it with his happiness. That&nbsp;
is, his happiness is part and parcel of his&nbsp;&nbsp;
triumph and revolt, rather than an outcome of it.
Likewise, for Camus, we should revolt against our&nbsp;&nbsp;
futile condition—against nihilism—with a kind of&nbsp;
defiant joy; an endeavor to spiritedly continue&nbsp;&nbsp;
not in denial of the absurdity of our lives but in&nbsp;
the face of it, embracing it without languishing.&nbsp;
This is perhaps where one might find themselves&nbsp;
unsatisfied with such a conclusion. On the&nbsp;&nbsp;
surface, without further context, analysis,&nbsp;
or interpretation, this can almost feel like&nbsp;&nbsp;
a form of just be happy advice. What if one&nbsp;
can’t imagine Sisyphus happy? What if one&nbsp;&nbsp;
can’t imagine themselves happy? Where does one go&nbsp;
from there? Is it hopeless? As I write in my book,&nbsp;&nbsp;
The Terrible Paradox of Self-Awareness:
We want more than the individual meaning&nbsp;&nbsp;
and purpose we imagine for ourselves;&nbsp;
we want more than to imagine we are&nbsp;&nbsp;
happy . . . we want the boulder to go&nbsp;
somewhere else, to mean something else.
Part of the problem, it seems, is Camus’s choice&nbsp;
of words here. “Happy” simply does not appear&nbsp;&nbsp;
to be the appropriate term. And within the work,&nbsp;
Camus does not clearly define it to mean anything&nbsp;&nbsp;
beyond the definition we tend to associate&nbsp;
it with. That is, the feeling of pleasure.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Even a broader definition of happiness,&nbsp;
like content, still seems a bit off—at&nbsp;&nbsp;
least on its own. Sisyphus is both in and aware&nbsp;
of a wretched condition. And he’s not a fool.&nbsp;&nbsp;
He’s smart to a fault. And continuing to strive to&nbsp;
do something over and over, by force or otherwise,&nbsp;&nbsp;
seems somewhat antithetical to contentment—at&nbsp;
least without imagining other things about him.&nbsp;&nbsp;
And perhaps that’s what we should do.
Rather than mere happiness, Sisyphus’s&nbsp;&nbsp;
ideal and realistic state can be understood&nbsp;
best through total acceptance. Camus writes:&nbsp;
The absurd man thus catches sight of a burning&nbsp;
and frigid, transparent and limited universe in&nbsp;&nbsp;
which nothing is possible but everything is given,&nbsp;
and beyond which all is collapse and nothingness.&nbsp;&nbsp;
He can then decide to accept such a&nbsp;
universe and draw from it his strength,&nbsp;&nbsp;
his refusal to hope, and the unyielding&nbsp;
evidence of a life without consolation.
Sisyphus has no choice in his condition. And so,&nbsp;
his choices become limited to the framework of his&nbsp;&nbsp;
perspective and attention. Though it is arguably&nbsp;
a big leap to suggest that from here, he can&nbsp;&nbsp;
brute force his way to happiness through merely&nbsp;
withstanding or revolting against his condition,&nbsp;&nbsp;
there is something profound still to be&nbsp;
maintained here. In radical acceptance,&nbsp;&nbsp;
Sisyphus can begin relieving the tension&nbsp;
between himself and his conditions, freeing&nbsp;&nbsp;
up his mental space and energy to determine&nbsp;
a better way to engage with his conditions.&nbsp;
This approach is supported in modern&nbsp;
psychotherapy with an evidence-based model,&nbsp;&nbsp;
ACT (Acceptance and Commitment Therapy). The&nbsp;
model works by first encouraging individuals to&nbsp;&nbsp;
open up to and accept their negative feelings and&nbsp;
thoughts, rather than resisting them. In doing so,&nbsp;&nbsp;
it is found that one can form a better&nbsp;
relationship with their thoughts and feelings,&nbsp;&nbsp;
creating a kind of distance between them and one’s&nbsp;
awareness or sense of self. Thoughts and feelings&nbsp;&nbsp;
become like passing objects in a sea of mental and&nbsp;
bodily activity of which the individual observes&nbsp;&nbsp;
rather than controls or attaches to. This approach&nbsp;
to self-awareness is what is known in the model as&nbsp;&nbsp;
self-as-context, which differs from the&nbsp;
more typical conception of oneself as&nbsp;&nbsp;
being one and the same as their thoughts and&nbsp;
feelings, which is known as self-as-content.&nbsp;&nbsp;
For Sisyphus, self-as-content might&nbsp;
entail an attitude like the following:&nbsp;&nbsp;
I am rolling this rock up this hill for no reason.&nbsp;
What I am doing is pointless. I’m pointless.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Everything is pointless. Whereas self-as-context&nbsp;
might entail something like: I am experiencing&nbsp;&nbsp;
thoughts about why I’m rolling this rock up this&nbsp;
hill for no reason. But my thoughts are not me,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and this condition is something I am observing.&nbsp;
This may seem like a semantic sleight of hand,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and that’s fair. But in the latter, there is a&nbsp;
gap created between phenomena and perception.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Awareness. And within this gap, we can change&nbsp;
the quality of our responses to our experiences.&nbsp;&nbsp;
In some cases, when we can, this might mean&nbsp;
stopping rolling the rock up the hill. In other&nbsp;&nbsp;
cases, it might mean orienting our perception&nbsp;
differently about the process of rolling the rock.&nbsp;
ACT suggests that we calibrate our&nbsp;
awareness according to our values—be&nbsp;&nbsp;
it curiosity, creativity, wonder, or compassion.&nbsp;&nbsp;
In other words, once we’ve accepted our&nbsp;
condition (or at least what we cannot change),&nbsp;&nbsp;
we still can and must decide what we should&nbsp;
do and what we should care about within our&nbsp;&nbsp;
constraints. In this, we paradoxically&nbsp;
reclaim ownership over our situations.&nbsp;&nbsp;
We go from using our energy to resist or deny&nbsp;
reality, or attempting to withstand it heroically,&nbsp;&nbsp;
to flowing with it and orienting ourselves&nbsp;
in simultaneously real and productive terms.&nbsp;
Perhaps Sisyphus might try to&nbsp;
be the best rock roller he can,&nbsp;&nbsp;
paying close attention to each step and each push,&nbsp;
getting better and better each time. Perhaps he&nbsp;&nbsp;
might try to consider as many perspectives and&nbsp;
interpretations as he can during each roll—every&nbsp;&nbsp;
angle of all the aspects and details of the&nbsp;
landscape around him. Perhaps he might try to&nbsp;&nbsp;
engage in the wonder and nature of the situation,&nbsp;
with no dependency on ever comprehending it fully.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Perhaps he might pride himself on not&nbsp;
harming any bug or creature on the hill.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Perhaps he might make ornate shaping—art—out&nbsp;
of the paths formed by the rock rolling.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Perhaps he might try to forget the rock and&nbsp;
focus on each step, each breath, each moment,&nbsp;&nbsp;
moving with the utmost presence, each push a&nbsp;
kind of mantra for the silence of all things.&nbsp;
Crucially, though this parallels&nbsp;
Camus’s philosophical framework,&nbsp;&nbsp;
it arguably isn’t happiness we are left with&nbsp;
nor in pursuit of. Rather, it is sort of a&nbsp;&nbsp;
non-attachment to happiness and unhappiness. It&nbsp;
is a movement toward the frequent dissolvement&nbsp;&nbsp;
of the very question of happiness and unhappiness.&nbsp;
“Am I happy? is one of those questions that, if it&nbsp;&nbsp;
has got to be asked, more or less dictates its&nbsp;
own answer,” wrote author David Foster Wallace.&nbsp;
To be fair, Camus is arguably producing a work&nbsp;
of literature here as much as he is philosophy.&nbsp;&nbsp;
In fact, Camus rejected the&nbsp;
title of philosopher, stating,&nbsp;&nbsp;
“I am not a philosopher, because I don’t believe&nbsp;
in reason enough to believe in a system.”&nbsp;&nbsp;
Any ambiguity or contradiction in&nbsp;
his work is likely, in some sense,&nbsp;&nbsp;
intentional (or at least accepted) for the very&nbsp;
purposes of further interpretation. This openness&nbsp;&nbsp;
and provocation of thought leaves room to both&nbsp;
critique and affirm his ideas simultaneously.&nbsp;
Like with Camus’s conclusion, this still isn’t&nbsp;
a resolution of nihilism or meaninglessness.&nbsp;&nbsp;
We are still left without consolation in&nbsp;
the form of objective meaning or truth.&nbsp;&nbsp;
But it is, arguably, a more&nbsp;
stable kind of resolution—a&nbsp;&nbsp;
paradoxical relief from the dependency on&nbsp;
any ultimate meaning, truth, and happiness.&nbsp;
In the end, like Sisyphus, we have no&nbsp;
choice. We have no choice in being born,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the nature of our minds and bodies, and&nbsp;
the conditions of the world and universe.&nbsp;&nbsp;
We too must accept what is happening, what we&nbsp;
find ourselves in. We too must be aware of the&nbsp;&nbsp;
phenomena comprising an existence inside a human&nbsp;
inside a world. It is our blessing and our curse.&nbsp;&nbsp;
To see, conceptualize, and hope but to never&nbsp;
control, resolve, or know. But we can still choose&nbsp;&nbsp;
values within the space of our awareness without&nbsp;
having to ground them in objective meaning;&nbsp;&nbsp;
and we can still use them to orientate ourselves&nbsp;
toward the heights not of a hill but of being.&nbsp;
Perhaps, in the end, the line could&nbsp;
read: One must imagine Sisyphus present.&nbsp;&nbsp;
One must imagine him focused. One must imagine him&nbsp;
enthralled with wonder. One must imagine Sisyphus,&nbsp;&nbsp;
even if only for brief moments, not&nbsp;
imagining anything at all, but simply being.
One of the most important things we can do in life&nbsp;
is learn how to better understand and live with&nbsp;&nbsp;
our awareness, the way we experience ourselves&nbsp;
and the world. This can often be painful and&nbsp;&nbsp;
convoluted, but if faced headon, it can also lead&nbsp;
to greater depths and qualities of experience,&nbsp;&nbsp;
helping us turn our self-awareness from a source&nbsp;
of constant overthinking, anxiety, regret,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and suffering to a source of wonder and poise. My&nbsp;
new book, The Terrible Paradox of Self-awareness,&nbsp;&nbsp;
attempts to do just that. It's a kind of&nbsp;
philosophical thought map that pulls from my years&nbsp;&nbsp;
of research on various schools of thought from&nbsp;
Dowoism to Buddhism to existentialism to absurdism&nbsp;&nbsp;
and more. All synthesized into one unique&nbsp;
exploration of awareness [music] and existence.&nbsp;&nbsp;
It doesn't sugarcoat things and it doesn't provide&nbsp;
easy answers, but it attempts to engage with&nbsp;&nbsp;
existence through a kind of compassion, openness,&nbsp;

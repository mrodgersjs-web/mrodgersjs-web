This is a version of a famous thought experiment&nbsp;&nbsp;
in game theory known as the prisoner’s dilemma—a&nbsp;
situation where two people would be better off&nbsp;&nbsp;
cooperating, but each person has some incentive&nbsp;
to go against the other. In both not cooperating,&nbsp;&nbsp;
however, both end up worse off. In this case, the&nbsp;
individual incentive is not spending time doing&nbsp;&nbsp;
the dishes. The outcome is either a messy kitchen&nbsp;
and messy roommate situation or a clean one.&nbsp;
Broadly, game theory is the mathematical study&nbsp;
of decision making and strategies in situations&nbsp;&nbsp;
where outcomes depend on others’ choices. More&nbsp;
specifically, it examines the nature and effects&nbsp;&nbsp;
of how conflict and cooperation among rational&nbsp;
decision-makers can lead to optimal or suboptimal&nbsp;&nbsp;
payoffs. In essence, it is a science of strategy.
In social situations, in business, in economics,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and in politics—in every interaction—whether&nbsp;
between as little as two people or as many as&nbsp;&nbsp;
nations, decisions are constantly being made&nbsp;
that affect everyone involved. As individuals&nbsp;&nbsp;
and collectives, we each possess the power to&nbsp;
not only change our own circumstances but also&nbsp;&nbsp;
the circumstances of others—of the world. These&nbsp;
decisions and their outcomes can be as benign as&nbsp;&nbsp;
who does the dishes in an apartment to as critical&nbsp;
as whether a country and its citizens survive.&nbsp;
Game theory suggests that every decision made with&nbsp;
a particular aim can, in principle, be represented&nbsp;&nbsp;
and understood as a mathematical model. In other&nbsp;
words, with a clear goal and defined constraints,&nbsp;&nbsp;
a rationally right choice can always be&nbsp;
determined. More yet, an optimal strategy&nbsp;&nbsp;
can be determined across multiple choices. And&nbsp;
through various computer programs and simulations,&nbsp;&nbsp;
researchers in the field of game theory have&nbsp;
actually found a strategy (or an approach and&nbsp;&nbsp;
temperament) that, under many conditions in&nbsp;
society and nature, has proven to consistently&nbsp;&nbsp;
be extremely effective. And surprising to many in&nbsp;
the field, the strategy is profoundly simple. Even&nbsp;&nbsp;
more compelling, it is hopeful. It is something&nbsp;
that each of us can apply to our own lives.&nbsp;
Before going any further, it’s important to note&nbsp;
that in the context of game theory, a “game” is&nbsp;&nbsp;
not how we conventionally think of one—though&nbsp;
it can also include traditional games. Rather,&nbsp;&nbsp;
“game” simply refers to any interaction that&nbsp;
occurs between multiple decision-makers,&nbsp;&nbsp;
where the outcome and payoff of the interaction&nbsp;
for each individual depends on the choices made&nbsp;&nbsp;
by the others. This can include games like chess&nbsp;
and poker, but it also includes nearly everything&nbsp;&nbsp;
else. Of course, not literally everything—but&nbsp;
all direct interactions that occur between&nbsp;&nbsp;
individuals or groups that involve competition or&nbsp;
cooperation, where there is a mutually affected&nbsp;&nbsp;
outcome. And this is almost everything.
Importantly, however, game theory does&nbsp;&nbsp;
delineate two types of interactions (or games):&nbsp;
cooperative and non-cooperative. Cooperative&nbsp;&nbsp;
game theory includes dynamics like players on&nbsp;
the same sports team, roommates (in theory),&nbsp;&nbsp;
business partnerships, as well as international&nbsp;
alliances and trade agreements. In these cases,&nbsp;&nbsp;
goals are shared, resources and information are&nbsp;
often freely exchanged, and fairness and mutual&nbsp;&nbsp;
benefit is both implied and actively pursued.
Non-cooperative game theory, however,&nbsp;&nbsp;
is far more prevalent in the world—and arguably&nbsp;
much more interesting. In non-cooperative games,&nbsp;&nbsp;
there are typically winners and losers, as players&nbsp;
act independently in their own interests, making&nbsp;&nbsp;
choices intentionally to benefit themselves,&nbsp;
potentially at the expense of their opponents.&nbsp;
This sort of non-cooperative dynamic and&nbsp;
tension is often used and simplistically&nbsp;&nbsp;
reproduced in game shows. For example, in the&nbsp;
late-2000s British game show Golden Balls,&nbsp;&nbsp;
two strangers would sit across from each other&nbsp;
and decide whether they wanted to split or steal&nbsp;&nbsp;
a large sum of money with the other person. Each&nbsp;
persons’ choice directly affected whether either&nbsp;&nbsp;
individual got any money and how much, but&nbsp;
neither would know the other’s final choice&nbsp;&nbsp;
until it was revealed and locked in. If both chose&nbsp;
to split, they shared the money equally. If one&nbsp;&nbsp;
chose to split, and the other chose to steal,&nbsp;
the one who chose to steal got all the money,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and the other person got nothing. If both&nbsp;
chose to steal, neither person got anything.&nbsp;
In these sorts of one-off situations, where one&nbsp;
can either split or steal–cooperate or defect—game&nbsp;&nbsp;
theory shows us that there is a clear rational&nbsp;
choice. What is known as the dominant strategy&nbsp;&nbsp;
refers to a choice that provides a player with the&nbsp;
best results no matter what the other player does.&nbsp;&nbsp;
And this is always the most rational choice&nbsp;
to make. The choice is not about what could&nbsp;&nbsp;
lead to the best possible outcome, but about&nbsp;
choosing for the best outcome no matter what&nbsp;&nbsp;
the other person decides to do—since you have&nbsp;
no control over that. And so, in Golden Balls,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the most rational thing to do would be to always&nbsp;
steal. This is because if one person splits,&nbsp;&nbsp;
then the other person does better by stealing.&nbsp;
If one person steals, again, the other person,&nbsp;&nbsp;
in a sense, does better by stealing, because&nbsp;
they get the same amount as splitting (zero),&nbsp;&nbsp;
but are not manipulated or exploited by the&nbsp;
other person. Technically, this is what game&nbsp;&nbsp;
theorists would call a weakly dominant strategy,&nbsp;
since the literal payoff in this later situation&nbsp;&nbsp;
would be equal to splitting, rather than better.
Of course, however, life is not a gameshow.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Interactions are almost never one-offs without&nbsp;
lingering, continued effects. Decisions are rarely&nbsp;&nbsp;
as simple as splitting or stealing, and outcomes&nbsp;
are rarely as simple as half, all, or zero. In&nbsp;&nbsp;
real life, there is almost always a much greater&nbsp;
interplay with time, repeated interactions,&nbsp;&nbsp;
uncertainty, leverage, and resources. If&nbsp;
someone does or doesn’t do the dishes once,&nbsp;&nbsp;
that game is not over. The relationship and space&nbsp;
are and can be either benefited or strained,&nbsp;&nbsp;
moving forward. When a business smears or partners&nbsp;
with another business, that game is not over.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Retaliation or a growth in resources can and will&nbsp;
likely follow. When a country attacks, retaliates,&nbsp;&nbsp;
or allies with another, that game is not over.&nbsp;
Wars can begin or end. Nations can begin or end.&nbsp;
With all this in mind, what is the most effective&nbsp;
decision-making strategy (or approach and&nbsp;&nbsp;
temperament) in life in general. Is there one?
In 1980, political scientist Robert Axelrod set&nbsp;&nbsp;
out to test and answer this very question.&nbsp;
Using computer programs to model different&nbsp;&nbsp;
decision-making strategies, Axelrod orchestrated&nbsp;
an experiment. He had leading theorists from&nbsp;&nbsp;
various disciplines and places around the&nbsp;
world create and submit programs that would&nbsp;&nbsp;
compete in a tournament of an iterated&nbsp;
version of the prisoner’s dilemma. The&nbsp;&nbsp;
goal was to submit the best strategy and win.
The rules of the tournament were simple. Each&nbsp;&nbsp;
player (or program) played a single game against&nbsp;
every other player—as well as a copy of itself.&nbsp;&nbsp;
In each game, each player had the option to either&nbsp;
cooperate with or defect against their opponent.&nbsp;&nbsp;
If both players cooperated, they both received&nbsp;
three points. If one cooperated and the other&nbsp;&nbsp;
defected, the player who defected received&nbsp;
five points, and the player who cooperated&nbsp;&nbsp;
received zero. If both defected, both received&nbsp;
one point. Each individual game contained 200&nbsp;&nbsp;
rounds. The player with the most points by the&nbsp;
end of all the matchups in the tournament won.&nbsp;
In total, fourteen programs were submitted—and&nbsp;
then Axelrod added one that defected or cooperated&nbsp;&nbsp;
at random, with a 50% probability each round.&nbsp;
Most of the submitted strategies began with&nbsp;&nbsp;
cooperation, while others began with early&nbsp;
defections. Some players were complex and&nbsp;&nbsp;
calculating, probing for weakness and then&nbsp;
exploiting it—like a program called Graaskamp.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Some mixed in random moves to utilize confusion&nbsp;
and surprise–like a program called Joss. Others&nbsp;&nbsp;
were far more straightforward. Together, the&nbsp;
programs spanned from what Axelrod referred&nbsp;&nbsp;
to as simple and nice to cunning and nasty.
After the tournament concluded, Axelrod, along&nbsp;&nbsp;
with many other game theorists, found the results&nbsp;
profoundly surprising. He ran the whole tournament&nbsp;&nbsp;
again, five times over, to ensure the results were&nbsp;
dependable and repeatable. Each time, the results&nbsp;&nbsp;
were consistent, and the same winner emerged: a&nbsp;
program called tit-for-tat, which was one of the&nbsp;&nbsp;
simplest and most cooperative programs of all.
To further elevate the complexity and better&nbsp;&nbsp;
mirror real-world circumstances, Axelrod conducted&nbsp;
a second tournament. This time, there was no&nbsp;&nbsp;
defined number of total rounds per game. With it&nbsp;
now being a random, unknown number, players could&nbsp;&nbsp;
no longer track and calibrate their decisions&nbsp;
against a defined endgame. Just like reality.&nbsp;
This time, sixty-two program strategies were&nbsp;
submitted—and again, Axelrod added one that&nbsp;&nbsp;
was random. The results were very consistent to&nbsp;
the first tournament. Again, tit-for-tat won.&nbsp;
Axelrod and many other game theorists found this&nbsp;
extremely surprising because the expectation was&nbsp;&nbsp;
that the winning strategy would be either&nbsp;
highly complex, highly competitive, or both&nbsp;&nbsp;
(i.e. cunning and nasty). And yet, tit-for-tat&nbsp;
was generally simple, nice, and forgiving.&nbsp;
In terms of specific gameplay, tit-for-tat&nbsp;
always starts with cooperation. From there,&nbsp;&nbsp;
it always copies its opponent’s last move. And&nbsp;
so, it continues to cooperate unless or until&nbsp;&nbsp;
its opponent defects. At that point, tit-for-tat&nbsp;
immediately defects back and continues to do so&nbsp;&nbsp;
unless or until its opponent cooperates again. As&nbsp;
soon as its opponent cooperates again, tit-for-tat&nbsp;&nbsp;
then forgives (or no longer accounts for previous&nbsp;
moves) and returns to cooperating unless or until&nbsp;&nbsp;
its opponent defects. So on and so forth.
Interestingly, the results of this strategy&nbsp;&nbsp;
equated to tit-for-tat never winning&nbsp;
any individual games, since one-on-one,&nbsp;&nbsp;
it can only lose or draw. But across all&nbsp;
match ups and games, it cooperated with&nbsp;&nbsp;
enough other players to consistently end up with&nbsp;
the highest score overall and win the tournament.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Axelrod writes in The Evolution of Cooperation:
What accounts for TIT FOR TAT’s robust success&nbsp;&nbsp;
is its combination of being nice, retaliatory,&nbsp;
forgiving, and clear. Its niceness prevents it&nbsp;&nbsp;
from getting into unnecessary trouble. Its&nbsp;
retaliation discourages the other side from&nbsp;&nbsp;
persisting whenever defection is tried. Its&nbsp;
forgiveness helps restore mutual cooperation.&nbsp;&nbsp;
And its clarity makes it intelligible to the other&nbsp;
player, thereby eliciting long-term cooperation.
Moreover, almost all top performing players&nbsp;
in the tournaments shared in these similar&nbsp;&nbsp;
qualities. And in later simulations with&nbsp;
even more realistic, chaotic conditions,&nbsp;&nbsp;
a more generous version of tit-for-tat, that&nbsp;
occasionally forgave defections instead of&nbsp;&nbsp;
reciprocating them, proved to be even more&nbsp;
effective. Nasty players, on the other hand,&nbsp;&nbsp;
seemed to often find themselves in defecting wars&nbsp;
that lead to mutual destruction. Axelrod says:&nbsp;&nbsp;
“What makes it possible for cooperation to emerge&nbsp;
is the fact that the players might meet again.”&nbsp;
The takeaway from this is reasonable clear.&nbsp;
In continued non-cooperative, competitive&nbsp;&nbsp;
interactions—like these tournaments—it is often&nbsp;
beneficial to, at the very least, try to be nice.&nbsp;&nbsp;
To lead with niceness and cooperation. This&nbsp;
is not a weakness, but a strength. Conversely,&nbsp;&nbsp;
an individual who often leads with or insights&nbsp;
defection is more likely to weaken itself&nbsp;&nbsp;
and lose over time—even if it initially&nbsp;
appears as if they are winning. Moreover,&nbsp;&nbsp;
holding grudges is a weakness; forgiveness is a&nbsp;
strength. Of course, however, weakness itself is&nbsp;&nbsp;
a weakness. That is, letting someone do you&nbsp;
wrong without consequence will lead to being&nbsp;&nbsp;
taken advantage of and losing. One’s approach to&nbsp;
exacting consequence, however, is also important:&nbsp;&nbsp;
it must be relatively equal, consistent,&nbsp;
and clear, not opaque and manipulative.&nbsp;
From a moral and historical perspective, the&nbsp;
winning tit-for-tat strategy essentially mirrors&nbsp;&nbsp;
an eye-for-an-eye ethos. That is, justice and&nbsp;
punishment should be proportional to the harm&nbsp;&nbsp;
caused by an offense, but after proportional&nbsp;
consequence, balance and cooperation can and&nbsp;&nbsp;
should be restored. On a more individual level,&nbsp;
it essentially equates to being kind, forthright,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and understanding, but never a push-over.
Of course, there are problems and limitations&nbsp;&nbsp;
both with Axelrod’s experiment as well as&nbsp;
game theory in general. Programs, simulations,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and theories can arguably never truly reproduce,&nbsp;
model, or assess the true scale and complexity of&nbsp;&nbsp;
real-world interactions. Real interactions&nbsp;
often involve many people and many issues;&nbsp;&nbsp;
many perspectives, many goals; shifting ideas and&nbsp;
opportunities; asymmetric leverage and resources;&nbsp;&nbsp;
known and unknown information; vast errors and&nbsp;
chaos; and, perhaps most importantly, they involve&nbsp;&nbsp;
the very emotional, sentimental, spiteful, and&nbsp;
irrational nature of the human mind. As humans, we&nbsp;&nbsp;
feel and hope and believe at least as much as if&nbsp;
not more than we assess, calculate, and execute.&nbsp;
Ultimately, however, game theory teaches us&nbsp;
many important things. Perhaps one of the most&nbsp;&nbsp;
important being is that every interaction&nbsp;
and game is not always about winning.&nbsp;&nbsp;
A strategy always focused on winning can actually&nbsp;
be the least effective at winning overall—whereas&nbsp;&nbsp;
one less focused on always winning can, over&nbsp;
the long term, win. If we want to truly be&nbsp;&nbsp;
successful across various areas of life, there&nbsp;
are going to be—need to be—many instances of draws&nbsp;&nbsp;
and losses. But so long as we continue forward&nbsp;
with each new moment and each new interaction,&nbsp;&nbsp;
open and willing to try again, ensuring we stand&nbsp;
up for ourselves and hold true to our values,&nbsp;&nbsp;
while striving to meet and unite with the&nbsp;
world around us, we can steadily and surely&nbsp;&nbsp;
move toward bigger, more important wins—wins&nbsp;
of cooperation, kindness, and mutual benefit.&nbsp;
We can never truly know or control if people will&nbsp;
cooperate or defect with us, but we can know and&nbsp;&nbsp;
control if we will and why. And we can know that&nbsp;
each decision we make will likely influence the&nbsp;&nbsp;
nature and outcome of all the games in which we&nbsp;
participate—present and future—potentially making&nbsp;&nbsp;
or breaking relationships, goals, systems,&nbsp;
or even society and the planet. And so,&nbsp;&nbsp;
at least for starters, for own sake, when our&nbsp;
day comes, let’s be sure we do the dishes.

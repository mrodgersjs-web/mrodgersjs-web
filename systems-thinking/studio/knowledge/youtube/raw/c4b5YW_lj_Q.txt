When it comes to risk assessment
in critical engineering and safety systems,
choosing the right methodology is
critical for accurate analysis and
effective decision-making.
In this step, we will explore how
error tree analysis (FTA) compares with the effects of failure mode
analysis (FMECA) and
Bowtie significance analysis (FMECA).  Methodologies serve various
purposes in
risk management, each with its own unique strengths and limitations.
Fault Tree Analysis (FTA) is a
hierarchical and straightforward approach that
uses a deductive method to identify the
potential causes of higher-level system failures.
It begins with a single
undesirable event at the top and
systematically identifies contributing causes
through logic gates and XOR/OR.
For example, in
our elevator reliability project, we use FTA to map failure modes
such as power outage control system failures and
mechanical failures, working our way back
to the root causes. The
moving fault tree provides a clear, accurate, and intuitive representation of
failure pathways.
FTA enables the risk analyst to
first identify weaknesses and determine the
critical components that significantly contribute to the
probability of failure. Second, it assigns hazards
and probabilities to
intermediate and baseline events to calculate the
probability of the top event occurring.
Third, it uses complex interrelationships and
Boolean logic to represent the relationships
between subsystems. However, the
deductive nature of FTA
may overlook failure modes that are not directly related to
the identified top event. It also requires
precise input data for accurate
quantitative analysis and is less
suitable for systems with
incomplete historical data. An
alternative methodology, FMECA, provides a
comprehensive bottom-up framework. In
contrast to FTA, FMEA is an
inductive analysis.  From the bottom up, it evaluates every
possible failure mode of the system components.
This method involves
identifying failure modes and
documenting all failure modes.  The conceivable ways in which a
component could fail, as well as assessing
the effects and determining the impact of
each failure mode on the overall system,
and finally classifying the importance and using a
matrix such as the occurrence of risk and
detectability to prioritize risks. For
example, in an elevator project,
FMECA would assess individual components such as
mechanical breakage or
control software and detailed failure modes such as
overheating or firmware corruption.
This meticulous approach ensures that no
potential failure is overlooked.
FMECA's strengths are threefold: first,
it offers comprehensive coverage, identifying all
failure modes, not just those related to a
predefined critical event; second, it
provides actionable insights, offering
specific recommendations for mitigating
critical failure modes; and third, it enables
continuous improvement, allowing for the
reduction of recurring risks by
tracking and addressing
high-priority risks.
However, FMECA's limitations are time-consuming, as it requires a
detailed examination of each component, which can be
resource-intensive. Furthermore,
unlike the FDA, it has limited visualization; it does
n't provide a visual representation
of interrelated failures, and ultimately, it
categorizes FMECA risks but doesn't calculate
overall failure probabilities. A final alternative methodology
is Bowtie analysis,
which combines elements of FTA and FMECA.
It provides a visual framework that
links risk causes to consequences through
proactive and reactive controls. A
Bowtie diagram centers on the top event,
with threats and preventative measures on the
left and consequences and
mitigations on the right. In
our elevator example, a Bowtie diagram can illustrate
threats such as power outages or
software errors alongside
preventative measures like
redundant power sources.  Or automated testing protocols
on the next side, which will explain in detail
mitigation methods such as
emergency braking systems or manual overtaking procedures.
The strengths of tie analysis include a
comprehensive overview and a
dual focus on prevention
and mitigation. It also improves
communication by providing
intuitive diagrams that make it easy to
explain risks to non-experts.

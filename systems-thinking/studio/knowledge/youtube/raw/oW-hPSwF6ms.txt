Normal Accidents
Charles Perrow
Introduction
Dive into the world of high-risk technologies
with the summary of Charles Perrow's insightful
book, "Normal Accidents: Living with High
Risk Technologies - Updated Edition."
This summary explores the complex systems
that govern our modern world, and examines
the intricacies of human-made accidents and
disasters within these systems.
Perrow discusses system accidents and their
various facets, including Design, Equipment,
Procedure, Operator, Supplies, and Environment
(DEPOSE).
Further, you'll discover the role of complexity
and coupling, which play a significant part
in determining the safety and reliability
of various technologies such as nuclear reactors,
chemical plants, dam structures, and aircraft.
Understanding System Accidents
The occurrence of system accidents is often
unexpected and can be complex.
These accidents arise from various errors
in Design, Equipment, Procedure, Operator,
Supplies and Environment, commonly referred
to as DEPOSE.
Failure in any of these areas can cause the
whole system to fail.
This point is illustrated in a situation where
a series of events led to a job interview
being missed.
A forgotten key, a friend with it, a neighbor's
car that wouldn't start, and a bus drivers'
strike prevented a taxi from being available.
The interdependent nature of all these systems
resulted in the entire system, in this case,
the interview, collapsing.
Although such accidents may not always lead
to a disastrous outcome, the implications
in nuclear or chemical plants, for example,
can be catastrophic.
Complex vs. Linear Systems
Complex interactions and tight coupling can
lead to catastrophic failures in systems.
While complex systems have multiple interconnected
components that may be hard to predict, they
can be flexible enough to recover from component
failures.
Linear systems, on the other hand, are easy
to predict, but a single component failure
can trigger failure in other components, leading
to catastrophe.
The Grand Teton Dam collapse in 1976 is an
example of how a tightly coupled linear system
failed due to an insufficient solution to
a known problem.
Understanding the characteristics of different
systems can help prevent disasters and improve
functionality.
Lessons Learned from the Three Mile Island
Disaster
The partial meltdown at the Three Mile Island
(TMI) nuclear power plant in 1979 provides
a valuable lesson on the dangers of complexity
and coupling in high-risk industries.
The accident resulted from four small, seemingly
unrelated failures that occurred within 13
seconds of each other, causing the core temperature
and pressure to rise.
Safety devices that were meant to prevent
disasters sometimes fail or even contribute
to the problem.
The first failure occurred when water leakage
caused a false signal to shut down the turbine
used to generate power.
The emergency water pumps were then inoperable
due to valves that were closed, causing the
core to overheat.
An automatic safety device designed to relieve
pressure got stuck and signaled that it had
closed when it remained open.
This failure led to the unstable core overheating
the coolants, which created hydrogen gas.
Despite the operators following safety protocols
and reducing the cold water feed, the core
remained uncovered, and a hydrogen bubble
had formed.
The lesson learned from TMI is that complex
and tightly coupled systems, like those in
high-risk industries, are unpredictable.
Trivial failures can interact and cause a
system accident.
In such industries, safety devices are installed
to prevent disasters, but they can sometimes
become ineffective or even damage recovery.
Thus, the system must always be monitored
to ensure its safety.
The Inherent Risks of Flying
Flying has become increasingly safe over the
years due to improved designs, safety measures,
and a reduction in complexity and coupling.
However, crashes will always occur due to
the inherent complexity of flying.
Even with the great improvements in air traffic
safety, a single component failure caused
by a maintenance error and design error in
the slats led to hundreds of deaths in the
DC-10 airplane crash.
Flying, with its multitude of components and
unpredictable interactions, will never be
risk-free.
The Hidden Risks of Petrochemical Plants
The dangers of petrochemical plants are often
overlooked, but they pose a significant risk.
Similar to the nuclear industry, the petrochemical
business also employs processes that can be
hazardous.
Although the industry has been in operation
for over a century, accidents have occurred.
While some are unpreventable, others, such
as the Bhopal gas tragedy, are avoidable and
can result in thousands of deaths due to negligence.
Safety precautions are put in place, but it
only takes a mixture of circumstances for
a catastrophic system accident to occur.
By understanding the potential dangers and
risks associated with petrochemical plants,
we can be better informed and take steps to
prevent such tragedies from happening.
The Dangers of Production Pressure
In a bid to meet up with deadlines and expectations,
production pressures oftentimes lead to the
creation of small errors that can cascade
into something uncontrollable.
This is similar to what happens with system
accidents.
While technology designed to aid safety and
navigation can be helpful, they can actually
increase the chances of accidents if not used
correctly.
A case in point is the BP oil tanker incident
that was caused by the captain's poor judgment
due to the pressure to deliver his cargo fast.
Marine accidents caused by operator's haste
and poor judgment are on the rise despite
the adoption of collision avoidance systems
and better communication between vessels.
Companies and operators prioritize quicker
delivery and lower expenses, oftentimes without
considering the potential risks.
This highlights the need for companies to
put safety first and for operators to resist
the urge to take unnecessary risks in a bid
to meet up with production demands.
Escaping Death in Space
Despite design failures, the crew of Apollo
13 successfully improvised and simplified
the system in order to escape death while
en route to the moon.
The Apollo 13 space mission was heading towards
a moon landing when the crew faced a near-death
experience due to design flaws despite the
contributions of experts and billions of dollars
in funding.
One of the oxygen tanks on board had never
been tested in space, which resulted in insulation
burning up and the wires generating heat that
caused an electrical discharge, and emptied
one of the tanks.
The second tank started leaking, yet nothing
onboard notified the crew or mission control
of the explosion of the tank.
The warning lights themselves were misleading.
The astronauts were only trained for failures
of one or two components, whereas they were
challenged with multiple failures.
The crew saved themselves by simplifying and
loosening the system.
They squeezed themselves into the tiny landing
module, which had been designed for two astronauts,
and rationed their supplies.
They conserved fuel by using the gravitational
force of the moon’s orbit to sling-shot
the craft back to Earth, simplified the operating
procedures for re-entry, and discarded automated
devices.
They also abandoned their goal of a moon landing.
This simplification gave them extra time and
meant the system’s coupling became looser
and less prone to total collapse.
Ultimately, their improvised “life raft”
landed safely in the ocean, and the crew was
rescued.
The story of Apollo 13 teaches us the importance
of simplicity and improvisation, especially
in life-threatening situations.
Despite the design and training failures,
the crew of Apollo 13 managed to salvage the
situation by simplifying their system and
the situation.
Intuition and Accidents on Mississippi River
The collision of two ships on the Mississippi
River has left many individuals wondering
about the role intuition and hunches play
in decision-making.
While people often rely on intuition when
making decisions, such judgments can sometimes
be incorrect, leading to accidents like the
non-collision course collisions that occurred
with the Pisces and the Trade Master ships.
These accidents can occur when individuals
construct an understanding of the world that
is divorced from reality.
In the case of the two ships, assumptions
and intuitions about the other ship's behavior
caused them to collide, resulting in millions
of dollars of damage.
The book delves into assessing the risks associated
with certain systems and what to do with such
information to avoid accidents.
Rationality and Risk Perception
The book challenges the idea of rational thinking
as the only way to understand risk and safety.
The author argues that experts tend to overlook
the social and cultural aspects of risk, while
laypeople overestimate the risks associated
with new technologies.
Do you believe that rational thinking is the
key to understanding risk and safety?
Think again.
In "Risk and Culture: An Essay on the Selection
of Technological and Environmental Dangers,"
author Mary Douglas challenges the widely
accepted idea that risk assessment should
focus solely on quantifiable data and mathematical
models.
Instead, the author argues that experts often
overlook the social and cultural aspects of
risk.
According to Douglas, experts tend to use
absolute rationality to assess risk, which
means that they don't differentiate between
deaths that occur under different circumstances.
Whether it's 100,000 traffic-accident deaths
or a single catastrophe, everything is just
a statistic.
In contrast, laypeople tend to overestimate
the risks associated with new technologies,
focusing on rare disasters rather than historical
records.
Douglas goes on to explain how this difference
in risk perception has broader social implications.
For example, experts rate nuclear power as
less risky than hazards like cars, guns, and
alcohol, while the public sees it as riskier.
Laypeople's risk perceptions are influenced
by their memories of catastrophes and fear
of the future, which makes it challenging
for them to understand technical reports.
In conclusion, the book challenges the idea
that rational thinking is the only way to
understand risk and safety.
Instead, the author suggests that we need
to take into account social and cultural factors
when assessing risks.
Experts need to recognize the limitations
of their models, while laypeople need to learn
more about technical reports.
Ultimately, the book motivates us to seek
a better understanding of risk and safety,
avoiding the pitfalls of both absolute rationality
and dread risk.
Balancing Risks and Benefits
The book discusses how to balance the risks
and benefits of complex systems such as dams,
universities, nuclear plants, and chemical
production.
Linear systems with tight coupling should
be centralized to provide an immediate response
prescribed in advance by a central authority.
Complex systems with loose coupling should
be decentralized to allow operators to diagnose
and analyze unexpected situations.
Complex interactive and tightly coupled systems
require both centralization and decentralization.
The catastrophic potential of a technology
should be assessed to determine whether it
should be improved, restricted, or abandoned
altogether.
Technologies with low to medium catastrophic
potential should be used with steps to improve
their safety.
On the other hand, technologies with substantial
catastrophic potential but lack of viable
alternatives should be restricted.
Lastly, nuclear power and nuclear weapons
should be abandoned.
Final Recap
Having delved into the intricate domains of
high-risk technologies, the summary of Charles
Perrow's "Normal Accidents: Living with High
Risk Technologies - Updated Edition" underscores
the need for a balanced approach towards centralization
and decentralization, while taking safety
precautions into consideration.
Surprisingly, humans' unwarranted sense of
intuition has often led to catastrophic accidents.
The right approach lies in the assessment
of a technology's catastrophic potential,
followed by identification of potential improvements,
restrictions, or complete abandonment.
It is essential to learn valuable lessons
from the past and have a calculated outlook
towards the future of high-risk technologies,
for the sake of posterity and the welfare
of humanity.

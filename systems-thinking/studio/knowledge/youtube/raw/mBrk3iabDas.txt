Organizational design is the blueprint you
create to ensure that your organization has
the suitable structures and resources to deliver
on the business strategy.
This blueprint is the outcome of various decisions
the organization needs to make as part of
a structured design process.
There are different organizational design
models, and each one has its advantages and
disadvantages.
Examples include Galbraith’s Star model,
the Weisbord six-box model, or the Burke-Litwin
model.
At AIHR, we use the Organizational Design,
or OD, Blueprint.
I will explain the OD blueprint using Judy
as an example.
Judy is the chef of an up-and-coming Italian
restaurant, Rosmarino in Soho, London.
She employs 47 employees.
Most of the team works in the kitchen next
to a small but lean customer-facing team.
Judy tries to interact as often as she can
to get a feel for the requirements of her
customers.
Judy’s customers have provided her with
feedback that there is a need for home delivery
services.
This will allow Rosmarino to reach more customers
beyond their current in-house dining options.
However, this will create a huge change in
how the restaurant operates currently, and
before Judy can decide whether this is the
right decision for Rosmarino, she needs to
engage in organizational design.
As you can see in our OD blueprint, Judy’s
starting point is the strategy and purpose
of Rosmarino: To bring authentic Italian cuisine
to London.
This will not change - in fact, it will be
enhanced with home delivery options.
She then reviews the data on her customers,
how often they buy from her, and their preferences
in terms of menu.
She also looks into her local competition
by partnering with a restaurant expert to
better understand the market and how she can
position Rosmarino.
After concluding that home delivery is a viable
option for Rosmarino, Judy starts to list
the organizational design objectives.
The purpose of Rosmarino - bringing authentic
Italian cuisine to London - is the first design
objective.
Other objectives include:
The expansion should not impact the current
experience of the dine-in customers.
Home service food should be of the same quality
as the in-house dining experience.
And the Rosmarino brand should be reflected
through both in-house dining and the at-home
experience.
Judy will use these design objectives as question
statements throughout her organizational design
process.
For example, in considering a change in menu,
she asks herself whether the food would be
of the same quality if it would be home-delivered.
If the answer is no, then that menu item is
not viable for the home dining menu.
Next, Judy develops a capability map that
helps her understand what capabilities she
needs to have in Rosmarino to execute the
home-dining strategy.
She identifies that she would require access
to an online order platform and expand her
marketing efforts to target a new customer
segment.
She would have to secure the capability to
deliver in a dependable, fast, and efficient
manner, decide on the type of packaging that
will still give home customers a feel of the
Rosmarino brand experience, and adapt her
menu to be feasible for home delivery.
She then considers that she needs to develop
a new pricing model that caters for the additional
expenses of delivery, whilst still remaining
profitable.
Judy uses the capability map to highlight
which of these capabilities she already has
versus which ones are new and would need to
be acquired.
Judy realizes that she’ll need to use various
strategies to gain access to these new capabilities.
First, she decides to partner with Delivery
Rabbit, a local food delivery service, and
lists Rosmarino on their platform.
They also provide some marketing services
on their platform, and she can adapt her current
marketing collateral to include home delivery
as a service.
Given the importance of the brand, she knows
she’ll need to acquire expertise in packaging—something
Judy knows nothing about.
Revising the menu is something that she can
do herself; however, she believes it’ll
be more productive to put one of her sous
chef’s focus specifically on the home delivery
menu.
Lastly, she’ll need to enlist the help of
a financial manager to help her understand
how to price the home menu to ensure profitability.
Next up, Judy starts to explore different
operating model options that she can use to
organize the capabilities.
She could run everything from one central
kitchen servicing both in-house and home services,
or she could decide to split up the kitchen
into two distinct areas, each with its own
focus.
For the front of house employees, she could
extend their scope to include managing the
deliveries on the online platform, or she
could hire a new home-delivery staff.
Judy works through various operating models,
looks at the benefits and limitations of each
of these and ultimately decides on the blueprint
that she believes will help her achieve her
objectives.
She uses a front-back operating model blueprint
which enables her to run everything from a
centralized kitchen, but split home delivery
and in-house dining services in separate service
lines, with their own managers and KPIs.
She will also have a function focused on operations,
including finance, legal, marketing, and IT
systems.
This model allows her to leverage some shared
capabilities in the back-office to drive cost
efficiency, while the front-office split helps
her to execute on two very different services
and markets that will each require something
different to be successful.
Each element in the blueprint is then structured
into a functional department.
For each department, she defines the processes,
the roles they would require, and how they
would work together to deliver value.
She also implements weekly rhythms to ensure
that these departments engage and that critical
decisions can be made with all the relevant
people around the table.
Once she has her structure in place, she evaluates
her current workforce to identify what gaps
she has, where she needs to reskill or where
she needs to acquire new skills.
For example, she will have to hire people
to do the home delivery and train them on
how to communicate with customers.
She may have to bring in expertise on packaging,
train station chefs on how to package and
arrange food when it comes to home delivery,
and she may also need to expand the marketing
team as their scope has also widened.
All these activities help her develop the
capabilities she needs to successfully deliver
food by matching new and existing talent with
roles.
Lastly, she defines performance metrics for
Rosmarino as a whole and then works with the
manager of each department to define more
specific metrics to track at the departmental
level.
Judy has now completed our organizational
design blueprint.
It has helped her understand how she needs
to set up Rosmarino to succeed in the home
delivery service market.
She will continuously monitor how the restaurant
performs and, when required, make the necessary
adjustments to ensure that Rosmarino keeps
on delivering on its core business purpose,
which is to provide an authentic Italian dining
experience for all her customers, regardless
of whether they eat in-house or dine at home.
So there you have it.
Organizational design is the process of developing
the best-fit blueprint to ensure that your
organization has the suitable structures and
resources to deliver on the business strategy.
In a constantly changing world, organizational
design has become a critical tool for the
HR professional’s toolbox.
Being able to guide the business along the
organizational design landscape and help them
make informed decisions is an essential part
of our role as HR to support our organization
to deliver value to their customers.
That's it for the organizational design process.

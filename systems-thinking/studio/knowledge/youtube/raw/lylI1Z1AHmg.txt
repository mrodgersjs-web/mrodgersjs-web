Why do we measure things?
That might seem like a silly question
until you consider just how much we do it.
From population data and GDP,
down to steps and screen time,
we measure almost every aspect of our lives,
but always for the same reason:
to make things better.
Measuring shows us where we've been,
to guide us where we want to go.
This idea has served us so well
that we take it for granted,
but it's worth pausing to think about –
because losing sight of exactly what, how, and why
we're measuring can end up leading us,
not to the sunlit uplands of a brighter tomorrow,
but straight into a minefield:
the minefield of metrics.
A metric is basically a system
for assessing something
and stating it in numbers.
Sounds pretty simple –
but this simplicity is the source of metrics' power,
because summing up complex situations
like this makes them easier to grasp.
For example, everyone deserves good health,
a sound education, and a safe community –
but these concepts are ambiguous
and hard to define;
so we simplify, focusing on things we can count:
for health, hospital waiting times;
for education, exam grades;
for safety, crime rates.
These provide a broad strokes picture
of what's going on,
and crucially,
what improvement should look like.
It's an effective system, but not without risks,
because whenever a multifaceted reality
is boiled down to mere numbers,
something will get left out –
and when that happens,
metrics' greatest strength becomes
our greatest weakness.
You see, the sad fact is that in a complex,
bewildering world, our brains love simplifications,
much like they prefer sugary snacks
over something less immediately gratifying,
but ultimately more nourishing.
As a result,
it's easy for us to forget that metrics
only show us a sample of reality,
never the whole thing.
And that starts to create problems
when we use them as ways to define success,
because this simplified perspective
can distract us from, and even damage,
the things we really care about –
sometimes with disastrous results.
To see this play out, consider 19th century India,
where, in a much-needed attempt at pest control,
the colonial government offered a reward
for killing poisonous snakes.
Sounds pretty reasonable –
except that enterprising locals
spotted a business opportunity
and began breeding cobras
specifically for this purpose.
When officials got wise to the scheme,
they unsurprisingly, stopped paying the bounty,
causing the breeders to set their livestock free.
The result?
More snakes on the loose, rather than less!
Now, there's some doubt as to whether or not
this actually happened, but either way,
the so-called "cobra effect"
has become a byword
for how oversimplified metrics
can come back to bite us.
The reason this happens was neatly explained
by British economist Charles Goodhart
in what has become known as "Goodhart's Law":
once a measure becomes a target,
it ceases to be a good measure.
And once we know to look for this problem,
we find examples of it all around us.
Crime statistics tell us the scale of social problems;
but just demanding police bring them down
makes the simplest option to...
report fewer crimes.
Exam results gauge the state of education,
but pressuring teachers to improve them
replaces creativity and critical thinking
with simply "teaching to the test".
Waiting times inside hospitals indicate
the efficiency of our healthcare systems,
but a fixation on bringing them down
leads to shortcuts,
like keeping patients waiting outside hospitals.
Again and again,
when we overemphasize a narrowly defined metric,
what suffers is the exact thing
we were trying to improve.
So what's the lesson?
That we should stop measuring things?
Well, no – clearly metrics are useful.
The real question is,
how can we use them
without developing tunnel vision?
In my experience,
a lot of this comes down to one word: 'while.'
We want to reduce costs, while maintaining quality;
to increase productivity
while maintaining reasonable working hours.
"Now hang on", I can hear you thinking
"all you're doing is adding more metrics" –
but the key is choosing metrics
in tension with one another.
It's easy to reduce costs
if you're happy for quality to go out the window,
or to increase productivity
if you don't mind sleeping 3 hours a night
But by using multiple metrics
and understanding
the relationship between them,
it's possible to reduce your blind spots
and maintain a more balanced view.
Ultimately, realising that metrics alone
will always represent an incomplete picture
is a good start towards
using them productively;
and considering the central role they play
in our societies, businesses, and everyday lives,
that understanding is essential.
But it's also important
that those in charge of setting targets
realise that they can – and should – go beyond
the broad strokes of metrics,
to fill in their outline with the detail
and shading of personal experience.
And to do that,
they need to hear from the people
whose performances are being measured –
those with first-hand knowledge
of what's missing from the numbers –
to get a more comprehensive
and detailed sense of what's working,
what's not, and what really counts as "better".

So what is the Prospect Theory?
First published in 1979, by two American-Israeli&nbsp;
psychologists, Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky,&nbsp;&nbsp;
it is a groundbreaking theory that describes&nbsp;
how individuals make decisions under risk.
Consider the following scenario
a man walks up to you on the&nbsp;
street, and offers you two options
Option A: 50% chance of getting 1000$&nbsp;
and 50% chance of getting nothing&nbsp;
Option B: 100% chance of&nbsp;
getting 450$, no questions asked
Now which one would you pick?
Traditional economic-theory states that&nbsp;
individuals should calculate the expected value&nbsp;&nbsp;
based on probability, so that the most profitable&nbsp;
and rational choice is always picked. So if option&nbsp;&nbsp;
A is picked, that will on average, yield 500$,&nbsp;
whereas Option B will always land you 450$.
Now Kahneman and Tversky found that the&nbsp;
vast majority would choose the “certain”&nbsp;&nbsp;
gain in these kind of scenarios&nbsp;
- which is what they dubbed “the&nbsp;&nbsp;
certainty effect”. So even if Option&nbsp;
B landed you less money on average,&nbsp;&nbsp;
more people were still inclined to pick&nbsp;
the safe choice, when dealing with gain.
When facing loss, the exact&nbsp;
opposite phenomenon occurred:&nbsp;
Consider the same situation,&nbsp;
but this time reversed:
Option A: 50% chance of losing&nbsp;
1000$, 50% chance of losing 0$&nbsp;
Option B: 100% chance of losing 450$
Following the same logic from the first scenario,&nbsp;
individuals SHOULD pick the certain loss for most&nbsp;&nbsp;
value, but as it turns out, as much as individuals&nbsp;
love certain gain, they despise certain loss. So&nbsp;&nbsp;
even after calculating the expected value, the&nbsp;
majority would still choose the worse option
In an 1981 publication, Kahneman and Tversky added&nbsp;
another important element: the framing effect.
The framing effect is a cognitive&nbsp;
bias that shows how our decisions&nbsp;&nbsp;
can be swayed by the way information is&nbsp;
presented. It's not just about the content;&nbsp;&nbsp;
it's about how that content&nbsp;
is worded or structured.
Consider this example: you’re at&nbsp;
a bar and you’ve been eyeing that&nbsp;&nbsp;
cute-looking girl standing over there for a while
Suddenly, the god of statistics appear in front&nbsp;
of you and tells you that there is a 90% chance&nbsp;&nbsp;
that she wants to go home with you and have a&nbsp;
pillow-fight… or whatever it is that you do.
You haven’t had a pillowfight in a long time,&nbsp;&nbsp;
so you seem super happy and you&nbsp;
approach her with a hopeful smile
Now lets rewind about 20 seconds and&nbsp;
go back to Johan Karl Friedrich Gauss -&nbsp;
and in this case the he tells you that there&nbsp;
is a 10% chance that she will turn you down&nbsp;&nbsp;
and you will be filled with humiliation&nbsp;
and embarrasment. After hearing this you&nbsp;&nbsp;
feel disheartened and continue listening to your&nbsp;
bankrupt uncle Mike talking about PUTS and WSB.
Both scenarios are the exact same percentages,&nbsp;
but you might feel more inclined to approach your&nbsp;&nbsp;
soon-to-be soulmate given the first scenario,&nbsp;
because it was positively framed. That is,&nbsp;&nbsp;
essentially, the framing effect and it can&nbsp;
change whether you spend the rest of your&nbsp;&nbsp;
life alone and miserable or spend the next&nbsp;
50 years growing old with the one and only.
For the prospect theory, Kahneman and&nbsp;
Tversky proposed an s-shaped value function.
The perception of gain and loss is dependent on&nbsp;&nbsp;
where you currently find yourself on&nbsp;
the curve, and where you want to be.
So if you’re standing in origo,&nbsp;&nbsp;
you would perceive the green area&nbsp;
as a win, and the red area as a loss
If you’re already in a winning-scenario,&nbsp;
you become risk-adverse, because the&nbsp;&nbsp;
opportunity for loss is much bigger&nbsp;
than the opportunity for gain
And if you’ve lost a lot, you become risk-seeking,&nbsp;
because it cant really get that much worse.

With your newfound confidence and beautifully&nbsp;&nbsp;

Tversky and Kahneman dubbed this phenomenon&nbsp;
“diminishing sensitivity”. Initially&nbsp;&nbsp;
gains or losses make a big impact, but the&nbsp;
addition of more and more or less and less,&nbsp;&nbsp;
will eventually diminish your perception of how&nbsp;
bad or good the situation is. Kind of like how&nbsp;&nbsp;
uncle Mike stopped caring after the value of&nbsp;
NIO dropped by 80% and he lost his entire 401k.
Finally the pain of a loss also hurts&nbsp;
significantly more than the pleasure&nbsp;&nbsp;
you would get from an equivalent gain.&nbsp;
So if uncle Mike stole 100$ from you&nbsp;&nbsp;
it would feel much worse than the happiness you&nbsp;&nbsp;
would get if he suddenly gave you 100$ for&nbsp;

So here's the core idea behind dynamic
batching. Instead of processing each
inference request one at a time, theuler
waits for a short window like 20
milliseconds to collect multiple
requests, then sends them to the GPU as
one big batch.
Here's why batching is so powerful. A
GPU running ResNet 50 processes one
image in 5 milliseconds, but it can
process a batch of 32 images in just 20
milliseconds, giving you 8 times better
throughput, which translates to 50 to
70% cost savings.
But here's the trade-off. That 20
millisecond batch window means every
request sits in the queue waiting before
any computation even starts. And if your
P95 latency SLO is 100 milliseconds,
you've already burned 20% of your budget
just waiting.
Under bursty or low QPS traffic, you'll
see something weird. The GPU sits mostly
idle because requests don't arrive fast
enough to form full batches. Yet, your
P95 and P99 latencies are terrible
because every request burns time waiting
in the queue.
In production, you tune three knobs.
TensorFlow serving typically uses 4 to
16 millisecond windows for strict
latency services, while Triton uses 50
millisecond windows with batch sizes of
64 to 128 for throughput optimized
workloads. And the key is separating Q
time from compute time in your metrics
to diagnose where the bottleneck
actually is.

So you're running a computer vision
model serving 10,000 queries per second
and it's eating up four V100 GPUs at
$20,000 per month. That's a lot of
hardware just to handle inference at

Here's the trick. You take that trained
FP32 model and convert it to FP16 using
Tensor RT for Nvidia GPUs. The
conversion halves your memory footprint
because each number now uses 16 bits
instead of 32. And modern Tensor cores
can crunch through FP16 operations 2x to
4x faster than FP32.
Same 10,000 queries per second, but now
you're running on just two GPUs instead
of four. and your monthly cost drops
from 20,000 to $10,000. That's a 50%
cost reduction just from changing number
precision.
Now, FP16 and BF16 both use 16 bits, but
here's the key difference. BF16 keeps
the same 8 exponent bits as FP32,
so it has the exact same dynamic range
and won't underflow on large values.
FP16 has fewer exponent bits, so it can
hit underflow with really large or small
numbers. But for most vision and
language models, you're looking at under
0.5% accuracy drop with either format.
If you want even more speed, INT8
quantization uses 8-bit integers instead
of floating point and can give you 4x
throughput over FP32. But here's the
catch. You can see 1 to 5% accuracy
degradation depending on how well you
calibrate the quantization ranges. So
you need careful testing with
representative data sets before you ship
it.
So here's the most common production
failure. Your P50 latency looks amazing
at 15 milliseconds and GPU utilization
is only 40%. But your P95 latency is
blowing up to 200 milliseconds and
violating SLOs's.
Here's what's happening. Dynamic
batching waits for requests to arrive
and form a batch. But during traffic
valleys, requests sit in the queue
burning latency budget before any
computation even starts. So the GPU is
idle while requests are waiting.
The fix is counterintuitive.
reduce batch window timeouts or disable
batching entirely during low traffic
periods, accepting lower GPU utilization
to actually meet your tail latency
commitments.
Memory exhaustion crashes services
silently. A 2 GB model with batch size
32 and 4 GB of activation memory running
with concurrency 2 needs 2 + 2 * 4
equals 10 GB minimum. And when you
exceed capacity, you get out of memory
errors mid request, returning cryptic
failures to clients.
Training serving skew creates silent
accuracy degradation. Models trained on
batch computed features but served with
realtime features can see 10 to 20%
accuracy drops because the computation
differs slightly in aggregation windows,
missing features or ordering changes.
And the fix is using feature store
abstractions that guarantee identical
computation offline and online.

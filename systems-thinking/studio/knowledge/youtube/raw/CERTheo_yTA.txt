Before I give you this amazing tool, we need
to talk about Conway’s Law.
Conway's law is named for Melvin E Conway.
Mr. Conway wrote a very influential paper
in 1968, and in that paper, he wrote “Any&nbsp;&nbsp;
organization that designs a system (defined more&nbsp;
broadly here than just information systems) will&nbsp;&nbsp;
inevitably produce a design whose structure&nbsp;
is a copy of the organization's communication&nbsp;
structure”.
This is Conway’s Law.
OK, that might be a little hard to understand.
Let’s try an example.
Mr. Conway talked about a system that the
US Army and the US Navy built together.
The final design looked like this: There was
an army module, and a navy module, and a command
module that controlled how the army and navy
pieces talked to each other.
The system looked exactly like the design
of the organisations that built the system.
Applied to software, Conway’s Law says that
your software architecture will look like&nbsp;&nbsp;
your organizational structure&nbsp;
and communication patterns.
So, if you set up a “Team A” to build some
features and a “Team B” to build some other&nbsp;&nbsp;
features, you’re going to end with a&nbsp;
“Team A” module and a “Team B” module.
I've personally seen this&nbsp;
play out hundreds of times.
I've seen Conway’s Law in airline software.
I've seen it in banks.
I've seen it in infrastructure&nbsp;
management platforms.
No matter what, the way you&nbsp;
structure your organization&nbsp;&nbsp;
ends up being your software architecture.
Actually, this is really&nbsp;
cool because it gives you an&nbsp;
incredibly powerful tool:&nbsp;
The Reverse Conway Manoeuvre.
Conway’s Law says that your organisational
design becomes your system.
So, if you want to have a new system architecture,&nbsp;
then you can implement it simply&nbsp;
by changing your organisation.
Here’s the steps:
- Figure out your&nbsp;&nbsp;
desired future state architecture
- Determine the future state organisational&nbsp;
structure that will match that architecture
Then,
- Start shifting your current organisational&nbsp;
structure to match the future state
This is the Reverse Conway Manoeuvre.
Here’s an example.
Say you have a backend team&nbsp;
that is working on a big&nbsp;&nbsp;
monolithic system that’s really hard to manage.
This monolith does a LOT of different stuff.
Too much.
To make it more manageable, we want to break
that big monolith into smaller microservices.
Let’s use the Reverse Conway Manoeuvre to
create a separate “Payments” system.
You start by building a new team.
Take some of those people that&nbsp;
are experienced with the monolith.
Carve them out into a new team&nbsp;
called “The Payments Team”.
This new team has the mission of building&nbsp;
all the payments related software.
What will organically happen is you'll end up with&nbsp;&nbsp;
a separate payment system that talks&nbsp;
to the original monolith in the same&nbsp;&nbsp;
way that the payments team talks&nbsp;
to the rest of the backend team.
You can repeat this process&nbsp;
over and over again until&nbsp;&nbsp;
the whole monolith is broken&nbsp;
into nice neat little chunks.
Pretty cool, yeah?
The “Reverse Conway Manoeuvre” works for
all kinds of systems, not just software systems.
There's also a lot of subtlety,&nbsp;
there's a lot of nuance.
However, the core concept is pretty simple.
The big take away here is that when you're&nbsp;
designing any kind of system always&nbsp;
think of your organizational design.
Conway’s Law decrees that your organisational
design will always drive your system architecture.
Once you understand how Conway’s Law works

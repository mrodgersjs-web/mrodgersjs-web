Cognitive Dissonance
A fox sneaked up to a vine. He stared intently&nbsp;
at the juicy, purple, overripe grapes.&nbsp;&nbsp;
He tried to get at the grapes, but they were too&nbsp;
high. Frustrated, he tried again. He launched&nbsp;&nbsp;
himself upward, but came no closer to the&nbsp;
fruit. He leapt for a third time, this time&nbsp;&nbsp;
landing hard with a thud. Still no grapes. The&nbsp;
fox turned up his nose: “I don’t really care.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Only grapes that aren’t even ripe. Why would I&nbsp;
want sour grapes? He walked back into the forest.
This is one of Aesop’s fables and also&nbsp;
where we get the term “Sour grapes” from.
The fox had 3 choices
Get to the grapes
Admit that he wasn’t smart&nbsp;&nbsp;
and/or skilled enough to obtain the grapes
Reinterpret the situation retrospectively.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Or put simply, create a new belief that&nbsp;
is in conflict with the first belief.
When we choose option three, and we&nbsp;
have two held beliefs that are in&nbsp;&nbsp;
conflict with one another, that is&nbsp;
an example of cognitive dissonance.
If you interview for a job, but someone else gets&nbsp;
it. Instead of reasoning that the other person&nbsp;&nbsp;
was better, you tell yourself that the job was no&nbsp;
good anyway, or that the interviewer was unfair.
When people can’t get what they want. They often&nbsp;
tell themselves it isn’t what they wanted anyway.
You think all rich people are greedy&nbsp;
and evil… but you also want to be rich,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the dissonance leads to discomfort, mental&nbsp;
stress and anxiety. If the dissonance grows&nbsp;&nbsp;
more intense it can lead to depression. If&nbsp;
you notice dissonance, you need to choose one.
Because When it comes to cognitive&nbsp;
dissonance, as Rolf Dobelli says “You&nbsp;&nbsp;
can play the clever fox all you want—but&nbsp;
you’ll never get the grapes that way.”
The Spotlight Effect
You arrive 5 minutes late to the office and you&nbsp;
feel like everyone is judging you. It’s your&nbsp;&nbsp;
first day at a gym and you feel like everyone is&nbsp;
watching you. You spill a small amount of sauce on&nbsp;&nbsp;
your shirt and you feel so embarrassed because&nbsp;
you think the whole party is going to notice!
The spotlight effect is the phenomenon in which&nbsp;&nbsp;
people tend to believe people are&nbsp;
observing them more than they are.
People are seldom interested in you&nbsp;
and your actions as much as you think,&nbsp;&nbsp;
so do yourself a favor, stop overestimating&nbsp;
how much people are observing you and reduce&nbsp;&nbsp;
the anxiety that is probably just&nbsp;
the result of the spotlight effect.
The Anchoring effect
Whenever we have to guess something—Let’s say&nbsp;
the population of Russia - we use anchors.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Starting with something we know for sure to&nbsp;
be true, OK so it must be greater than 1,&nbsp;&nbsp;
Must be less than 7 Billion and is&nbsp;
less than the population of China…..&nbsp;&nbsp;
We take these anchors and then we&nbsp;
explore the unfamiliar territory.
Unfortunately, we use anchors&nbsp;
when we don’t need to.
Take a moment to pause and&nbsp;
look at these questions.
Is the height of the tallest redwood&nbsp;
tree more or less than 1,200 feet?&nbsp;
What is your best guess about the&nbsp;
height of the tallest redwood?
If we asked group A, these two questions,&nbsp;
and group B these 2 questions….. We would&nbsp;&nbsp;
consistently get very different answers….&nbsp;
Because of the anchoring effect.
But the anchoring effect doesn’t&nbsp;
only apply to numbers that appear&nbsp;&nbsp;
informative such as in the tree&nbsp;
example, According to Kahneman
“ …Anchors that are obviously random can be just&nbsp;
as effective as potentially informative anchors”
An experiment was conducted on German judges.&nbsp;
With an average of 15 years experience.
Each judge was read a description&nbsp;
of a woman who had been shoplifting.
Then asked to roll a pair of dice, which&nbsp;
were loaded to only add up to 3 or 9.
As soon as the dice stopped they were asked to&nbsp;
answer how long they would sentence this woman.
The judges who rolled a 9, on&nbsp;
average gave her an 8 month sentence.
The judges who rolled a 3, on&nbsp;
average gave her a 5 month sentence.
The Researchers found that the anchoring&nbsp;
effect was influencing their judgements.&nbsp;&nbsp;
numerous other studies using arbitrary&nbsp;
numbers like the last digits of phone&nbsp;&nbsp;
numbers or social security numbers&nbsp;
also confirmed our anchoring bias.
In sales and negotiations, Anchors are being&nbsp;
used all the time, and there will be people who&nbsp;&nbsp;
are willing and able to set up this mind trap&nbsp;
and exploit the anchoring effect against you.
The Car salesman setting a&nbsp;
high price from the start,&nbsp;&nbsp;
so the price he actually wants to&nbsp;
get from you seems like a good deal.
The $150 dollar dress at the front of the store,&nbsp;&nbsp;
sets the anchor for the $50 dress&nbsp;
on sale at the back of the store.
Online Stores, Salary negotiations and Real estate&nbsp;&nbsp;
deals are all playgrounds&nbsp;
for the anchoring effect.
And it’s one of our most powerful&nbsp;
biases. You can’t turn it off,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but you can remind yourself of your&nbsp;
vulnerability to it and try to proactively&nbsp;&nbsp;
set your own mental anchors before going&nbsp;
into any sales or negotiation environment.
“They have sent us the asking price for the&nbsp;
home, let’s not let this number influence&nbsp;&nbsp;
our thinking. Set it aside. Let’s perform our&nbsp;
own due diligence and arrive at our own number”
“Our objective in this negotiation is to move&nbsp;
first and get them anchored to this number”
The Halo Effect
What do you think about Alan and Ben?
Alan is&nbsp;&nbsp;
intelligent--industrious--impulsive--critical--stubborn--envious
Ben is envious -- stubborn -- critical&nbsp;
-- impulsive -- industrious --intelligent
If you are like most people you see Alan&nbsp;
in a better light than you do Ben…. even&nbsp;&nbsp;
though the traits mentioned are exactly the same.
When it comes to the halo effect,&nbsp;
sequence matters. More weight is&nbsp;&nbsp;
given to the first piece of information&nbsp;
we receive.The first piece of information&nbsp;&nbsp;
helps us quickly create a story of&nbsp;
the person or situation in our minds.
Sure Alan, is stubborn and envious,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but that is because he is intelligent&nbsp;
and wants to win in business.
Yes, Ben is intelligent… but he uses&nbsp;
that intelligence in envious ways…
The halo effect occurs when&nbsp;
a single, initial aspect of a&nbsp;&nbsp;
person or thing determines and affects or&nbsp;
“outshines” how we see the full picture.
When you first start dating someone, both parties&nbsp;
in the relationship are on their best behavior.&nbsp;&nbsp;
You start to develop a halo of positive thoughts&nbsp;
around this person. Small traits you dislike&nbsp;&nbsp;
might begin to pop up but often go unnoticed&nbsp;
because “the halo”, the positive emotions&nbsp;&nbsp;
and the initial information you gathered on this&nbsp;
person is blinding out any of the negatives. The&nbsp;&nbsp;
honeymoon phase of a relationship is often when&nbsp;
the Halo effect influences your judgment the most.
If we learn that someone graduated&nbsp;
from a prestigious university,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the halo effect will distort all other traits we&nbsp;
attribute to that person without any evidence.
Bernie Maddof was the darling of Wall Street,&nbsp;&nbsp;
a legendary investor. The amazing returns and&nbsp;
reputation of his company were the Halo that&nbsp;&nbsp;
made people also conclude his company must be&nbsp;
trustworthy. The Halo outshone the numbers that&nbsp;&nbsp;
made no sense and the underlying fact he was&nbsp;
running the biggest Ponzi-Scheme in history.
Numerous studies have shown that attractive&nbsp;
people are automatically perceived as nicer,&nbsp;&nbsp;
more honest, and more intelligent.
The Halo Effect can also be found in schools.&nbsp;
If a student answers 2 essay questions and the&nbsp;&nbsp;
teacher gives the first essay a high grade,&nbsp;
he/she is prone to subconsciously give that&nbsp;&nbsp;
more weight and give the second essay a higher&nbsp;
grade also, and vice versa for low grades.
In the work environment, the standard practice&nbsp;
of most meetings is to have open discussions&nbsp;&nbsp;
on a topic, Daniel Kaheneman in the book&nbsp;
Thinking Fast and Slow, argues that it is&nbsp;&nbsp;
better to gather independent judgments on&nbsp;
the topic from everyone in the group before&nbsp;&nbsp;
the issue is discussed because far too often&nbsp;
the opinions of the first people to talk are&nbsp;&nbsp;
given too much weight and influence the groups&nbsp;
input… especially if the boss speaks first.
Modern Research suggests that the old saying&nbsp;
“First impressions last” turns out to be true.
After meeting someone for the first time,&nbsp;&nbsp;
our judgment of that person can influence&nbsp;
us for a long time into the future.
We jump to conclusions and our perception of&nbsp;
true characteristics is distorted by the halo&nbsp;&nbsp;
effect. To combat this, try to move beyond the&nbsp;
first appearance of someone or something and&nbsp;&nbsp;
decorrelate error. Remember, your brain is trying&nbsp;
to help you by making the most complete story it&nbsp;&nbsp;
can on the limited information it is provided,&nbsp;
the problem is that these “mental shortcut&nbsp;&nbsp;
stories” we tell ourselves about a person&nbsp;
or thing are often inaccurate from reality.
“He knows nothing about her personality,&nbsp;&nbsp;
all he is going by is how good looking she&nbsp;
is, he is succumbing to the Halo Effect”
“This new applicant graduated&nbsp;
from Harvard, she doesn’t have&nbsp;&nbsp;
any experience in a similar position, but&nbsp;
I think we should interview her anyway”
“Let’s gather ideas independently&nbsp;
on this topic before the meeting,&nbsp;&nbsp;
I don’t want my ideas to influence the groups”
Gambler’s fallacy
Three times, a coin is flipped and lands&nbsp;
on heads each time. Let's say that someone&nbsp;&nbsp;
forces you to wager thousands of&nbsp;
dollars of your own money on the&nbsp;&nbsp;
next toss. Would you bet on heads or&nbsp;
tails? If you think like most people,&nbsp;&nbsp;
you will almost always choose tails,&nbsp;
although heads is equally likely. But why?
We believe in some kind of&nbsp;
balancing force in the Universe.
If we asked people to choose&nbsp;
which sequence is more probable,&nbsp;&nbsp;
most would pick the top sequence. But&nbsp;
both sequences are equally probable.
We generally underestimate the likelihood&nbsp;
of streaks occurring by chance.
We are led to believe that something needs&nbsp;
to change due to the gambler's fallacy.
However there is no such balancing force,&nbsp;
the coin can not remember that heads was&nbsp;&nbsp;
flipped 3 times in a row, the ball cannot&nbsp;
remember that it just landed on black.
Casinos love the gambler’s fallacy because it&nbsp;
creates the illusion in the gambler’s mind that&nbsp;&nbsp;
they can predict where “the balance” of&nbsp;
the dice or roulette wheel will go next.
This fallacy can apply anywhere&nbsp;
there is a sequence of decisions.
That awkward feeling you get when&nbsp;
you’ve answered 5 C’s in a row&nbsp;&nbsp;
of a multiple choice exam is this fallacy at work.
A university of Chicago review found&nbsp;
Asylum judges were 19% less likely&nbsp;&nbsp;
to approve an asylum seeker if they&nbsp;
had just approved the previous two.
The same person applying for a loan was&nbsp;
more likely to get approved for a loan&nbsp;&nbsp;
if the previous two applicants were&nbsp;
rejected and was more likely to be&nbsp;&nbsp;
rejected if the previous two&nbsp;
applications were approved.
Similar findings were also&nbsp;
found with Baseball umpires.
Take a closer look at the independent&nbsp;
and interdependent events around you.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Independent events are not influenced&nbsp;
by balancing forces of nature.
The Contrast Effect
If you see some leather seats for 3000&nbsp;
dollars, they may seem a little expensive.
If you are buying an $80,000&nbsp;
dollar car on the other hand,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the $3000 leather seat upgrade&nbsp;
seems almost like nothing.
Research shows that people will walk an&nbsp;
extra 10 minutes if it means saving $10&nbsp;&nbsp;
on food. However, nearly all wouldn't walk&nbsp;
ten minutes to save $10 on a $1,000 suit.
It's easy to think something is attractive,&nbsp;
large, or expensive when it sits next to something&nbsp;&nbsp;
ugly, small, or cheap. Absolute&nbsp;
judgments can be difficult to make.
Try to catch yourself the next&nbsp;
time you go shopping to see if&nbsp;&nbsp;
your purchasing decisions are being&nbsp;
influenced by the Contrast effect.
Confirmation Bias
You have an existing belief about something.&nbsp;
You go in search of evidence that supports that&nbsp;&nbsp;
belief, which further reinforces the belief.&nbsp;
And you continue this cycle, and continue to&nbsp;&nbsp;
reinforce the belief. If you come across evidence&nbsp;
that doesn’t support your belief, you filter the&nbsp;&nbsp;
disconfirming evidence and your brain actively&nbsp;
“forgets” it after a short period of time.
This is confirmation bias.
The tendency to interpret new information so&nbsp;
that it becomes compatible with our existing&nbsp;&nbsp;
theories and beliefs. Our brains&nbsp;
are hardwired to maintain beliefs,&nbsp;&nbsp;
not readily accept new ones. Because accepting&nbsp;
new beliefs is psychologically draining.
As opposed to the Scientific Method, where you&nbsp;
form a hypothesis or ask a question, Gather&nbsp;&nbsp;
evidence and then test the hypothesis. This is&nbsp;
hard work!, and may entail some unpleasant truths.
For those reasons, most people prefer the&nbsp;
easier route which makes them feel good.
Jon begins with a simple theory or belief.
He turns to Google.
He subconsciously goes in search of the first&nbsp;&nbsp;
information that confirms his&nbsp;
belief and ignores the rest.
Philosophers of science would tell&nbsp;
us to try and refute the hypothesis.
But Rarely do people actively try&nbsp;
to seek out disconfirming evidence .
To make matters worse, the biggest platforms&nbsp;
now tailor content to personal interests and&nbsp;&nbsp;
browsing history, supercharging confirmation&nbsp;
bias on a mass scale. We find ourselves in&nbsp;&nbsp;
communities of like-minded people,&nbsp;
also referred to as echo chambers,&nbsp;&nbsp;
thereby reinforcing our convictions-and&nbsp;
the confirmation bias becomes stronger.
The more you conform the facts to&nbsp;
fit your beliefs the narrower your&nbsp;&nbsp;
perspective becomes, until that narrow&nbsp;
reality becomes all that you can see.
Confirmation bias is the&nbsp;
genesis of the “I’m always&nbsp;&nbsp;
correct ego” …especially in political discourse.
What planet are they living on?
They must be living in an alternate reality?!
“I’m completely right about everything,&nbsp;
look at all the facts on my side…”
As opposing perspectives become narrower,&nbsp;
discourse about facts that are being interpreted&nbsp;&nbsp;
differently becomes nearly impossible because&nbsp;
both sides of an argument are seeing the evidence&nbsp;&nbsp;
through the lens of their theories and only&nbsp;
looking for what confirms their existing beliefs.
According to Daniel Kahneman,
“A reliable way to make people believe&nbsp;
in falsehoods is frequent repetition,&nbsp;&nbsp;
because familiarity is not&nbsp;
easily distinguished from truth.”
When people hear the same thing repeated enough&nbsp;
times, and facts may as well go out the window.
Echo chambers are the flywheels for&nbsp;
frequent repetition and dissemination&nbsp;&nbsp;
of ideas. The same ideas are shared, liked&nbsp;
and repeated and any new beliefs are quickly&nbsp;&nbsp;
shut down. All divergent thinking&nbsp;
and opinions begin to disappear.
There is no way to eliminate confirmation Bias,&nbsp;
only ways to reduce its effect on you. First is&nbsp;&nbsp;
to simply become aware this mind trap exists, if&nbsp;
time allows you to reflect, try to “Think Gray”,&nbsp;&nbsp;
If you truly want to become an independent&nbsp;
thinker, you need to explore the gray areas&nbsp;&nbsp;
and expand beyond the hive mind of the group.&nbsp;
Get your information from a variety of sources&nbsp;&nbsp;
and avoid being influenced into a belief because&nbsp;
it's what others are telling you to think or has&nbsp;&nbsp;
been repeated enough times you accept it as truth.&nbsp;
Confirmation Bias is a perspective narrower, Widen&nbsp;&nbsp;
your perspective, in most cases the objective&nbsp;
facts lie somewhere in between in the gray area.
The Baader Meinhof Phenomenon
You buy a certain brand of&nbsp;
car, and all of a sudden,&nbsp;&nbsp;
you start seeing that car everywhere,&nbsp;
whereas you didn’t in the past.
When you learn a new word or new concept, suddenly&nbsp;
you start seeing it everywhere in your life.
You start thinking, “Wow, this is weird,&nbsp;
How is it possible I’ve never seen this&nbsp;&nbsp;
word in my life and now I have seen&nbsp;
it 3 times this week or why do I keep&nbsp;&nbsp;
seeing those new shoes everywhere I go&nbsp;
now, they must be becoming so popular.
The Baader Meinhof phenomenon is an&nbsp;
illusion in which, after noticing&nbsp;&nbsp;
something for the first time, there is a&nbsp;
tendency to notice it more often. It occurs&nbsp;&nbsp;
when increased awareness of something creates&nbsp;
the illusion that it is appearing more often.
This phenomenon is augmented by two&nbsp;
other biases, The Recency Effect,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which inflates the importance of&nbsp;
recent stimuli, and confirmation bias,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which confirms in your mind these strange&nbsp;
coincidences you think you are having and then&nbsp;&nbsp;
perpetuates your search to keep confirming that&nbsp;
these coincidences must have some kind of meaning.
Basically, our brains are master&nbsp;
pattern recognition machines that&nbsp;&nbsp;
are always searching for meaning in data.
What is amazing are all the patterns&nbsp;
and stimuli flooding you every single&nbsp;&nbsp;
day that your brain simply ignores&nbsp;
because it is not in your awareness.
We only see the things we are looking out for.
In reality you have most likely seen&nbsp;
that word or car a number of times,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but your mind simply wasn’t&nbsp;
interested in noticing it.
Zeigarnik Effect
We can almost always remember incomplete&nbsp;
tasks, but we easily forget completed tasks.
To put simply, Incomplete tasks will stick&nbsp;
around in our memory longer than completed tasks.
Originally it was believed that the only way to&nbsp;
prevent the Zeigarnik Effect from gnawing away at&nbsp;&nbsp;
our thoughts was to complete the incomplete tasks,&nbsp;
however further research into the Zeigarnik Effect&nbsp;&nbsp;
found that simply having or writing down a plan to&nbsp;
complete the task was enough to stop the effect.
So if you find yourself awake at night with&nbsp;
these incomplete tasks stressing you out,&nbsp;&nbsp;
grab a pen and pad and write down a quick&nbsp;
plan to get the job done. Getting the tasks&nbsp;&nbsp;
out of your head and onto paper combats this&nbsp;
effect and will give you more peace of mind.
The Paradox of Choice
At a supermarket, two experiments were conducted.
In the first experiment, 24 different types of jam&nbsp;&nbsp;
were available to freely test&nbsp;
and buy for a discounted price.
In the second experiment, only&nbsp;
6 different types of jam were&nbsp;&nbsp;
available to freely test and&nbsp;
buy for a discounted price.
The first experiment attracted&nbsp;
60% of shoppers and 3% bought jam.
The second experiment attracted 40%&nbsp;
of the shoppers and 30% bought jam.
Even though more shoppers were initially&nbsp;
attracted to more variety…...With less&nbsp;&nbsp;
choices the supermarket was able&nbsp;
to sell 10x the amount of jam.
This is the paradox of choice. For most people&nbsp;
a large selection of any given product is seen&nbsp;&nbsp;
as a net positive. But once the number&nbsp;
of choices increases past a threshold,&nbsp;&nbsp;
our subjective state becomes negative and&nbsp;
leads to inner paralysis and decision fatigue.
The paradox of choice can also be found&nbsp;
in modern day dating. In the past,&nbsp;&nbsp;
you would marry people you met locally.&nbsp;
Nowadays we have too many choices,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and you may think that all that variety would&nbsp;
make it easier to find the perfect partner,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but more optimal decisions can be made when&nbsp;
given a smaller amount of options to choose from.
When faced with a small number of options,&nbsp;
people can easily weigh the pros and cons&nbsp;&nbsp;
of each and be fairly satisfied&nbsp;
with whichever option they chose.
When faced with a large number of options, knowing&nbsp;
which option is best becomes more difficult,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the more options there are, the more&nbsp;
chances there are of feeling regret.&nbsp;&nbsp;
With More options , the more you feel&nbsp;
the need to compare. The attractive&nbsp;&nbsp;
features of the alternatives, diminish&nbsp;
the satisfaction in your final choice.
Even if we have made an excellent&nbsp;
decision, the opportunity costs of&nbsp;&nbsp;
the other options subtract from the&nbsp;
overall satisfaction of our choice.
Too many choices also often leads to people not&nbsp;&nbsp;
making any choice and giving&nbsp;
up on the endeavor altogether.

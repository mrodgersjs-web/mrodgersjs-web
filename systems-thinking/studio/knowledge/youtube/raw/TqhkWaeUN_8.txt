Not many things in software&nbsp;
design get called a ‘law’.&nbsp;&nbsp;
For most programming decisions&nbsp;
we infamously say, “it depends”.
But not Conway’s Law. This applies&nbsp;
to every architecture. It says that
"Any organisation that designs a system&nbsp;
will produce a design whose structure is&nbsp;&nbsp;
a copy of the organisation's&nbsp;
communication structure."
You can draw the best boxes and arrows that&nbsp;
slice up your problem domain brilliantly,&nbsp;
but if that doesn’t reflect how the&nbsp;
developers communicate with each other,&nbsp;&nbsp;
you’re going to have problems.
Let’s take look at some examples…
Let’s say that all the front-end developers&nbsp;
in a company work in the same team&nbsp;&nbsp;
and talk to each other daily.
They concentrate on enhancing&nbsp;&nbsp;
the user experience and grow their&nbsp;
understanding of their users together.
They agree on the frameworks and&nbsp;
styles that work best for them,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and when they read each other’s code,&nbsp;
they can just talk to each other,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which makes it easier to understand&nbsp;
the code and interact with it.&nbsp;
The code becomes coupled by function calls&nbsp;
and also by implicit shared assumptions.&nbsp;
You can see how the team most likely creates&nbsp;
one cohesive and unified user interface system.
Other teams might have requirements too, but won’t&nbsp;
find it as easy to communicate with the frontend&nbsp;&nbsp;
developers as they do with each other.
Maybe they have to schedule a meeting,&nbsp;&nbsp;
go through the team’s manager, or put written&nbsp;
requirements straight into the backlog.
Now, imagine the rest of the organisation&nbsp;
is sliced by layers in the tech stack,&nbsp;&nbsp;
with a back-end team specialising&nbsp;
in building server-side logic,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and a separate team managing the database.
Each team naturally builds their own system,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and we end up with a layered architecture;&nbsp;
a mirror image of the team structure.
Each team excels in their respective areas,&nbsp;
but have to collaborate to build features.&nbsp;
So when the front-end team want to display some&nbsp;
new information, they request that the backend&nbsp;&nbsp;
team make it available via some API endpoint.
The backend team design something that returns&nbsp;&nbsp;
the information in the way&nbsp;
they think makes most sense.&nbsp;
The same thing happens when the backend&nbsp;
team has requirements for the database team.
A layered architecture naturally forms, and&nbsp;
the interfaces between the layers has been&nbsp;&nbsp;
designed based on the communication&nbsp;
that has happened between the teams.
Let’s take another example where the organisation&nbsp;
is split by stages in the product lifecycle:&nbsp;
a team of developers, a team of&nbsp;
testers, and a team of operations.
As a product passes through its lifecycle,&nbsp;
it passes between different teams.
Developers write the source code.
Then the communication is through&nbsp;&nbsp;
a well-defined handoff process where they&nbsp;
document the expected behaviour and hand&nbsp;&nbsp;
over to the testing team, who write&nbsp;
an automated test suite based on that.
The testing team would tend to build a&nbsp;
unified test suite with a set of standard&nbsp;&nbsp;
testing practices across all tests.
They’ll refactor tests according to&nbsp;&nbsp;
their knowledge of the whole suite,&nbsp;
extracting common test arrangement&nbsp;&nbsp;
or assertions into shared functions.
The test suite becomes its own system,&nbsp;&nbsp;
with tests having more knowledge of each&nbsp;
other than of the code they’re testing.
And then later, the operations&nbsp;
team manages the release.&nbsp;
They’ll provision the infrastructure, deploy&nbsp;
the software, build dashboards and alerts&nbsp;&nbsp;
based off their understanding&nbsp;
of what the software does.&nbsp;
Again, this all becomes almost its own subsystem,&nbsp;
and our architecture mirrors the team structure.
But what if you organise into cross-functional&nbsp;
teams containing designers, developers,&nbsp;&nbsp;
testers, SREs, everyone needed to&nbsp;
solve a specific business problem,&nbsp;&nbsp;
with each team centred around a business domain.
Now the architecture will end&nbsp;
up more vertically sliced,&nbsp;&nbsp;
with the front-end and back-end concerns&nbsp;
of a feature more closely related to each&nbsp;&nbsp;
other than to their counterparts&nbsp;
in another part of the business.
Each slice is owned by one team, who&nbsp;
make the decisions on how it’s built,&nbsp;&nbsp;
what the public interface&nbsp;
looks like, and crucially,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the model of its domain and even the&nbsp;
words used to describe the objects.
This starts to look like the kind of&nbsp;
architecture promoted by Domain-Driven Design:&nbsp;
splitting the software into subsystems aligned&nbsp;&nbsp;
to problem domains, each with&nbsp;
their own model and language.
When there’s a dependency between two&nbsp;
domains, the teams need to communicate.&nbsp;
Each team only has expertise in their domain, so&nbsp;
they’ll have to simplify things in conversations&nbsp;&nbsp;
with each other by abstracting away&nbsp;
some of their domain’s complexity.&nbsp;
Then the teams design the interface between&nbsp;
the systems based on that conversation.
And because teams are cross-functional, the&nbsp;
integration could happen in the frontend,&nbsp;&nbsp;
backend, wherever the teams agree makes most&nbsp;&nbsp;
sense. It’s the discussion between&nbsp;
the teams that will decide that.
So once we recognise that Conway’s law is a thing,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and our architecture will eventually resemble&nbsp;
the communication structures of our organisation,&nbsp;&nbsp;
then when we have a desired architecture we&nbsp;
want to get to, we can intentionally shape&nbsp;&nbsp;
the organisation in a way that will produce it.
This is called the Inverse Conway Manoeuvre.&nbsp;
Now it’s the architecture we intend to build&nbsp;
that influences how we shape the teams.
And if you’re practicing Domain-Driven&nbsp;
Design and you’ve identified separate&nbsp;&nbsp;
problem domains and designed&nbsp;
Bounded Contexts for your system,&nbsp;
this can be a great way to&nbsp;
build that architecture.&nbsp;
Each team can work in their own Bounded Contexts&nbsp;
and develop their own models and language.
You could also see how this works well with a&nbsp;
microservices architecture, where each team is&nbsp;&nbsp;
responsible for building and running their&nbsp;
own services and defining their own APIs.
You can watch my videos on both of&nbsp;
those concepts on my channel here.
You can combine all these&nbsp;
ideas together if you like,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but remember that Conway’s law exists whether&nbsp;
you’re doing these other things or not.&nbsp;
Keep it in mind when designing your&nbsp;
architecture and organising your teams.

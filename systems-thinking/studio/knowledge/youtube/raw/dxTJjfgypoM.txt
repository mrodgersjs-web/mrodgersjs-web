How often do you feel stuck or
overwhelmed when making important
decisions in life? Can we really control
the choices we make or are we just
victims of our circumstances?
Every day we are faced with countless
decisions. Some are small, what to have
for lunch, which route to take to work.
Others are life-changing. Should we take
that new job, make a big financial
investment, or start a new relationship?
Decision-M isn't just a skill. It's the
foundation of how we shape our lives.
Poor decisions can lead to regret while
wise choices can lead to fulfillment and
success. The Stoics like Marcus
Aurelius, Senica, and Epictitus believe
that how we make decisions defines who
we are and how we live. Their teachings
offer timeless wisdom on making better
choices, especially in times of stress
and uncertainty. In this video, we will
explore the Stoic approach to decision-m
and how it can help you become more
confident, rational, and at peace with
your choices. By the end of this video,
you'll learn how to make decisions with
a clear mind without being swayed by
emotions or external pressure. Stoicism
originated in ancient Greece and was
later developed by Roman philosophers.
It teaches us to live in accordance with
nature, except what we cannot control
and focus on what we can influence, our
thoughts, actions, and reactions. One of
the core principles of stoicism is that
we don't control external events, but we
do control how we respond to them. This
principle is essential when it comes to
decision-m. Often we feel pressured by
circumstances, emotions, or others
expectations. But the Stoics show us how
to filter out these distractions and
focus on what truly matters. The Stoic
philosopher Epictitus famously stated,
"Some things are up to us and some
things are not." This is known as the
dichotomy of control which divides our
lives into two categories. One, things
we can control, our thoughts, attitudes,
choices, and actions. Two, things we
cannot control, external events, other
people's actions and outcomes. By
applying this concept to decision-m, we
learn that we should focus only on what
we can control, our decision-making
process, our actions, and our attitudes.
While accepting that we cannot control
the results or external events, focus on
virtue, acting with wisdom, justice,
courage, and self-discipline. The Stoics
believe that living virtuously leads to
a good life and that decisions should be
made with these virtues in mind rather
than emotions or superficial desires.
Emotions are natural responses to
external stimuli. However, the Stoics
believe that we must separate our
emotions from our actions to make clear,
rational decisions. Marcus Aurelius in
his meditations reflects on how emotions
like anger, fear, and desire cloud
judgment and lead to poor choices. In
order to make better decisions, we must
learn to regulate these emotions and
prevent them from driving our actions.
The stoic approach to emotions can be
summarized by the idea that we should
not suppress emotions, but rather
observe them and then decide how to
respond.
Practical exercise. When you feel an
emotion, eg anger, excitement, anxiety,
rising during decision-making, pause,
reflect. What is the cause of this
emotion? Is it something I can control
or is it external? How should I respond
in a virtuous way? Example, imagine
you're making a career decision and
you're feeling pressured to accept a job
out of fear of missing out. The stoic
approach would be to pause, evaluate the
job based on its alignment with your
values and goals rather than your fear
and make a decision grounded in reason.
Wisdom is considered the highest virtue
in stoicism. It is the ability to
understand what is truly important and
act according to that understanding.
When making decisions, wisdom helps us
to weigh different options, anticipate
consequences, and make choices that
align with our long-term values. Senica
teaches that the wise man is not
disturbed by external events but remains
calm and rational acting according to
reason and virtue. Practical application
to cultivate wisdom in decision-m ask
yourself one will this decision align
with my long-term values? Two, what
would a wise person do in this
situation? Three, what will the
consequences be in the long term?
Self-reflection is another key stoic
tool. Before making a decision, the
Stoics practice journaling or mental
reflection to clear their minds and
evaluate their choices more clearly.
Practical exercise. Take a few moments
each day to reflect on your decisions.
Consider what went well, what could have
been improved, and how your actions
align with your core values. Step one,
clarify your values and goals. The first
step in stoic decision-making is to
define your core values and long-term
goals. Without clarity about what is
truly important to you, you will likely
make decisions based on fleeting
emotions or external pressures.
Exercise. Write down your top five
values. Then, for each decision you
face, ask yourself, does this decision
align with my values? Step two, analyze
the situation objectively. When faced
with a decision, the Stoics encourage us
to take a step back and evaluate the
situation without bias. Objectivity
allows us to see the facts clearly and
make rational decisions. Exercise. List
out the pros and cons of each option.
Avoid making assumptions or judgments
based on emotion. Stick to the facts.
Step three. Consider the long-term
consequences. Stoics believe in
long-term thinking. In decision-m, ask
yourself, what are the long-term effects
of this decision? How will it impact my
life and the lives of others? Exercise.
Imagine that this decision is already in
the past. Reflect on its outcome and how
you feel about it. This exercise will
help you make choices that align with
your future well-being. Step four, take
action and accept the outcome. Once a
decision is made, take decisive action
and commit fully to it. The Stoics
emphasize that we should not dwell on
the potential outcomes or second-guess
ourselves. Instead, focus on doing our
best and then accept the outcome with
equinimity. Exercise. After taking
action, remind yourself, I did what I
could with the information available.
The outcome is beyond my control. Case
study one. Marcus Aurelius and
leadership. Scenario. Marcus Aurelius
had to make tough decisions during his
reign, including how to handle the
Marcomomanic Wars, which threatened the
Roman Empire. Stoic decision-making.
Marcus used his stoic training to focus
on the larger picture, protecting the
empire, and maintaining the integrity of
his leadership while not allowing
emotions like fear or anger to cloud his
judgment. Case study two, Epictitus and
personal freedom. scenario. Epictitus, a
former slave, had to decide how to
respond to the injustice of his slavery.
Stoic decision-making. Instead of
letting anger control him, Epictitus
focused on his internal freedom,
choosing to live virtuously no matter
his external circumstances. His decision
to embrace his inner freedom led to his
philosophical work, which continues to
inspire millions. In conclusion, stoic
decisionmaking isn't about making
perfect choices every time. It's about
making decisions with wisdom, clarity,
and virtue. By practicing stoic
principles, we can navigate life's
challenges more effectively, creating a
life of inner peace and fulfillment.
Final encouragement, start small. Begin
with daily decisions and apply stoic
principles. Over time, you'll notice how
much calmer and more confident you
become in your ability to make sound
choices.
If you want to start making better
decisions, start today. Reflect on your
values, stay focused, and stick to it.

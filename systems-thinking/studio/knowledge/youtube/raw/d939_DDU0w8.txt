The concept of emergent phenomenon explains that any&nbsp;
high energy, far-from-equilibrium system&nbsp;&nbsp;
will organize itself in ways that cannot&nbsp;
be predicted from knowing its individual&nbsp;components.
In a nutshell, an emergent system will organize itself so that energy flows within the system will be less chaotic.
Emergence is not a theory; it is a&nbsp;
way of looking at many different kinds of energetic systems.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Emergence is closely related to self-organization,&nbsp;
complexity and evolution. Many things&nbsp;&nbsp;
exhibit emergent behavior. Animals, ecosystems,&nbsp;
oceanic currents, hurricanes and spiral galaxies,&nbsp;&nbsp;
hydrothermal systems, and civilizations are just&nbsp;
a few examples. In emergent systems, low-level rules for constituent parts give rise to
higher-level system complexity. Entirely new properties&nbsp;
and behaviors "emerge," without direction and with&nbsp;&nbsp;
characteristics that cannot be predicted&nbsp;
from knowledge of the constituents alone.
In the January issue of GSA Today
Bob Stern of UT Dallas and Taras Gerya of ETH Zurich discuss how thinking about
emergence can help us gain new perspectives about earth history.
In this short Groundwork article they discuss how thinking about emergence
can help us understand Earth and other silicate planets and moons
and how their tectonics evolved. Convecting silicate planets are high energy, far-from equilibrium&nbsp;&nbsp;
systems, and as their interiors cool, their&nbsp;
lithospheres respond by becoming thicker, denser,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and stronger. On Earth these tectonic styles are likely to have emerged into the plate tectonic system we see today.

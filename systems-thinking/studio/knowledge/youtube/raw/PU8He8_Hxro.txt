&gt;&gt; Let me be precise.
Coordination tax is the measurable
overhead introduced by agentic systems
when they require fragile interfaces,
brittle state, or fuzzy decision
boundaries. It shows up as longer lead
time, increased rework, and slow
decision latency. And you can instrument
those directly.
&gt;&gt; Hm. And for leaders, the immediate
question is where am I paying it? There
are three predictable spots: interfaces,
state management, and decision
boundaries. And each has a different
fix.
&gt;&gt; Start with interfaces.
Imagine your agent calls an internal
endpoint and the field
customer_status_code
comes back null in certain edge cases.
The agent flags it and a human gets
pulled in to decide what's valid. That
pull multiplies as you scale.
&gt;&gt; Wait. You mean we shouldn't be tuning
prompts every week when those edge cases
show up?
&gt;&gt; Not as a first resort. Frequent prompt
tuning is a smell. Tighten the contract
first. Explicit field semantics,
documented edge behavior, and a version
API so agents don't break on small
changes.
&gt;&gt; Good. Next is state management. Agents
that assume a single, up-to-date context
suffer when multiple partial contexts
exist. If your onboarding flow stores
context across caches instead of a
single shard like onboarding context V1,
you'll get conflicting recommendations
and needless reconciliation.
&gt;&gt; Exactly. Pick one canonical shard for
the flow and accept that other teams get
read-only projections. Writable copies
are what create the tower of
reconciliations.
&gt;&gt; And the third spot, decision boundaries.
If it's unclear when an agent should act
versus when a human should intervene,
escalation becomes default. Meetings
balloon, throughput slows, and
automation births bureaucracy. Give me
an example of an intense signal.
&gt;&gt; A simple boolean or enum, intent equals
onboard, verify, escalate. Add a
confidence score, too. Use a rule like
escalate only if confidence less than
60% and no resolution within 4 hours.
That binary rule prevents constant human
triage.
&gt;&gt; Curious. Measurement, then. How do you
prove the tax shrank and you didn't just
move it out of sight?
&gt;&gt; Keep metrics simple and redundant.
Instrument three signals for a pilot
flow. Trigger to resolution latency,
rework rate, percentage of items
returned for correction. And decision
reversals, percentage of agent decisions
later changed by a human.
Pair numbers with quick qualitative
sampling.
&gt;&gt; Also, a guardrail on sample size. If the
flow sees fewer than 100 events a day,
extend the pilot to 7 days or aggregate
multiple flows. Otherwise, noise will
dominate your signal.
&gt;&gt; And keep changes minimal and reversible.
A single narrow contract tweak can be
rolled in an hour if it increases
incidents.
Treat metrics as hypotheses, not
absolutes.
&gt;&gt; Here's a 24/72 hour play you can run
this week.
Day one, pick one high friction flow,
onboarding, triage, or reporting, and
map every handoff.
Day two, add the three gauges and roll
one contract change that clarifies an
ambiguous field or adds an intent flag.
Day three, run under normal load and
evaluate. Small change, fast signal.
&gt;&gt; We tested this at a mid-sized SaaS firm.
Their onboarding agent routed compliance
documents and flagged borderline cases.
We added a one-line rationale, a
confidence score, and tightened the
contract so borderline cases were routed
to a specialist queue.
Within 48 hours, latency dropped and
rework fell by nearly 30%.
&gt;&gt; Real result, not a model upgrade. But a
caution, don't over-optimize
micro-coordination.
If you lock contracts too tightly, you
can block legitimate emergent cases.
Always include rollback criteria and an
incident playbook.
&gt;&gt; One common stall is arguing over state
ownership. My recommendation, assign the
shard to the team closest to the
customer outcome. Give them the writable
shard and require minimal read contracts
for others. Create projections for
different views rather than writable
copies.
&gt;&gt; I'll push back a bit. Closeness to the
customer doesn't guarantee incentives
for global hygiene. Pair ownership with
a small measurable SLA and a monitoring
strip that catches divergence early.
Ownership plus accountability, not
ownership alone.
&gt;&gt; Fair.
Ownership, SLAs, and minimal monitoring.
Practical, not bureaucratic.
&gt;&gt; Quick recap for implementation. Spot the
flow, map handoffs, measure latency,
rework reversals, make one narrow
reversible contract change, and evaluate
with both numbers and a few failed case
reads. Patterns create power. Look for
repeated handoffs that fail.

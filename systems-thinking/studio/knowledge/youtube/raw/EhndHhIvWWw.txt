Diffusion models are now used in just&nbsp;
about every domain you can think of:
generating images, videos, music, 3D models&nbsp;
— and even discovering new molecules.
But while the results are very impressive, the&nbsp;
underlying theory can feel a bit, well… dense.
There are lots of important details, and the&nbsp;
math isn’t always friendly at first glance.
We’ll try to untangle the key&nbsp;
ideas behind diffusion models by
following step by step the research&nbsp;
paper that made them so popular.
The paper that brought diffusion models&nbsp;
into the spotlight is called "Denoising
Diffusion Probabilistic Models", or&nbsp;
DDPM, by Jonathan Ho and collaborators,
and it was published in 2020.
Despite its reputation,
this paper did not introduce diffusion&nbsp;
models, nor did it coin the term.
The earliest known reference to diffusion&nbsp;
models as we know them comes from a 2015
paper titled "Deep Unsupervised Learning&nbsp;
using Nonequilibrium Thermodynamics",
by Jascha Sohl-Dickstein and colleagues.
In 2015, GANs were considered state of
the art in image generation research, and&nbsp;
diffusion models received little attention.
In contrast, the 2020 DDPM paper&nbsp;
quickly gained traction—it is now
the most cited work in the field, with more&nbsp;
than twenty thousand citations as of 2025.
If you look up diffusion models&nbsp;
on any search engine, this is
probably the first paper you'll come across.
The main contribution of this paper was to
take diffusion models from a theoretical&nbsp;
concept—previously limited to simple toy
datasets—into a state-of-the-art&nbsp;
technique for image generation.
On the technical side, it mainly&nbsp;
introduced a much simpler training
objective compared to the 2015 version.
This made the method more accessible,
and highlighted interesting connections&nbsp;
to earlier works on denoising models.
Now, let’s break down the core idea.
In the context of data generation,
we start with a probability distribution p&nbsp;
of x, which represents our training data.
For instance in the case of images, we can&nbsp;
think of this distribution as representing
all natural images, or even just a subset&nbsp;
of natural images containing only faces.
Of course, this distribution is so complex&nbsp;
that we can't really find a single expression
to describe it completely, and there&nbsp;
are probably no such expression anyway.
But even without an explicit formula,&nbsp;
we still want to generate new images,
which is equivalent to sampling new&nbsp;
points from this underlying distribution.
So the challenge is to find a way to create&nbsp;
new samples without actually having a good,
comprehensive, way of describing the distribution.
Diffusion models tackle this problem with
a seemingly unrelated process — by&nbsp;
removing Gaussian noise from images.
First, we gradually corrupt the&nbsp;
images by adding Gaussian noise.
At each step, a small amount of noise is added,&nbsp;
slowly erasing the structure of the image.
If we continue this process for many steps,&nbsp;
the image eventually turns into pure noise.
The key idea is then to train a model&nbsp;
that learns to slowly reverse these steps,
and remove the noise bit by bit.
If the model is effective,
it should be able to start from random noise and&nbsp;
refine it step by step into a meaningful image.
Now, let’s see how the DDPM paper&nbsp;
formulates this process mathematically.
Let's begin with an image, which&nbsp;
we'll call x-zero, since it's
the starting point of the noising process.
For now this image does not contain any noise.
To generate the next image, x-one, we use a&nbsp;
conditional distribution: q of x-one given x-zero.
We produce x-one by adding some&nbsp;
amount of Gaussian noise epsilon.
The amount of noise is controlled&nbsp;
by a scalar value, beta.
Then actually the distribution describing&nbsp;
x-one is just a Gaussian centered on
x-zero with noise of variance beta.
So rather than seeing this as adding
noise to x-zero, we can just imagine that we&nbsp;
take a sample from a Gaussian distribution of
variance beta, centered on x-zero.
Then, to go to the next step,
we do the same thing.
This time we label the
distribution as q of x-two knowing x-one.
Again the operation is simply taking the
previous step and adding some&nbsp;
amount beta of Gaussian noise.
Which is the same as taking a&nbsp;
sample from a Gaussian distribution
centered on x-one with variance beta.
Now since we just sum the noise terms,
we can go straight from x-zero to x-two.
We created x-two by adding twice as much noise,
which is the same as sampling from a Gaussian&nbsp;
centered on x-zero, with variance two beta.
Now we keep repeating this process, step&nbsp;
by step, until we reach the timestep t.
If we think a little, we can write a&nbsp;
simple formula to describe what happens
at any given step: we simply add t times&nbsp;
beta amount of Gaussian noise to x-zero.
So at timestep t, x-t knowing&nbsp;
x-zero is a Gaussian distribution
centered at x-zero, with variance t-beta.
Now we have an issue with this process.
What we wanted was a process&nbsp;
that gradually transforms our
data into a standard normal distribution;&nbsp;
clearly, that's not what's happening here.
The mean always stays fixed at x-zero and&nbsp;
is never modified by the process, while the
variance keeps increasing indefinitely.
So in this setup, there is absolutely
no chance that our diffusion process&nbsp;
converges to the normal distribution.
In this diffusion process, the variance just keeps&nbsp;
growing without bound: in the litterature we often
say that the variance explodes; and we call&nbsp;
this process the variance exploding diffusion.
In the DDPM paper, the authors made the choice to&nbsp;
use a noising process that converges to a normal
distribution, which is often called variance&nbsp;
preserving, so let's see how they do it.
Let’s now define a diffusion process that&nbsp;
actually works — one that gradually transforms
our data into a standard normal distribution.
For this, we need to satisfy two conditions:
the mean has got to converge to&nbsp;
zero and the variance to one.
To do that we'll modify the way we&nbsp;
go from a given step to the next.
Obviously, we'll need to change the&nbsp;
mean of our distribution at each step.
And so we'll need a coefficient in front&nbsp;
of the mean to make it decrease to zero.
This magic number that we will use is&nbsp;
square root of one minus the variance beta.
Hm well, why do we choose&nbsp;
this number in particular ?
To be honest, multiple other valid choices&nbsp;
exist — however, the authors of the DDPM paper
specifically opted for this coefficient.
The main reason for this choice
is probably its simplicity.
I'll skip the derivations because
this is quite honestly pretty boring, but the&nbsp;
important thing is that this choice results in
a straightforward and convenient expression for&nbsp;
the conditional distribution at any time step.
So to go from the noiseless input x-zero, to&nbsp;
a given time step of our diffusion process,
we just have to apply this little formula.
It depends on the term alpha-t bar,
which is simply defined as one minus&nbsp;
beta, raised to the power of t.
Now does this thing actually&nbsp;
converge to the normal distribution ?
Well if we choose beta strictly between zero&nbsp;
and one, it is easy to see that alpha-t-bar
will converge to zero as t grows to infinity.
Then the mean of the conditional distribution
goes to zero, while the variance goes to one.
And just like that, we finally have a proper
variance preserving diffusion process.
So, this formula is supposed to take our
distribution of data p of x, and slowly&nbsp;
transform it into a normal distribution:
let's see this on a simple 1D example.
Let's say this is our original
noiseless data distribution p of x.
After applying just one diffusion step,
the distribution barely changes.
However, if we repeat this process
for about a hundred steps, we can clearly&nbsp;
see the effect: the mean approaches zero,
and the spread of the distribution stabilizes.
And the nice thing is that we can go directly
from the input image to a given time-step !
Now, if you've explored the DDPM paper yourself,
you might have noticed they use a slightly&nbsp;
different definition for alpha-bar.
In practice, diffusion models&nbsp;
commonly apply varying amounts of
noise at each step, rather than a fixed beta.
Indeed, any noise schedule can be chosen as
long as each beta remains between zero and one.
How does that fact impact our formula to go from
the noiseless image to any timestep ?
Well when beta varies at each step,
alpha-bar at step t simply becomes the product&nbsp;
of one minus beta at every previous step,
which is the expression you can find in the paper.
So we have now defined a proper diffusion process
that will progressively transform any&nbsp;
distribution into a normal distribution.

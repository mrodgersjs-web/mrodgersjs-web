Anchoring. Anchoring is the first number
that gets thrown into a negotiation. It
could be a salary offer, a house price,
or how much you're willing to pay for a
used car. Whatever that first number is,
it doesn't just start the conversation.
It shapes everything that follows. Your
brain latches onto it, even when you
know you shouldn't. That initial figure
becomes the reference point, and every
other number gets measured against it.
If someone offers you $60,000 for a job,
suddenly $55,000 feels low, and $65,000
feels high, even if the role is actually
worth $80,000. The anchor pulled your
expectations toward it. Here's how it
works. When you hear a number first,
your brain uses it as a mental starting
line. Even if you try to ignore it, even
if you think it's unfair, that number
has already set the frame. Psychologists
have tested this over and over. Show
people a random number before asking
them to guess something completely
unrelated, and their guesses will drift
toward that number. The effect is
invisible, but powerful. So, if you want
to use anchoring yourself, go first.
Throw out a number that's bold, but not
absurd. If you're selling something,
start higher than you expect to get. If
you're buying, start lower. The key is
believability. Go too extreme, and you
lose credibility. But go first with
confidence, and you've just set the
boundary for everything that comes next.
Even when the other side counters,
they're countering within the world your
anchor created. And here's the hardest
truth about anchoring. Even when both
people know the trick, it still works.
Awareness doesn't make you immune. The
first number still plants itself in your
mind, still tugs at your sense of what's
reasonable. That's why anchoring isn't
just a tactic, it's the opening move
that decides the shape of the entire
game. Mirroring. Mirroring is repeating
the last few words someone just said
back to them, but as a question. That's
it. You take their exact words, give
them an upward inflection, and wait. It
sounds too simple to matter, but it
triggers something automatic in the
human brain. When you hear your own
words reflected back, you instinctively
want to explain more, to clarify, to
fill in what you think the other person
didn't understand. So, you keep talking.
And the more you talk, the more you
reveal. Here's what happens in real
time. Someone says, "I can't go lower
than $500." You respond, "Lower than
500?" with a slight questioning tone.
They hear that and their brain
interprets it as confusion or doubt, so
they elaborate. "Well, I mean, I have
costs to cover and the market rate is
usually around that range, but I guess
if you're paying cash I could maybe do
475." You didn't argue. You didn't
counter. You just repeated three words
and they gave you new information and a
better price. Mirroring works because it
feels like active listening. The other
person thinks you're engaged, trying to
understand them, and you are, but
strategically. You're extracting details
they didn't plan to share. The beauty of
mirroring is that it requires almost no
effort and carries zero risk. You're not
making claims, not showing your hand.
You're just echoing and they're doing
all the work. People want to be
understood. They'll keep talking until
they feel like you get it. And while
they're talking, you're learning exactly
where the edges of their position really
are. Tactical silence. Tactical silence
is saying absolutely nothing after the
other person finishes talking. No
response, no counter, no
acknowledgement, just silence. And that
silence becomes unbearable. Human beings
are wired to avoid awkward pauses in
conversation. Dead air feels wrong,
uncomfortable, like something needs to
be fixed immediately. So, when you stay
quiet the other person's brain starts
working overtime to fill that gap. And
when they do, they usually give you
something valuable. Maybe they lower
their price without you asking. Maybe
they reveal a concern they were hiding.
Maybe they just keep talking and
accidentally expose the limits of their
position. The discomfort of silence
creates pressure and pressure makes
people move. Here's how it works in
practice. Someone makes you an offer.
Instead of responding right away, you
just pause. 5 seconds. 10 seconds. You
stay calm. you hold eye contact if
you're in person, and you let the quiet
sit there. That pause sends a message
without words. It signals doubt,
hesitation, dissatisfaction, and the
person across from you will interpret
that silence as rejection, even though
you haven't said a thing. So, they start
backpedaling, sweetening the deal,
offering explanations. Silence forces
them to negotiate against themselves.
The power of tactical silence lies in
restraint. You're not being rude or
difficult, you're simply refusing to
rush. And in that refusal, you create
space for them to second-guess,
reconsider, and concede. Most
negotiations are lost by the person who
speaks first after an offer lands.
Silence hands you control without you
having to say a single word. The flinch.
The flinch is a visible reaction of
surprise or disappointment when you hear
their number. You don't argue yet. You
don't counter. You just react. Your
eyebrows go up, you lean back slightly,
maybe you let out a small breath or
shake your head. The reaction itself
becomes the message, and that message
plants immediate doubt in the other
person's mind. They start wondering if
they aimed too high, if they misread the
situation, if maybe they should adjust
before you even say anything. The flinch
makes them question their own position
without you having to challenge it
directly. Here's why this works. When
someone states a price or makes a
demand, they're often unsure if they got
it right. They're testing the waters,
gauging your reaction. If you stay
neutral or accept too quickly, they
assume they left money on the table. But
if you flinch, if your face shows
genuine shock, they interpret that as
proof they overshot, and people hate
overreaching. They want to seem
reasonable, fair, competent. So, when
they see your reaction, they often walk
themselves back before you've said a
word. The key to the flinch is
authenticity. If it looks fake or
overdone, you lose credibility. But a
natural, honest reaction to a number
that genuinely surprises you carries
real weight. You can flinch physically
with body language or verbally with a
phrase like, "Wow, okay." Or, "That's
higher than I expected." Either way,
you're buying time and creating doubt.
The other person now has to justify
their number, defend it, or soften it.
And all you did was show them your face.
Good cop, bad cop. Good cop, bad cop is
two people playing opposite roles in the
same negotiation. One person is harsh,
unreasonable, and inflexible. The other
is friendly, understanding, and wants to
help you find a solution. You've seen
this in every police interrogation scene
on television. One detective slams the
table and threatens consequences. The
other leans in, offers water, and says
they can make this easier if you just
cooperate. The psychology is simple, but
devastatingly effective. After dealing
with the bad cop, the good cop feels
like relief. You start to trust them,
align with them, even feel grateful
toward them. So, when they ask for
concessions, you give them willingly
because they feel like they're on your
side, but they're not. They're working
together toward the exact same goal.
Here's how it plays out in real
negotiations. Maybe you're buying a car.
The salesperson says they'd love to give
you that price, but their manager would
never approve it. The manager is the bad
cop you never see. Then the salesperson
goes to fight for you and comes back
with a slightly better offer, acting
like they barely convinced their boss.
You feel like they went to bat for you,
so you meet them halfway. You just made
a concession to someone who was never
your ally. The door in the face. The
door in the face is asking for something
huge and unreasonable first, getting
rejected, then following up with what
you actually want. The second request
seems modest by comparison, and people
feel almost obligated to say yes because
they already turned you down once. The
technique gets its name from the image
of someone slamming a door in your face
after your first ask, but then cracking
it open for the second. Here's how the
psychology works. When someone refuses
your initial request, they feel a small
pang of guilt or social discomfort.
Saying no feels negative, even when the
ask was absurd. So, when you come back
with something smaller and more
reasonable, their brain sees it as a
compromise. They think you've already
conceded by lowering your demand. So,
now it's their turn to meet you halfway.
Reciprocity kicks in. You moved, so they
feel like they should move, too. Say you
want to borrow someone's car for the
weekend. Instead of asking directly, you
first ask if you can borrow it for the
entire month. They say no, obviously.
Then you say, "Okay, what about just
this weekend?" Suddenly, the weekend
feels small, manageable, reasonable.
They're more likely to agree because the
contrast makes your real request look
minor. The key is making sure your first
ask is big enough to get rejected, but
not so outrageous that you lose
credibility. If it sounds like a joke,
the tactic falls apart. But, if it seems
like a genuine stretch, the follow-up
lands perfectly. The rejection isn't
failure. The rejection is the setup that
makes your actual goal feel like the
fairer middle ground. BATNA. BATNA
stands for Best Alternative to a
Negotiated Agreement. In simpler terms,
it's your backup plan. What will you do
if this negotiation completely falls
apart? If you're negotiating a job offer
and it doesn't work out, do you have
another offer waiting? If you're selling
your car and the buyer walks away, do
you have other interested people lined
up? Your BATNA is your safety net, and
knowing what it is changes everything
about how you negotiate. When you have a
strong alternative, you negotiate from a
position of power. You can walk away
without fear because you're not
desperate. You don't need this deal, you
just want it on the right terms. That
confidence shows. You make bolder moves,
you hold your ground, you don't cave
under pressure. But, when your BATNA is
weak or nonexistent, you negotiate from
desperation, and desperation is visible.
The other side can sense it in your
tone, your concessions, your willingness
to accept terms you shouldn't. Here's
the reality. You cannot bluff a strong
BATNA. If you don't actually have
alternatives, pretending you do will
eventually collapse. The other person
will test you, call your bluff, and when
you don't walk, they'll know. So, the
goal isn't to fake a good BATNA. The
goal is to build one before you ever sit
down to negotiate. Apply for multiple
jobs, not just one. Get multiple quotes
before selling. Line up options so that
no single deal holds all your leverage.
Your BATNA determines whether you're
negotiating or begging. The stronger it
is, the less you need any particular
outcome. And when you don't need
something, you're suddenly in the best
position to get it. The nibble. The
nibble is asking for one more small
thing after the deal is essentially
done. The price has been agreed on, the
terms are set, everyone is mentally
ready to close. Then, right at the end,
you casually mention one last request.
Could you throw in free delivery? How
about an extended warranty? Would you
cover the installation fee? The other
side has already invested hours, maybe
days, into this negotiation. They're
tired. They want it finished, and your
request is small enough that saying no
feels petty, like torpedoing the entire
deal over something minor. So, they say
yes just to get it over with. That's the
nibble. It works because of timing and
psychology. By the time you ask, the
other person has already made their
mental commitment. They've agreed to the
big number. They've told their boss or
their spouse. They've started imagining
the outcome. Walking away now over a $50
add-on feels absurd. The sunk cost of
all that effort makes the small
concession feel cheaper than restarting
from scratch. Here's how to use it. Wait
until the main terms are locked in. Let
the other person feel like they've won,
like the hard part is behind them. Then,
introduce your nibble as an
afterthought, almost apologetically.
"Oh, one last thing. Would it be
possible to include shipping?" Keep it
genuinely small. If you ask for
something huge at the finish line,
you'll blow up the deal. But, if you
nibble correctly, you get an extra win
without renegotiating anything major.
The other side will give it to you just
to cross the finish line and move on.
The nibble doesn't reopen the
conversation. It ends it, but on
slightly better terms than before. The
ultimatum. The ultimatum is drawing a
hard line. Take it or leave it. Final
offer. This is non-negotiable. You're
eliminating all the back and forth and
forcing the other person to make a
decision right now. Either they accept
your terms or the deal ends. There's no
middle ground, no room for compromise,
no next round of discussion. Ultimatums
create urgency and finality. They cut
through endless negotiation and demand a
choice. When used correctly, they can
close deals that would otherwise drag on
forever or collapse entirely. But when
used wrong, they blow everything up.
Here's when an ultimatum works. You need
leverage first. If the other side needs
you more than you need them, an
ultimatum can push them to commit. If
you have a strong BATNA and you're
genuinely willing to walk away, then
drawing a line shows confidence and
clarity. It signals that you've reached
your limit and further discussion won't
change anything. The other person
respects that boundary because they
believe you'll enforce it. But here's
when it backfires. If you use an
ultimatum too early, before trust is
built or terms are explored, you come
across as aggressive and unreasonable.
If you're bluffing and the other side
calls you on it, you either have to back
down and lose all credibility or walk
away from a deal you actually wanted. An
ultimatum also generates resentment.
Even if they accept, they'll remember
being forced into a corner and that
damages long-term relationships. So the
rule is simple. Only deliver an
ultimatum when you're truly prepared to
walk away and live with the
consequences. If you're not ready to
lose the deal, don't threaten to end it
because once you say the words, you've
locked yourself in. And if they call
your bluff, you're done. Trading, not
giving. Trading, not giving means never
handing over a concession for free. If
they ask for something, you ask for
something in return. Every move you make
toward their position should cost them a
move toward yours. Otherwise, you're
just losing ground while they rack up
wins without giving anything back.
Negotiation is exchange, not charity.
When someone requests a discount, faster
delivery, better terms, or any kind of
accommodation, your instinct might be to
just say yes to keep things moving or
seem agreeable, but that's a mistake.
Free concessions signal weakness. They
tell the other side that asking gets
results, so they'll keep asking. And
worse, they'll assume there's more
you're hiding if you gave that up so
easily. Here's how to trade instead.
When they ask for something, respond
with a conditional phrase. If I can do
that, would you be willing to commit
today? Or I could lower the price to
that number if you increase the order
size. You're not refusing their request.
You're making it conditional on them
giving you something valuable in return.
This establishes reciprocity as the rule
of the conversation. Every exchange
becomes mutual. They learn quickly that
nothing comes without a trade. The
psychology behind this is simple. People
value what they work for more than
what's handed to them. If you give a
concession freely, they'll see it as
expected or even owed. But if they have
to trade for it, they'll feel like they
earned it through negotiation. That
satisfaction makes them more likely to
accept the overall deal. Trading also
keeps the negotiation balanced. You're
not the only one making sacrifices.
You're both moving, both compromising,
both invested in reaching an agreement.
And when both sides feel like they've
given and gained, the deal holds
stronger and lasts longer.

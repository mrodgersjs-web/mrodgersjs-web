In the early hours of June 6th, 1944,
the German High Command received
multiple reports of Allied landings in
Normandy. They had expected the Allied
invasion in northern France, not
Normandy, and as a result, they thought
it was a decoy and they didn't engage
their reserve Panzer divisions. The
uncertainty remained for several hours,
and by the time they released the Panzer
divisions, it was too late. The Allied
had a strong foothold in France from
which they started their liberation of
Western Europe.
I'm Arnauld Chevallier, Professor of
Strategy at IMD. As an executive, you
must make decisions under uncertainty
all the time, and we each have our own
favorite way of doing this. Some of us
rely fully on instincts. Others prefer
not to move forward until we're
absolutely sure we have the right
decision.
But neither one of these extremes is
ideal. Instincts can be misleading,
whereas as the German response to D-Day
shows, striving for full certainty might
result in you missing your window. The
bottom line is, as an executive, your
job isn't to eradicate uncertainty, but
to make it manageable.
And this session gives you concrete

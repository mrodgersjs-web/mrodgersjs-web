At this point in the series, we’ve&nbsp;
introduced aspects of the immune system&nbsp;&nbsp;
at the molecular, cellular, and organismal&nbsp;
levels. So now it’s time to start learning&nbsp;&nbsp;
about how all these different components work&nbsp;
together to recognize and eliminate pathogens.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Let’s start out by introducing the innate&nbsp;
immune system, so that we can get an overview&nbsp;&nbsp;
of some of the things we will discuss in&nbsp;
greater detail over the next few tutorials.&nbsp;
The innate immune system is an&nbsp;
evolutionarily ancient defense&nbsp;&nbsp;
system, with elements conserved across plants,&nbsp;
invertebrates, and even single-celled organisms.&nbsp;&nbsp;
It is called the innate immune system because it&nbsp;
consists of immune mechanisms that are hard-wired&nbsp;&nbsp;
to quickly recognize and neutralize a broad range&nbsp;
of threats, regardless of previous exposure. It&nbsp;&nbsp;
differs from the adaptive immune system, which&nbsp;
relies on highly antigen-specific defenses&nbsp;&nbsp;
that take some time to launch, and which are&nbsp;
strengthened by repeated exposure to the antigen.&nbsp;
So what are some of the most important&nbsp;
features of the innate immune system&nbsp;&nbsp;
that we will be investigating? The first is&nbsp;
barrier defense. We will learn about some of&nbsp;&nbsp;
the many strategies our bodies have evolved&nbsp;
to keep pathogens out of various surfaces.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Barrier defense goes way beyond just structural&nbsp;
barriers, however. There are also some incredible&nbsp;&nbsp;
chemical and biological defenses that occur at&nbsp;
barrier surfaces, and many of these defenses&nbsp;&nbsp;
are coordinated by the cells lining the skin,&nbsp;
airway, and gut. These cells are right at the&nbsp;&nbsp;
interface between what is outside versus&nbsp;
inside the body, and they represent the&nbsp;&nbsp;
first line of defense that a pathogen has&nbsp;
to overcome in order to infect a host.&nbsp;
If a pathogen is able to slip through&nbsp;
physical and chemical barriers,&nbsp;&nbsp;
one of the first internal immune defenses&nbsp;
it will meet is the complement system.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The complement system involves a series of&nbsp;
cleavable proteins that circulate through the&nbsp;&nbsp;
bloodstream, and this is activated when&nbsp;
proteins recognize microbial surfaces,&nbsp;&nbsp;
bind antigen-antibody complexes, or experience&nbsp;
low-level spontaneous cleavage. Activation&nbsp;&nbsp;
of the complement system initiates a chain&nbsp;
reaction of proteins cleaving other proteins,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and the byproducts of these reactions have a&nbsp;
broad range of effects, including recruiting&nbsp;&nbsp;
phagocytes, coating pathogens to mark them for&nbsp;
phagocytosis in a process called opsonization,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and even forming pores on microbial surfaces to&nbsp;
directly kill pathogens. We’ll talk much more&nbsp;&nbsp;
about this process a bit later in the series.
Once the initial signs of barrier breach&nbsp;&nbsp;
and infection begin to manifest, the affected&nbsp;
area enters a state of inflammation. We will learn&nbsp;&nbsp;
how to recognize the four signs of inflammation,&nbsp;
which include heat, redness, swelling, and pain,&nbsp;&nbsp;
as we will gain an understanding of what is&nbsp;
transpiring on the cellular and molecular level&nbsp;&nbsp;
when this happens. Although inflammation&nbsp;
gets a bad rap sometimes, we’ll learn how&nbsp;&nbsp;
important and effective this process is for&nbsp;
quickly containing and neutralizing a threat.&nbsp;
We’ve introduced innate immune cells and&nbsp;
pattern recognition receptors already,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but as we move forward we will devote more time to&nbsp;
understanding these powerful mechanisms of immune&nbsp;&nbsp;
defense as well. Many immunology courses can be&nbsp;
rather focused on the adaptive immune system,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and will thereby avoid going into detail&nbsp;
regarding aspects of innate immunity,&nbsp;&nbsp;
as some consider it to be less sophisticated or&nbsp;
less interesting than the adaptive immune system.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Again, this is perhaps due to the fact that&nbsp;
innate immunity is highly conserved across&nbsp;&nbsp;
so many species, while adaptive immunity is&nbsp;
found exclusively in vertebrates, thereby&nbsp;&nbsp;
representing evolutionary progress. But there is&nbsp;
so much to learn about innate immunity as well,&nbsp;&nbsp;
as this system is truly complex, elegant,&nbsp;
and incredibly important. So let’s get a&nbsp;&nbsp;
closer look at all these components&nbsp;
of the innate immune system now.

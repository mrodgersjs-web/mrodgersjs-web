Once he found the right way to present
his argument and got his article
published on his second try, Granet
really went to town with his basic idea
about weak ties as bridges between local
clusters of strong ties. A year after
publishing this journal article, he came

idea. The book expanded on his doctoral
dissertation research at Harvard in
which he collected information from a
sample of people who changed jobs. He
wanted to know how they found out about
the new jobs they eventually took and
invested a lot of time and effort in
tracing the chains of contacts people
had used.
This book has become a classic, too.
Frequently cited in business schools as
well as sociology courses. Grenovator
made a lot of money consulting with
corporations about recruiting their
employees. The bottom line of his
research found that just as you might
expect from the article you've read
here, it turned out that weak ties were
crucial to finding out about promising
career possibilities.
This book establishes the old saying
that it's not just what you know, but
who you know as a scientific fact of
occupational life. He was surprised to
find that most of the information chains
connecting people to their new employers
not only ran through weak ties most of
the time, but that these information
chains were not even that long. Usually
only a few steps removed between job
seeker and employee. The more weak ties
were involved, the shorter the chains
turned out to be and the more successful
the job search was.
Today, people seeking jobs are told to
cultivate such weak ties in their lives
deliberately. And if they have any
doubts about this, they're directed to
Granavet's book for the proof that it
works. The same network principles and
the same value of weak ties appears in
many other contexts not focused on
getting a job. If you consider the
members of the board of directors of any
major corporation in the United States
today, each of these business leaders
forms mostly weak ties with the other
members of that board of directors. They
don't want or need in most cases to
develop these ties into strong primary
ties. The weak ties formed sitting
around the boardroom table are the most
important thing. After the meeting, each
of these directors goes off in his or
her own direction and before long is
sitting in a meeting of another board of
directors for another corporation. In
fact, several studies have documented
that most of the top corporations in the
country are all interlocked in this way
with most of the others through the
boards of directors, forming what one
study called a seamless web of hands
across the boardroom table. Information
about business trends, labor relations,
economic opportunities, and many other
tidbits of news advantageous to each

this network of interlocking weak ties,
ensuring business success for all. In
case you're not a corporate executive
who needs to cultivate all these weak
boardroom ties, other aspects of life

ties. People experiencing desperate
personal challenges have found networks
of weak ties can be every bit as helpful

even solve problems created by others
strong ties or their loss or absence.
Granoveter himself illustrates how weak
ties also can play a crucial role in
political organization. He gives us the

Boston known as the West End where he
shows us an area filled with very strong

groups but with few or no weak ties
between these different families. As a
result, when the impersonal machinery of

Boston to tear down all the old
neighborhood and renew it with new
development, these residents had no
bridges across which they could
coordinate their resistance or even
exchange information about the threat
itself. This absence of weak ties doomed
the entire area to demolition. Even
though the people had very strong,
vibrant local clusters of primary
relationships and flourishing family
lives. This example leads Granet to ask
the next key question. What could have
saved the West End? What could have
created some weak ties there? This
question of what facilitates formation
of weak ties bridging through social
networks has become the subject of a lot
of research since Granaveter's article
appeared half a century ago. One
important answer seems to be
participation in organizations larger
and more inclusive than just a local
cluster or primary group of people.
Church congregations are good examples,
as are civic clubs and other community

opportunity for interactions between
people beyond the boundaries of their
immediate primary groups. Such
interactions don't have to become
primary ties. In fact, as weak ties,
they are even more important for
coordination of action and the spread of
information.
From the inception of the civil rights
movement in the 20th century to
contemporary social justice and
environmental movements, organizations
that create opportunities to form weak
ties have proven again and again to be
the key to success.

- One of the most important things
that you can do in the
data world is interpret the
meaning of the results.
And every now and then,
something really weird happens with data.
A good example of this is
known as Simpson's Paradox
or a paradox that happens
when you have aggregated
and disaggregated data.
This is best known from
a 1970s lawsuit on gender
discrimination at the University
of California Berkeley
for graduate admissions,
where it was found
that female applicants had a
lower overall acceptance rate
than male applicants.
Well, the university decided
we need to fix this problem,
and so they went and looked
at 48 different graduate
programs to find out what was going on.
And what they found was really weird.
Only a small number of
programs showed a preference
for male applicants
and an equal number of
for female applicants,
and the majority showed
no overall difference.
And the idea is, what happened?
It was there when we were
looking at everything,
but it disappeared when we were
looking at individual cases.
This is a statistical
paradox, and what happens
is that the female applicants
were generally applying
to programs that were smaller
and more selective, so they
accepted a smaller number
of candidates, whereas the male applicants
were generally applying to
programs that were larger
and had higher acceptance rates.
When you combine those things,
you can get an overall effective
discrimination even when
it's not there at the individual level.
A similar thing shows up, for instance,
when you're comparing states.
Compare Utah to the rest
of the United States.
It's different in so many different ways,
and people sometimes talk about
it being Utah magical mojo
that makes Utah operate
in particular ways.
But really what a lot of
it is, is the fact that,
for instance, we have
a lot of kids in Utah,
and so anything that's more common
among kids is going to
be more common in Utah.
There are other demographic distinctions
that can explain what's going on here,
and you have to watch
out for the differences.
If you're comparing different
states like California,
which is larger than a number
of countries in the world,
and you're comparing to say, Montana,
which is the size of
some cities, you're going
to find these statistical artifacts.
If you combine them, you'll
find overall patterns
that you don't see when
you take them apart.
Now, going back to this case
of gender discrimination,
a lot of people look at it
and say, we disaggregated it.
We found that there was
not a reliable pattern of
gender discrimination, problem solved.
Actually, no, not problem solved.
The next question is,
why were the programs
that women were going to apply to smaller
and why did they have
lower acceptance rates?
Was there gender
discrimination in the funding
and the staffing of the programs?
And so you haven't answered the question
by finding a paradox, you simply
push it to the next level.

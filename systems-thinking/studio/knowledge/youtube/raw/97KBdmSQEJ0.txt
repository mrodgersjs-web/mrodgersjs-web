This is a classic example of a simple&nbsp;
Bayesian Network model called the Asia&nbsp;&nbsp;model.
The objective of the model is to determine&nbsp;
the most likely diagnosis for a patient entering a&nbsp;&nbsp;
chest clinic. The nodes here represent variables&nbsp;
which may or may not be known and for simplicity&nbsp;&nbsp;
all of these nodes are Boolean meaning they have&nbsp;
just two states which were taken to be yes or no&nbsp;&nbsp;
in this case. There's a directed edge between&nbsp;
two nodes if there is a causal or influential&nbsp;&nbsp;
relationship between those nodes. For example&nbsp;
a person who is a smoker is more likely to have&nbsp;&nbsp;
lung cancer than a person who is a non-smokerm&nbsp;
similarly bronchitis, and a person who has any&nbsp;&nbsp;
of these conditions is more likely to have the&nbsp;
symptom of dyspnea which is shortness of breath.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Associated with every node is a probability&nbsp;
table. So, for example, for the node 'smoker'&nbsp;&nbsp;
the probability table is just 50:50 for yes or&nbsp;
no because that represents the fact that 50%&nbsp;&nbsp;
of the people who enter the chest Clinic&nbsp;
are smokers whereas for 'visit to Asia'&nbsp;&nbsp;
only one percent of the people have had a recent&nbsp;
visit to Asia. For nodes which have parents the&nbsp;&nbsp;
probability table is a conditional probability&nbsp;
table so we have to get the probabilities&nbsp;&nbsp;
conditioned on the states of the parents nodes.&nbsp;
So if a person is a smoker there's a 10% chance&nbsp;&nbsp;
that this person will have been diagnosed with&nbsp;
lung cancer and if the person is a non-smoker&nbsp;&nbsp;
there's only a one percent chance they would&nbsp;
been diagnosed with lung cancer. In its prior&nbsp;&nbsp;
or so-called marginal state the probabilities here&nbsp;
represent just what is known about the population&nbsp;&nbsp;
of people entering the chest clinic, but of course&nbsp;
we want to use the model for individualized risk&nbsp;&nbsp;
assessment. So suppose a patient comes in and is a&nbsp;
non-smoker. We're going to enter that observation&nbsp;&nbsp;
and now what's going to happen is we're going&nbsp;
to run the model and it's going to do all of&nbsp;&nbsp;
the Bayesian inference and in particular look&nbsp;
at how the probabilities of these nodes change.&nbsp;&nbsp;
So for a non-smoker the probability of lung cancer&nbsp;
went down probability of bronchitis went down;&nbsp;&nbsp;
similarly these all went down. At this point&nbsp;
if that's all you know about this person then&nbsp;&nbsp;
it's unlikely that they've got any of these&nbsp;
conditions because the highest probability&nbsp;&nbsp;
is 30% for bronchitis. But suppose they report&nbsp;
shortness of breath. When we run the model again&nbsp;&nbsp;
again see what happens. These two increase by&nbsp;
a little bit but they're still quite low but&nbsp;&nbsp;
bronchitis is now over 75% so you would diagnose&nbsp;
bronchitis as a likely condition in this case.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Suppose we send the patient for an X-ray and&nbsp;
it comes back positive. Run the model again&nbsp;&nbsp;
..TB and lung cancer have gone up quite a bit now&nbsp;
but they're still way below bronchitis which is&nbsp;&nbsp;
still the likely diagnosis at this point. However&nbsp;
suppose we decide to check if this person had a&nbsp;&nbsp;
recent visit to Asia and it turns out they did&nbsp;
in this case. When we run the model everything&nbsp;&nbsp;
changes. Tuberculosis almost from nowhere now&nbsp;
becomes the most likely probable diagnosis

Welcome to Five Minute Friday.
Today’s topic is “Goodhart’s Law: How to&nbsp;
destroy productivity” (in five minutes or less).&nbsp;
Do you set goals?
This is particularly important if you&nbsp;
set goals for a team or an organisation.
If so, be careful.
There’s a frighteningly easy&nbsp;
way to destroy productivity.
Just take your important performance metrics&nbsp;
and turn them into performance targets.&nbsp;
At this point, that probably&nbsp;
sounds really confusing.
So, I’m going to explain
by telling a story about…
a factory.
There was once a factory&nbsp;
that made carpentry nails:
The kind of nails that you&nbsp;
use for building houses.
The manager of the factory thought to himself,
“I’m a clever guy, I’m going to&nbsp;
increase the production of nails!”
So, he announced that the workers would be paid&nbsp;&nbsp;
according to the number of&nbsp;
nails that they&nbsp;produced.
Make more nails, make more money!
Pretty soon, the workers were making lots&nbsp;and lots&nbsp;&nbsp;
of really tiny nails that&nbsp;
were pretty much useless.
The factory started losing a lot of money.
The factory manager was pretty&nbsp;
frustrated, and tried to fix it.&nbsp;
He announced that the workers would&nbsp;
be paid according to the total weight&nbsp;&nbsp;
of nails produced. Quantity didn’t matter.&nbsp;
Then, the workers started producing&nbsp;
super big heavy nails that also&nbsp;&nbsp;
weren’t very useful or easy to sell.
The factory manager remained frustrated,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and eventually the factory&nbsp;
ran out of money and closed.&nbsp;
It’s kind of a funny story, but&nbsp;
let’s look at it for a moment.&nbsp;
For the nail factory, there&nbsp;
was a super important metric:&nbsp;&nbsp;
The number of nails produced (probably per hour).
The factory manager wanted&nbsp;
to make that metric go up,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but the moment that he started setting goals&nbsp;
around that metric, everything got screwed up.&nbsp;
This phenomenon is called Goodhart’s&nbsp;
Law, after this guy: Charles Goodhart.&nbsp;
Mr. Goodhart is a British economist, and in 1975&nbsp;
he wrote an article that said, “Any observed&nbsp;&nbsp;
statistical regularity will tend to collapse once&nbsp;
pressure is placed upon it for control purposes.”&nbsp;
In 1997, the anthropologist Marilyn Strathern&nbsp;
made it a little easier to understand when she&nbsp;&nbsp;
restated it as: “When a measure becomes a&nbsp;
target, it ceases to be a good measure”.&nbsp;
The core of Goodhart’s law is that if you&nbsp;
care about a metric, ANY kind of metric,&nbsp;&nbsp;
DON’T set targets based on that metric.
This is true for performance&nbsp;
metrics, quality metrics, anything.&nbsp;
I’ll give you a real example&nbsp;
that I saw inside a large bank.&nbsp;
In this bank, the head of engineering wanted&nbsp;
to encourage automated testing of code. So&nbsp;&nbsp;
she said that all code needed to&nbsp;
have at least 70% test coverage.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Test coverage would be tied&nbsp;
to performance reviews.&nbsp;
So, the very clever engineers started&nbsp;
writing a lot of automated tests.&nbsp;
However, these tests didn’t actually test&nbsp;
anything, and they were completely useless.
Worse than useless, actually, because they&nbsp;
gave the illusion that testing was happening.
So, how do you avoid this trap?&nbsp;
How do you avoid Goodhart’s Law?&nbsp;
Well, if you want to improve a metric,&nbsp;
you make changes that you *think* will&nbsp;&nbsp;
improve the metric. Then, use&nbsp;
the metrics as… well… metrics.
In our story, the nail factory&nbsp;
manager could have run some&nbsp;&nbsp;
experiments and then checked&nbsp;
the results using his metrics.
Once you understand Goodhart’s Law,&nbsp;
you’ll start seeing it everywhere.&nbsp;
Charles Goodhart was originally&nbsp;
writing about British monetary&nbsp;&nbsp;
policy, and Marilyn Strathern was&nbsp;
writing about educational policy.
I’ve personally seen people turn metrics&nbsp;
into targets in all sorts of businesses,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and in governments around the world.&nbsp;
There’s almost always negative consequences.&nbsp;
If you have your own examples&nbsp;
of Goodhart’s Law in action,&nbsp;&nbsp;
go ahead put them in the comments for this video.
Metrics and goals can be super tricky,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but next time you’re setting a&nbsp;
goal, remember Goodhart’s Law
and you’ll be a lot happier.

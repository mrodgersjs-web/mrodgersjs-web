What is organizational structure?
Organizational structure refers to the
framework that defines how work is
divided, grouped and coordinated in a
company. It shows who reports to whom,
how tasks are assigned, how decisions
are made, and how communication flows.
In short, it's like the skeleton of a
business. It holds everything together
and helps it function smoothly.
Different companies follow different
structures based on their size, goals,
and strategies.
Now that we understand what an
organizational structure is, let's see
the main types that companies use.
Although there are many variations, most
organizations follow three main
structures. Functional structure groups
people based on job functions like
marketing, finance, production etc.
Divisional structure groups people based
on products, regions or customers.
Matrix structure a combination of
functional and project based reporting.
We will discuss each of these types in
detail in the upcoming slides.
Let's start with the most common type.
The functional structure. In this
structure, the organization is divided
based on functions or departments such
as marketing, finance, production, human
resources. Each department is managed by
a specialist in that area.
The CEO is at the top giving directions
to all departments. Each department is
led by a functional expert. Teams within
each department handle day-to-day work.
This diagram visually shows how
authority flows vertically within
departments and how work is grouped
function wise.
Example in a toothpaste company the
marketing team handles advertising the
production team handles manufacturing
and so on. Advantages: Specialization
improves efficiency.
Clear roles and responsibilities.
Easy supervision and training.
Limitations: Poor communication between
departments.
Slow decision making
can create departmental silo where
teams don't cooperate well.
Next is the divisional structure which
is more common in large companies with
multiple products or markets. Here the
organization is divided based on
products, regions or customers. Each
division has its own departments like
sales, HR etc. The CEO supervises
multiple divisions each focuses on the
specific product. Each division has its
own departments like HR, marketing,
finance, etc. This structure works best
for large companies with diverse product
lines.
Example, a company like Samsung may have
separate divisions for mobile phones,
TVs, and home appliances. Each division
operates almost like a mini company.
Advantages: Focused attention on
products or markets,
quicker decisions,
better accountability,
limitations,
duplication of resources,
higher cost, risk of competition between
divisions.
Now let's look at more complex
structure. The matrix structure.
This is a hybrid system where employees
report to two bosses, one functional
head like marketing and finance and one
project manager project A, B or C. For
example, a marketing executive working
on a project A reports to both the
marketing head and project A manager.
This structure supports flexibility,
crossf functional collaboration and is
ideal for project based work.
Example, in a tech company, a software
engineer may reports to the IT head and
to a project manager for a client.
Advantages efficient use of resources.
Flexible and dynamic structure.
Promotes teamwork across departments.
confusion due to dual reporting,
possible power struggle between
managers, requires strong communication
skills.
To summarize, an organizational

functional structure works best for

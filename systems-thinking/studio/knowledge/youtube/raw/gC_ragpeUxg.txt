The human brain has one big advantage over today’s&nbsp;
artificially intelligent systems. It runs on&nbsp;&nbsp;
dramatically less energy, a hundred thousand times&nbsp;
less. So why don’t we just build computers out of&nbsp;&nbsp;
brain cells, and program those. Not only would&nbsp;
we save a lot of energy, we’d also have machines&nbsp;&nbsp;
that are neatly biocompatible for our impending&nbsp;
upgrade. This already exists. Let’s have a look.
The Australian company Cortical Labs sells&nbsp;
a biological computer called the CL1,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which contains about eight hundred thousand&nbsp;
living human neurons grown on a chip. The&nbsp;&nbsp;
neurons are hooked onto electronics that can be&nbsp;
used to stimulate them and record their activity.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The neurons are housed in a life-support module&nbsp;
that keeps the cells alive for several months.
The device was launched in 2025,&nbsp;
and reportedly costs around&nbsp;&nbsp;
thirty-five thousand dollars , though&nbsp;
the company also offers cloud access.
In 2022 the company made headlines by publishing&nbsp;
a paper in which they explained how they trained&nbsp;&nbsp;
a cluster of live neurons to play the computer&nbsp;
game pong, in the sense that it learned to update&nbsp;&nbsp;
the position of a hypothetical paddle given a&nbsp;
stimulus that encodes the position of the ball.
The company FinalSpark in Switzerland works&nbsp;
on a similar thing. They grow tiny chunks&nbsp;&nbsp;
of human brains with about ten thousand&nbsp;
neurons each on electronic chips and,&nbsp;&nbsp;
since earlier this year, they offer remote access,&nbsp;&nbsp;
for as little as five thousand US dollars&nbsp;
per month, and if you have that much money,&nbsp;&nbsp;
please check out my patreon. They also published&nbsp;
a research paper a few months ago in which they&nbsp;&nbsp;
showed that one of their neuron chunks could be&nbsp;
used to encode Braille with about 80% accuracy.
Much like today’s quantum computers, these&nbsp;
tiny brains don’t have any particular uses,&nbsp;&nbsp;
so far, and are aimed at research labs. But&nbsp;
the potential of this technology is huge and&nbsp;&nbsp;
I think that compared to quantum computers,&nbsp;
it’s a rather neglected area. Maybe this is&nbsp;&nbsp;
because it’s highly interdisciplinary which makes&nbsp;
it more difficult to generate community interest.
But this isn’t just about the potential energy&nbsp;
savings that a computer built of human neurons&nbsp;&nbsp;
could offer. For one thing, these organic&nbsp;
computers learn with the physical substrate&nbsp;&nbsp;
by rewiring their connections, which is very&nbsp;
different from today’s artificially intelligent&nbsp;&nbsp;
systems. Maybe this will get us to AGI. There&nbsp;
are some chips, the so-called ‘neuromorphic&nbsp;&nbsp;
chips’ that try to emulate physical learning,&nbsp;
but they are still very much in their infancy.
And then there are medical uses. Suppose you&nbsp;
could grow and pre-train parts of human brains.&nbsp;&nbsp;
These would be biocompatible by design. One&nbsp;
day they could be used to help patients with&nbsp;&nbsp;
brain damage or dementia. Or maybe we can all&nbsp;
extend our cognitive functions with extra memory,&nbsp;&nbsp;
faster data processing, or better jokes. I&nbsp;
hope I can uninstall the dad jokes update.
This is still very far in the future though. These&nbsp;
tiny brain computers currently face three major&nbsp;&nbsp;
challenges. The first is that they don’t live&nbsp;
very long, a few months at most. The second is&nbsp;&nbsp;
that they’re too small and no one knows how to&nbsp;
scale them up while still being able to control&nbsp;&nbsp;
them. The third issue is reproducibility. If you&nbsp;
let the stuff organically grow, no two are alike.
Also, it is entirely unclear whether these chunks&nbsp;
of neurons can learn to think on their own. The&nbsp;&nbsp;
demonstration with the pong “playing” might sound&nbsp;
impressive, but it’s not like the neurons learned&nbsp;&nbsp;
themselves. Rather, the researchers gave them&nbsp;
electronic feedback depending on whether the&nbsp;&nbsp;
neuron firing was closer or farther away&nbsp;
from the right result. Electroshock them&nbsp;&nbsp;
until they get it right might also teach children&nbsp;
algebra, but maybe it’s not the optimal approach.
And then there is the ethical&nbsp;
question. Even if we could,&nbsp;&nbsp;
should we be growing parts of human brains&nbsp;
in the laboratory? What if they actually&nbsp;&nbsp;
become sentient. What if they suffer?&nbsp;
We don’t even know what to look for.
&nbsp;
To me this line of research opens another&nbsp;
question. That’s whether we actually want&nbsp;&nbsp;
to artificially build human intelligence from&nbsp;
human organoids. I mean, if a new human brain is&nbsp;&nbsp;
what we want, why not just have a baby. To me the&nbsp;
great appeal of artificial general intelligence,&nbsp;&nbsp;
if we get there, is that they will almost&nbsp;
certainly NOT be like us. They will&nbsp;&nbsp;
have different strengths and weaknesses.
Still I think that this avenue, of trying&nbsp;&nbsp;
to grow intelligent systems from human brain&nbsp;
cells, will certainly continue for some while.
But maybe one day, when you forgot to buy milk,&nbsp;&nbsp;
it’ll make sense to say “sorry,&nbsp;
I had my thoughts in the cloud”.

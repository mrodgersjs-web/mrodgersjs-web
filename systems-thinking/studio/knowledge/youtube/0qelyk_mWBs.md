# Influence | The Psychology of Persuasion by Robert Cialdini ► Book Summary

This video summarizes Robert Cialdini's six principles of influence—reciprocity, commitment/consistency, social proof, liking, authority, and scarcity—and explains how they can be strategically deployed to increase compliance in relationships, business, and career.

## Procedure / models distilled
- **Reciprocity**: Give a small, unexpected favor or free value first to trigger a felt obligation to reciprocate, often at a larger scale.
- **Commitment and consistency**: Secure a small initial verbal or written commitment so the person is more likely to follow through with larger, consistent actions later.
- **Public commitment for goals**: Write goals down or announce them publicly to harness self-image pressure and increase follow-through.
- **Trip-wire offers**: Use a low-risk, low-cost paid offer to convert a prospect into a customer, then build on that initial commitment with higher-value offers.
- **Social proof**: Show that others have already chosen an action to reduce uncertainty and guide the behavior of people who are unsure.
- **Liking through similarity**: Identify shared interests, values, and viewpoints to build rapport and increase the chance the other person says yes.
- **Attractiveness halo**: Recognize that attractive people are automatically attributed traits like honesty, talent, and intelligence.
- **Authority signaling**: Display symbols of expertise—uniforms, titles, credentials, or professional dress—to increase perceived authority and compliance.
- **Scarcity framing**: Frame opportunities as limited in time or availability to make loss feel more motivating than equivalent gain.
- **Personal scarcity**: Communicate limited availability of your time to increase your perceived value and the weight of access to you.

## Failure modes named
- Complying with fake authority because of uniforms or surface symbols of status, as in the parking-inspector uniform scam.
- Over-reciprocating to small initial favors and feeling obligated into larger purchases or commitments.
- Being driven by artificial scarcity such as "limited time only" rather than genuine value.
- Using social proof as a shortcut when reviews or crowds can be fabricated or irrelevant.
- Confusing attractiveness, similarity, or likability with competence or trustworthiness.

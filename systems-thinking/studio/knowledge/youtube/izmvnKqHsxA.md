# The Strength of Weak Ties (Granovetter 1973) - Weekend Classics

Granovetter's classic paper argues that weak social ties, not just strong ones, are the main channels through which novel information flows and large-scale social structures are shaped.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Define tie strength as a combination of four observable factors: time spent together, emotional intensity, mutual confiding, and reciprocal services.
- Apply the forbidden triad: if person A has strong ties to both B and C, then B and C are very likely to be connected to each other too.
- Recognize that strong ties tend to cluster, so information circulating inside them is redundant and echo-chamber-like.
- Identify local bridges as ties that provide the only or shortest path between otherwise separate social circles.
- Infer that weak ties are more likely than strong ties to serve as local bridges because they extend outside dense clusters.
- Use frequency of contact as a practical proxy for tie strength when operationalizing the concept.
- Test the theory empirically by asking job seekers about the contact who gave them their job lead and classifying that contact's tie strength.
- Compare percentages across tie-strength categories to show that weak ties disproportionately deliver novel opportunities.
- Link micro-level relationships to macro-level social structure by treating network patterns as mechanisms that produce societal outcomes.
- Adopt relational thinking by analyzing the structure of connections between actors, not just individual attributes.
- Map clusters and bridges in a network to understand where information, support, or innovation can travel.
- Treat network analysis as a core sociological method rather than a peripheral descriptive tool.
- Diagnose modern information systems by checking whether digital connectivity cultivates diverse weak-tie bridges or reinforces homophilous echo chambers.

## Failure modes named
- Assuming strong ties are the best source of new opportunities, when they usually circulate redundant information.
- Treating weak ties as socially insignificant because they are less intimate.
- Using jargon-laden titles or framing that routes your work to the wrong evaluators or audiences.
- Focusing only on individual attributes and missing the relational architecture that shapes outcomes.
- Mistaking a large number of shallow digital connections for genuinely diverse weak-tie bridges.
- Designing systems that amplify filter bubbles and strong-tie clustering, thereby blocking novel information.

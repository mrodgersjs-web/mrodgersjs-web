# How To Make The Right Decision (Even When It Will Cost You)

A Stoic guide to acting on principle when the right choice is costly, lonely, or scary.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Treat voluntary discomfort (cold plunges, rigorous training, practicing poverty) as a rehearsal for doing what you should do even when part of you does not want to.
- Reframe fear as a signal of scarcity and value: if the right thing were easy, everyone would do it, and courage would not be required.
- Use premeditatio malorum and logic to interrogate fear by asking, "What is actually the worst thing that could happen here?"
- Convert vague, ill-defined consequences into concrete specifics, because uncertainty exaggerates risk while clarity makes action possible.
- Accept that doing the right thing often means standing alone, and deliberately cultivate a character that is comfortable being the odd one out.
- Anchor decisions in duty, honor, code, and fiduciary-style responsibility rather than popularity, comfort, or career preservation.
- Write down your code, priorities, and values in advance so that hard choices become muscle memory when pressure arrives.
- When weighing whether to act, ask Hillel's question: "If not me, then who? If not now, then when?" to expose procrastination and identify unique responsibility.
- Refuse "obeying in advance" by not self-censoring, going along to get along, or accepting bribes and threats from corrupt interests.
- Distinguish legitimate strategic compromise inside a flawed system from self-flattering martyrdom that enables harm; most people need to speak up, not silently moderate.
- Recognize that fear and risk are indicators of growth and potential reward; the absence of a well-trodden path is often a sign that courage matters.
- Stop deferring courage to a future when you are "more secure," because later usually means never.

## Failure modes named
- Preferring comfort or avoiding embarrassment over doing the right thing.
- Obeying in advance through self-censorship, accommodation, or silence in the face of threats.
- Staying inside a corrupt system while flattering yourself as the "adult in the room" who is preventing worse outcomes.
- Accumulating power, wealth, or influence and then failing to use it when principle is tested.
- Confusing necessary compromise with enabling behavior that protects your own position.
- Letting vague, imagined fears overwhelm clear, logical analysis of real consequences.
- Believing fear should disappear before you act, rather than pushing through fear that persists.
- Conforming to the herd or mob to avoid criticism, even when your conscience says otherwise.

# Become a great strategic thinker | Ian Bremmer

Ian Bremmer defines strategic thinking as meta-thinking about how one builds and updates a worldview, emphasizing pattern recognition, intellectual openness, and continuous revision so that one's models do not become wrong simply because the world kept moving.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Treat strategic thinking as meta-thinking: examine how you form your worldview rather than reacting to events or headlines.
- Practice pattern recognition across macro themes operating at the country, company, and individual levels.
- Map trade-offs among leaders or actors who have different priorities, needs, and timeframes.
- Conduct a worldview audit: identify what would change your worldview and why you weight certain factors over others.
- Recalibrate your worldview on a cadence faster than the world changes, since any analytic view decays toward wrongness over time.
- Use cross-contextual imagination: mentally place the same person or issue in a different national or cultural origin to surface hidden assumptions.
- Engage different worldviews as different stories rather than as opposing or villainous positions.
- Build tolerance for dissonance through daily practice, like going to the gym, so discomfort with disagreement becomes routine.
- Distinguish tactical response from strategic thinking: tactics optimize within a worldview; strategy questions and updates the worldview itself.
- Separate immediate justice from long-term outcomes when making consequential decisions, taking the longest view your context allows.

## Failure modes named
- Reacting to headlines or media cycles instead of analyzing underlying patterns.
- Using accumulated personal history and data to instantly categorize new information without testing for dissonance.
- Following sources or people who only confirm your priors, which may produce strong tactical thinking but weak strategic thinking.
- Treating different worldviews as oppositional or villainous rather than as different stories.
- Failing to revisit worldviews as the world changes, causing a once-correct view to become wrong by standing still.
- Letting personal vendettas or the demand for immediate justice override the long-term future.
- Needing to appear right rather than admitting wrongness, updating beliefs, and moving on.

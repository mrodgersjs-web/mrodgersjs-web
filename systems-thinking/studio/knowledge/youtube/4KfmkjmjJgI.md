# Charlie Munger: Powerful Mental Models Used By Great Investors

Charlie Munger and Warren Buffett explain how rationality, circle-of-competence discipline, comparative opportunity analysis, and honest learning from mistakes produce durable investment and life results.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Treat rationality as a moral duty: see the world as it is, not as you wish it to be, and remove easily removable ignorance.
- Define your circle of competence and stay inside it; only deploy capital where you can reasonably understand the future 5–15 years out.
- Know your limitations and say no to most things; a high "no" rate is part of the system.
- Compare opportunities relatively, especially when capital is constrained: measure what you already own against each new opportunity.
- Prefer high-certainty decent results over low-certainty brilliant results; let temperament tilt toward the sure thing.
- Keep reading, thinking, and screening continuously; let deals come to you rather than forcing elaborate plans.
- Pay the tuition of real mistakes; getting your nose whacked hard is one of the most reliable generators of wisdom.
- Learn to distinguish good businesses from bad ones by direct experience with both; bad businesses sharpen discrimination.
- Choose admirable partners, managers, and collaborators; recognize character early and avoid those who lack it.
- Enjoy the work itself; sustained interest and temperament outperform clenched-teeth discipline over decades.
- Morph beyond passive stock-picking into building or stewarding real enterprises; buying pieces of paper alone is not enough of a life.
- Think deeply and sequentially; reject multitasking as a path to shallow, poor thinking.
- Adapt to structural change even when you dislike it; the internet and other disruptions change every business and cannot be ignored.
- Beware naive causality and selection bias; do not attribute outcomes to a single variable when the compared groups differ to start with.
- Watch for "prices rising to what can be collected" and the rationalization that the service must be worth it; right-size overhead before a crisis forces it.

## Failure modes named
- Keeping easily removable ignorance and therefore staying stupider than you have to be.
- Confusing a shrewd stock-picking life with a fully adequate contribution to society.
- Multitasking instead of doing the hard, sequential thinking required for complex decisions.
- Believing headline statistics that conflate selection effects with causation.
- Allowing bloat and overstaffing to persist until a financial shock forces painful cuts.
- Letting fraud-, incentive-, or litigation-driven costs spiral until public rebellion finally corrects them.
- Accepting sticker-shock prices because the system can collect them, rather than seeking alternatives.

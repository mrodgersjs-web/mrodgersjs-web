# Bob Allen: Why the Industrial Revolution first happened in Britain

Bob Allen argues that Britain's Industrial Revolution began with labor-saving inventions like the spinning jenny because high wages—driven by imperial trade—made mechanization profitable enough to fund its own R&D, a condition absent in other countries.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Frame the Industrial Revolution as a mode-of-production shift from cottage industry to factory production, not merely a burst of inventions.
- Treat technological adoption as a profitability calculation: labor saved must offset the extra cost of machinery.
- Use relative wage ratios as a proxy for the value of labor-saving innovation.
- Model the diffusion of mechanization as a response to rising rates of return on capital equipment.
- Trace high domestic wages back to demand shocks from expanding imperial export markets.
- Map colonial acquisitions onto captive markets for manufactured goods (e.g., sugar colonies, North America, Canada, Bengal).
- Recognize that imperial prosperity tightened domestic labor markets and pushed up wages, especially in textiles.
- Compare rates of return across countries to explain why the same technology was adopted in Britain but not elsewhere.
- Treat invention as an R&D investment whose cost is justified only when expected profitability is high enough.
- See macro-historical shifts (imperial expansion) as the upstream cause of micro-level innovation decisions.

## Failure modes named
- Assuming the Industrial Revolution happened simply because inventors were clever or technology was available.
- Believing that inventions spread automatically without checking whether local factor prices made them profitable.
- Treating the Industrial Revolution as a purely European or British-internal phenomenon while ignoring the imperial and global-trade roots.
- Confusing the existence of a machine with the economic conditions needed to invest in developing and adopting it.

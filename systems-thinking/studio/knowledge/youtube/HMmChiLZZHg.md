# Dana (Donella) Meadows Lecture: Sustainable Systems (Part 1 of 4)

Meadows defines sustainability through four biophysical and social rules, introduces systems as elements, interrelationships, and purpose, and uses a simple fishery stock-and-flow model to show why unsustainability is an emergent systems behavior rather than a failure of individuals.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Define sustainability using Daly’s three biophysical rules: renewable resources used at or below regeneration rates; non-renewable resources used at or below the rate of renewable substitution; pollution emitted at or below absorption/neutralization rates.
- Add the fourth social rule: a sustainable system must be fair and equitable enough that people feel secure and the social system does not self-destruct.
- Define a system as a set of interrelated elements organized around a function, goal, or purpose—distinguishing elements, interrelationships/structure, and the system’s overall purpose.
- Recognize that behavior recurring across scales, actors, and ecosystems signals a systems problem, not a people or technology problem.
- Map a system as stocks and flows: identify accumulations (fish, boats) and the inflows/outflows that change them (regeneration, harvest, investment, depreciation).
- Identify information links and feedback loops as the structure that governs behavior, not the individual elements.
- Model nonlinear relationships such as fish regeneration peaking at intermediate stock levels and catch-per-boat declining as fish stocks fall.
- Simulate a system by setting initial stock levels, defining the relationships, and running it forward to observe emergent behavior over time.
- Rank intervention leverage by depth: first purpose/function, then interrelationships/feedback/information flows, and only then elements/actors.
- Distinguish events (snapshot news) from behavior (long-term system tendency) when diagnosing what a system is actually doing.
- Contrast holism with reductionism; reject economy-vs-environment trade-offs as falsely separating integrated systems.
- Use deliberately simple models to extract the majority of insight before adding complexity.

## Failure modes named
- Treating unsustainability as caused by bad actors or bad technologies rather than by system structure.
- Swapping people, technologies, or policies (elements) without changing the system’s purpose or information flows.
- Focusing on events and personalities while missing long-term system behavior.
- Talking about trade-offs between the environment and the economy as if they were separate systems.
- Ignoring the fourth social-equity condition of sustainability.
- Letting a system produce a result that no one wants because its structure overrides individual intentions.

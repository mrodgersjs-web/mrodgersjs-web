# 2. Human Behavioral Biology: Evolution of Behavior 1 by Robert Sapolsky

Sapolsky explains that behavior evolves not “for the good of the species” but through individual selection, kin selection, and reciprocal altruism, with game theory revealing how cooperative and punitive strategies cycle under different social and ecological conditions.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Treat behavior as individual selection: animals act to maximize copies of their own genes in the next generation, not group welfare.
- Replace “for the good of the species” with the “selfish gene” (more accurately selfish genome) as the level at which selection operates.
- Use kin selection / inclusive fitness: helping relatives can spread shared genes, so a costly act can evolve when its benefit is weighted by genetic relatedness.
- Think of relatedness as the probability that two individuals share an allele by common descent, which sets how much costly help is evolutionarily worthwhile.
- Map reciprocal altruism onto repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma: cooperate now to gain future benefits when the same interactants meet repeatedly.
- Play Tit-for-Tat: start by cooperating, then copy the partner’s last move to reward cooperators and punish defectors.
- Use Forgiving Tit-for-Tat when interactions are noisy: allow occasional defections to pass so a single error does not trigger endless retaliation.
- Track strategy cycles: all-defect → Tit-for-Tat → Forgiving Tit-for-Tat → always-cooperate → cheater invasion → all-defect, with transition speed set by error rates and relationship length.
- Look for green-beard-like recognition: cooperators must be able to find and preferentially interact with each other for Tit-for-Tat to outcompete pure cheaters.
- Recognize simultaneous games: an individual who “cheats” in one context may be contributing in another, so evaluate behavior across multiple domains.
- Test predictions against real animals: vampire bat blood-sharing, stickleback fish territorial defense, and lion coalition behavior all show punishment, reward, and cross-domain compensation.
- Treat “the animal wants…” language as shorthand for evolutionary outcome, not evidence of conscious strategy or species-level intent.

## Failure modes named
- Group-selection fallacy: explaining behavior as “for the good of the species, group, or population.”
- Mistaking evolutionary shorthand for conscious motivation or species-level planning.
- Assuming one strategy, such as Tit-for-Tat, is universally optimal regardless of noise, relationship length, or other games.
- Letting Forgiving Tit-for-Tat become so trusting that cynics exploit it after building false cooperation.
- Confusing survival of the fittest with reproduction of the fittest.
- Treating genes as direct, deterministic causes of behavior rather than contributors to probabilistic propensities.
- Judging cooperation or defection in a single context while ignoring cross-talk between multiple simultaneous games.
- Interpreting every defection as intentional cheating rather than a signal error that warrants forgiveness.

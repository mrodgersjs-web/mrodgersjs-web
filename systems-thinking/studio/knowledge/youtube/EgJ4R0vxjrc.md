# How Game Theory Solves Tough Negotiations: Corporate Tax Cuts, Nuclear War, and Parenting| Big Think

Game theory reframes strategic conflict as a set of repeated, conditional micro-interactions that can transform zero-sum dilemmas into stable cooperation.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Define the situation as a strategic game: each actor pursues their own interests while anticipating the other’s moves.
- Recognize a social dilemma or prisoner’s dilemma when individual incentives produce a collectively worse outcome.
- Convert one-shot, all-or-nothing conflicts into repeated, smaller interactions.
- Use conditional cooperation: cooperate in the next round only if the other party cooperated in the previous round.
- Build incremental verification into each step before advancing to the next.
- Apply the small-steps model to high-stakes negotiations like nuclear arms reductions.
- Apply the same model to domestic coordination problems by splitting large chores into turn-based, contingent tasks.
- In competitive-policy dilemmas, model the risk of mutual defection producing a race to the bottom.
- Design coordinated international restraint instead of relying on unilateral good faith.
- Build institutional checks, balances, and punishments rather than defaulting to pure privatization or a single world government.
- Generalize the same game-theoretic moves across economics, biology, international relations, and interpersonal relationships.

## Failure modes named
- Race to the bottom: mutual defection drives outcomes toward zero.
- Free riding or shirking because one party expects the other to bear the cost.
- Trusting promises without verification or enforcement mechanisms.
- Treating a repeated cooperation problem as a single, all-or-nothing demand.
- Assuming a single world government or total privatization are the only solutions to international dilemmas.
- Unilateral corporate tax cuts that invite rival countries to undercut them.

# PHIL-2780 - Philosophy of Science - Overview

An overview of a philosophy-of-science course that frames philosophy as the normative discipline needed alongside history and sociology, then surveys core problems: objectivity, scientific method, explanation, biology versus physics, function talk, and meaning under a scientific worldview.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Distinguish descriptive questions (how science does work) from normative questions (how it should work) and assign the normative task to philosophy of science.
- Treat "objectivity" as a cluster term whose sense must be pinned down before assessing whether science is objective (candidate senses: agreement, truth, value-neutrality, absence of perspective).
- Adopt Grinnell's insider model: values, intuitions, and passions play a legitimate motivational and structuring role in science without reducing to bias or destructive subjectivity.
- Map the linear model of science as the anti-model: a naive "pile of facts" picture of steady accumulation that is historically inadequate.
- Use Kuhn's pattern of normal and revolutionary science as a non-linear model of how scientific knowledge changes.
- Apply Feyerabend's methodological anarchism as a provocative heuristic: successful science sometimes ignores the best available arguments and borrows methods or ideas from anywhere.
- Analyze "social construct" without the made-up fallacy: science is a social process, yet its socially produced results can still be constrained and non-arbitrary.
- Evaluate scientific explanation via the deductive-nomological model (general law + fact entails the explanandum) and notice where it breaks down.
- Compare disciplinary characters: physics-centered models of law and exception may fail when extended to biology and other historical sciences.
- Model biological function talk as either real purposive facts or shorthand for natural-selection stories.
- Hold the question of meaning under a scientific worldview as a live issue, not settled by expelling teleology from physics.

## Failure modes named
- Using "objective" or "subjective" without defining which sense is meant, producing empty or equivocal claims.
- Confusing descriptive history/sociology of science with normative philosophy of science.
- The linear-model trap: assuming science accumulates facts in a straight line and that past theories were merely incomplete rather than fundamentally wrong.
- The God's-eye-view trap: defining objectivity as perspective-free knowledge, which makes objectivity impossible.
- Ad populum fallacy: inferring truth from agreement.
- Dismissing ad hoc moves as mere butt-covering when they can be productive theory development.
- The "social construct = made up" conflation.
- The universal-law assumption: assuming every science must explain by subsuming facts under universal laws.
- Extending physics-based models to biology without accounting for exceptions, variation, and function talk.
- Confusing the absence of teleology in physics with the impossibility of meaning or purpose under a scientific worldview.

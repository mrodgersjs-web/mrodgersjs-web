# HARVARD Negotiator:  How To Get What You Want Every Time

This video distills the Harvard Negotiation Project’s principled-negotiation method into a four-step framework for reaching wise, mutually satisfactory agreements while preserving relationships.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Separate the people from the problem: be soft on the person and hard on the problem.
- Focus on interests, not positions; ask “why” to uncover underlying needs.
- Communicate your own interests clearly so the other side knows what you need.
- Translate interests into concrete actions by asking what you want the other side to actually do if they agree tomorrow.
- Invent options for mutual gain through open brainstorming before judging or selecting.
- Use differences between parties as a source of creative value, not only as conflict.
- Use fair, objective standards (market price, law, expert opinion, agreed criteria) instead of positional haggling.
- Agree on fair standards together before applying them to the decision.
- Build a relationship with the other side before negotiating; informal contact increases success rates.
- Develop your BATNA (best alternative to a negotiated agreement) before negotiating to know your walk-away point.
- Recognize and name dirty tactics (e.g., good cop/bad cop) to neutralize them without personal attacks.
- Use negotiation jujitsu when attacked personally: sidestep, ask why, ask for their advice, and invite criticism.
- Keep the goal on working together, not on “winning,” because asking “who’s winning” means you’ve already lost.

## Failure modes named
- Framing negotiation as win/lose or asking “who is winning.”
- Positional bargaining: arguing over fixed demands instead of underlying interests.
- Compromising at 50/50 when an integrative solution could give both sides more.
- Failing to ask why the other side wants what they want.
- Not communicating your own interests, leaving the other side guessing.
- Letting anger, ego, or frustration block agreement and damage the relationship.
- Confusing perceptions with reality and misinterpreting the other side’s words.
- Negotiating without a developed BATNA, weakening your walk-away power.
- Turning a power imbalance into a “gunfight” instead of building alternatives.
- Defending against personal attacks, which escalates conflict instead of resolving it.

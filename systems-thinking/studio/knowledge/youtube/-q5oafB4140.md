# Agent-Based Modeling: Description, Explanation, Experimentation, and Analogy

Agent-based models are a simplification toolkit for describing systems, testing explanations of emergent behavior, running virtual experiments, and generating cross-domain analogies.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Build an agent-based model as a simplified description of a real or artificial system, intentionally omitting detail to make it usable.
- Treat a model as a written “text” that captures the essential dynamics of what is happening around us.
- Make the model as simple as possible, but not simpler, because over-detailed models become unusable.
- Use a model as a proof-of-concept explanation: encode a candidate mechanism and check whether it reproduces observed aggregate patterns.
- Use models to illuminate emergence: run simple local rules and observe whether they generate the macro-level patterns seen in reality.
- Run ABMs repeatedly under varied conditions to see how outcomes change when parameters or rules shift.
- Use the virtual laboratory of an ABM to explore cases and outcome sets that cannot be directly observed in the real world.
- Return model experiments to real-world observations to validate findings before treating them as actionable.
- Use flocking-style models analogically: transfer insights from birds/fish to other coordinated systems such as drone swarms.
- Extend analogies across domains: apply biological flocking models to engineering or social systems that look nothing like the original.
- Use ABMs for education and communication to make complex, emergent dynamics tangible.
- Build ABMs as a cornerstone or focal object that centers discussion and further inquiry about a system.
- Use ABMs as thought experiments to sharpen intuition about how mechanisms might produce outcomes.
- Generate predictions by simulating future or alternative scenarios.

## Failure modes named
- Building a model that includes every detail (the Borges map as large as the territory) makes it unusable rather than useful.
- Oversimplifying: making a model simpler than the phenomenon requires can lose explanatory power (“as simple as possible, but not simpler”).
- Treating a pithy attribution (e.g., Einstein’s exact words) as literally true, when it is itself a simplified model of what was said.

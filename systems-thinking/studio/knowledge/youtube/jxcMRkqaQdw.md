# The Art of War by Sun Tzu: Entire Unabridged Audiobook

An unabridged narration of Sun Tzu's treatise on strategy, covering calculation, deception, logistics, terrain, leadership, fire, and espionage as a unified system for winning before fighting.

## Procedure / models distilled
- Use the five constant factors—moral law, heaven, earth, commander, method and discipline—as a checklist before any engagement.
- Compare the seven deliberations—moral accord, generalship, terrain advantage, discipline, strength, training, constancy in reward and punishment—to forecast victory or defeat.
- Make deception the base of operations: appear weak when strong, near when far, inactive when active, and use baits to lure the enemy.
- Shape the enemy’s psychology and posture: irritate the choleric, tire the resting, divide the united, and attack when unprepared.
- Keep campaigns short and feed forces by foraging on the enemy, treating captured resources and personnel as supplements to your own strength.
- Prefer to take the opponent whole and intact; supreme excellence is breaking resistance without fighting, then thwarting plans, preventing junctions, and only then engaging in the field.
- Apply force-ratio rules: surround at 10:1, attack at 5:1, divide at 2:1, offer battle when equal, avoid when slightly inferior, and flee when greatly inferior.
- First make yourself un-defeatable through defensive strength, then wait for the enemy to provide the opportunity for offense.
- Classify ground into nine varieties and tailor tactics to each, using deep penetration for cohesion and desperate ground to provoke total commitment.
- Coordinate five classes of spies—local, inward, converted, doomed, and surviving—to build foreknowledge and feed deception.
- Attack by fire only when conditions are dry and windy, with material ready and a prepared response to five possible developments.
- Move only when there is advantage, use troops only for gain, and never act from personal pique or spleen.

## Failure modes named
- Prolonged war dulls weapons, dampens ardor, exhausts strength, drains treasure, and invites new enemies.
- Sieging walled cities consumes months, wastes a third of an army, and often fails to take the objective.
- A ruler who commands advances or retreats the army cannot obey hobbles the army and causes anarchy.
- Governing an army like a kingdom or employing officers without regard to circumstances produces restlessness and lost confidence.
- Stupid haste and long delays are both fatal; cleverness is never associated with prolonged campaigns.
- Ignoring the cost of 100 ounces of silver for intelligence while spending a thousand a day is inhumane and self-defeating.
- Acting from anger or vexation can destroy a kingdom or waste lives that cannot be restored.
